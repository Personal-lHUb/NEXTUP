## Di che cosa parla il libro
What it means for you when a parent, sibling or grandparent has dementia (Alzheimer's, frontotemporal dementia or another form). The book covers how to read your family history and what it says about your own risk and your children's, whether and when to take a genetic test and what to arrange first, how to recognize early changes and get a diagnosis taken seriously, and how to build a family plan for care, work, money and the years ahead.

## A chi è rivolto
Adults in the United States, roughly 30 to 65, who have watched dementia reach someone in their family. Often it is a parent diagnosed young, a sibling, or several relatives across generations, and the reader now carries the question of whether it will happen to them and to their children. Many are already helping to care for the person who is ill while holding down a job. Some first heard the name of the disease on a scan report and had to look it up. Others lived with years of other diagnoses before anyone said dementia. A second group is the spouse of a person with dementia who is arranging genetic tests for the children and grandchildren. Neither group is made of science readers. They have the disease at home, they have read the stories, and they want the answers a story can't give for their own case.

## Che cosa si porta a casa il lettore
The reader comes away with four things. First, a clear picture of what a relative's dementia means for their own risk and their children's: which forms are inherited and which are not, the difference between a gene that causes the disease and a gene that only raises the odds, and what each number really says. Second, what they need to decide, with a genetic counselor, whether and when to be tested, and what to put in place before the result and after it. Third, the signs that call for a medical evaluation and the questions that get a diagnosis taken seriously. Fourth, a practical plan for the family: who cares for whom, the help that exists, work, money and legal papers, and their own future. The goal is to turn waiting into a set of decisions. The book explains and helps the reader decide. It does not promise anything. No test, habit or program in these pages can guarantee that a person will not develop dementia, and the book says so.

## Il buco che questo libro copre
La lacuna 1, la più ricorrente (5 recensioni): «Toccherà a me? Il rischio ereditario e il test per chi resta». I lettori che la demenza ce l'hanno in casa escono dal libro con le domande aperte: che cosa vuol dire la mia storia familiare, se fare il test, che cosa posso fare nel frattempo. È la ragione d'essere del libro, e ne occupa più di metà. Accanto ci sono, come parti di servizio, la lacuna 2 (accorgersene: il cambiamento che sembra un'altra cosa, la sigla da cercare, la diagnosi da ottenere; 3 ricorrenze) e la lacuna 3 (dopo la diagnosi: chi cura, con quali aiuti, lavoro e soldi; 2 ricorrenze). Riguardano lo stesso lettore, che ha un familiare malato e si chiede che cosa succederà a sé e ai figli. La lacuna 4 (la speranza promessa e quella trovata) dà il tono: una speranza che sta in quello che si può decidere e organizzare adesso, non in una cura annunciata. La lacuna 5 impone di accompagnare il lettore e non solo di riaprire la ferita. La 6 chiede pochi personaggi, ben riconoscibili. La 7 chiede un apparato snello, con le pagine spese sul contenuto. La confidenza dell'analisi è bassa: 8 recensioni, nessuna sotto le 4 stelle, lacune lette fra le righe.

## Quello che i lettori di questa nicchia si aspettano e va mantenuto
- The balance of human story and clear science. Every chapter pairs a family's situation with the science behind it, explained plainly: how inheritance works, what a gene does, what a test can and cannot see. Readers of this niche praise exactly this balance.
- People at the center, told with compassion. A small cast of composite families, declared as composites in the opening pages, three at most, followed through the book and easy to tell apart, so the reader never needs a family tree to keep up.
- It reads like a story, not a manual. Each chapter opens with a scene and continues in prose. Practical steps come out of the narrative, not as checklists.
- Emotional truth without clinical distance. It names the fear, the anticipatory grief and the loss of someone who is still alive, the way people who have lived it recognize.
- Hope beside the pain, kept honest. It is the hope of decisions that can be made now, help that exists and research families can take part in, never the promise of a cure.

## Temi da trattare
- Reading your family tree. Which dementias run in families and which simply happen in them. Early onset versus late onset. A gene that causes the disease versus a gene that raises the odds (APOE e4). Why a grandparent diagnosed at 85 is a different story from a parent diagnosed at 50.
- Numbers that frighten and numbers that inform. What 'fifty-fifty', 'twice the risk' and 'increased risk' actually mean. Absolute versus relative risk. Age as the largest factor. How to turn a percentage into something you can decide with.
- The genetic tests. Why the person who is ill is usually tested first. Predictive testing for relatives at risk. Consumer DNA tests that report an APOE result nobody asked for. What a positive, a negative and a 'variant of uncertain significance' say, and what they don't.
- To know or not to know. How genetic counseling works before and after a test. The reasons people choose to test and the reasons they choose not to. Timing, the right not to know, and siblings who decide differently.
- What to arrange before the test. Federal genetic nondiscrimination protections and their limits: health insurance and most employment are covered, life, disability and long-term-care insurance are not. Who sees the result, and what to put in place first.
- Living with a result. A positive one, a negative one, an uncertain one. Survivor's guilt among siblings. How and when to tell a partner and children. Not turning every lost word or misplaced key into a symptom.
- When the change looks like something else. Early signs of frontotemporal dementia (personality, behavior, judgment, language) and of Alzheimer's (memory). Why the first changes are often taken for depression, another psychiatric illness or a midlife crisis. When and how to insist on a neurological evaluation.
- Understanding the diagnosis. Reading a scan report or a test result you got before the doctor called. The main types of dementia in plain words. The questions to bring to the appointment. Specialist centers and second opinions.
- The family plan. Who does what, and sharing care among siblings and a spouse. Work and leave, including who federal family leave covers and who it doesn't. Money: disability benefits and fast-track programs for early-onset conditions, and Medicaid. Legal documents to sign while capacity remains.
- Help many families don't know exists. Support groups and specialist associations, respite care, palliative care, and hospice eligibility for dementia. How to ask for each, and when.
- Your own future under uncertainty. Money, career and advance directives for yourself. The decision about having children and the options people consider, presented without judgment. What the evidence says about general brain health, and what it cannot promise.
- Hope you can use. Family registries, observational studies and prevention trials open to people at risk: what they ask, what they give back, how to judge one. Carrying anticipatory grief and fear without letting them run your life.

## Quello che il libro dichiaratamente NON fa
- It does not tell the story of one family, and it is not a history of dementia research or of the race for a cure.
- It promises no prevention, reversal or slowing of dementia. There is no program, diet, supplement or brain exercise. Where it reports the evidence on modifiable risk factors, it says what that evidence can and cannot claim, and why it applies differently to a gene that causes the disease.
- It gives no medical or genetic advice for the individual reader and does not replace a genetic counselor or a neurologist. It does not tell anyone whether to be tested: it prepares the decision and the conversation.
- It does not recommend or evaluate drugs or treatments. Clinical trials appear only as something families can choose to look into, with the questions to ask.
- It is not a day-to-day caregiving manual: no hands-on care routines, no handling of challenging behaviors, no activities. Those books exist. This one covers the family's plan, not the daily care.
- It has no pages to fill in: no worksheets, logs or blank charts. Questions and lists stay in the text.
- Its practical information on insurance, work leave, disability benefits and end-of-life care is for the United States. Other countries' systems are not covered, and figures and rules are dated with a pointer to where to check.
