## What the book is about

A hypnotist's account of what people say under regression — told as testimony,
not as recovered history. Every session in this book is reported the way it
happened: this is what the person said, this is what they felt, this is what
changed for them afterwards. Where a session touches something that can be
checked against the record — a date, a place, a named person, an object — the
book checks it and says plainly what it found, including when the record says
no.

The book is not a debunking. It takes the chair seriously, and it takes the
reader seriously enough not to dress a session up as proof.

## Who it is for

Readers who are drawn to past-life regression, QHHT and between-lives work,
and who have read books in this niche that made claims they could disprove
with a search — and who lost trust in the whole shelf because of it. They want
the experience, the stories and the practical guidance. They do not want to be
asked to believe something demonstrably wrong as the price of admission.

## What the reader can do by the end

- Tell the difference, in any account of a regression, between what was said in
  the chair and what is being claimed about the world.
- Know what a session actually feels like, how it is set up, and what a
  practitioner does and does not do.
- Prepare for their own session: what to bring, what to ask, what a good
  practitioner will refuse to promise.
- Read the rest of this shelf with a working filter instead of either
  swallowing it whole or throwing it out.

## Topics to cover, in no fixed order

- What a regression session is, minute by minute: induction, the questions, the
  person's own words, the return. Written so a reader who has never done one
  knows exactly what happens.
- The difference between testimony and history, made concrete: the same session
  reported both ways, side by side, so the reader sees what changes.
- Sessions in full: several accounts, each with what the person said, what they
  felt, what it did for them — and a short, honest note on what part of it
  could be checked and what came back.
- Where checkable claims come from and why they go wrong: names, dates, famous
  figures, buried objects. Why these are the first thing a sceptical reader
  looks up, and the first thing that loses the book its reader.
- What repeats across sessions and what does not: the patterns a practitioner
  sees again and again, described as patterns and not as proof.
- Why it helps even when it cannot be verified: grief, fear, recurring dreams,
  the things people arrive with and what shifts. The evidence here is the
  person's life afterwards, and that is the claim the book makes.
- What the practitioner will not do: no diagnosis, no prophecy, no guarantees,
  no reading of another person who is not in the room.
- How to choose a practitioner, and the questions that separate a serious one
  from a performer.
- Preparing for a first session, and what to do with what comes out of it in
  the weeks after.
- Where the field's own literature stands: what is honestly reported, what is
  claimed beyond the evidence, and how to read the difference.

## What must NOT be in it

- Any statement about a real, named person — living or dead — presented as fact
  on the strength of a session.
- Dates, places, archaeological claims or historical events asserted as true
  without a source that holds.
- Promises: healing, cure, life change, contact, guaranteed outcomes.
- Contempt for the reader's beliefs, or for the practitioners in this field.
- The name of any other book, author or practitioner used as a foil.

## Tone

Warm and first-person, the voice of someone who sits with people in the chair.
Plain words. Unhurried. Honest about uncertainty without being cold about it —
the reader should finish a chapter feeling respected, not corrected.
