# What the book covers — What They Remembered

## What the book is about

A hypnotist's account of what people say under regression — told as testimony,
not as recovered history. Every session in this book is reported the way it
happened: this is what the person said, this is what they felt, this is what
changed for them afterwards. Where a session touches something that can be
checked against the record — a date, a place, a named person, an object — the
book checks it and says plainly what it found, including when the record says
no.

The book is not a debunking. It takes the chair seriously, and it takes the
reader seriously enough not to dress a session up as proof.

## Who it is for

Readers who are drawn to past-life regression, QHHT and between-lives work,
and who have read books in this niche that made claims they could disprove
with a search — and who lost trust in the whole shelf because of it. They want
the experience, the stories and the practical guidance. They do not want to be
asked to believe something demonstrably wrong as the price of admission.

## What the reader can do by the end

- Tell the difference, in any account of a regression, between what was said in
  the chair and what is being claimed about the world.
- Know what a session actually feels like, how it is set up, and what a
  practitioner does and does not do.
- Prepare for their own session: what to bring, what to ask, what a good
  practitioner will refuse to promise.
- Read the rest of this shelf with a working filter instead of either
  swallowing it whole or throwing it out.

## Topics to cover, in no fixed order

- What a regression session is, minute by minute: induction, the questions, the
  person's own words, the return. Written so a reader who has never done one
  knows exactly what happens.
- The difference between testimony and history, made concrete: the same session
  reported both ways, side by side, so the reader sees what changes.
- Sessions in full: several accounts, each with what the person said, what they
  felt, what it did for them — and a short, honest note on what part of it
  could be checked and what came back.
- Where checkable claims come from and why they go wrong: names, dates, famous
  figures, buried objects. Why these are the first thing a sceptical reader
  looks up, and the first thing that loses the book its reader.
- What repeats across sessions and what does not: the patterns a practitioner
  sees again and again, described as patterns and not as proof.
- Why it helps even when it cannot be verified: grief, fear, recurring dreams,
  the things people arrive with and what shifts. The evidence here is the
  person's life afterwards, and that is the claim the book makes.
- What the practitioner will not do: no diagnosis, no prophecy, no guarantees,
  no reading of another person who is not in the room.
- How to choose a practitioner, and the questions that separate a serious one
  from a performer.
- Preparing for a first session, and what to do with what comes out of it in
  the weeks after.
- Where the field's own literature stands: what is honestly reported, what is
  claimed beyond the evidence, and how to read the difference.

## What must NOT be in it

- Any statement about a real, named person — living or dead — presented as fact
  on the strength of a session.
- Dates, places, archaeological claims or historical events asserted as true
  without a source that holds.
- Promises: healing, cure, life change, contact, guaranteed outcomes.
- Contempt for the reader's beliefs, or for the practitioners in this field.
- The name of any other book, author or practitioner used as a foil.

## Tone

Warm and first-person, the voice of someone who sits with people in the chair.
Plain words. Unhurried. Honest about uncertainty without being cold about it —
the reader should finish a chapter feeling respected, not corrected.
STRUTTURA COMPLETA DEL LIBRO
Tesi portante: A regression session is testimony: an accurate record of what a person said and felt in a chair, and not a record of the world outside it. This book reports eight sessions in full, checks every claim inside them that can be checked, says plainly what came back, and argues that what the work does for a person is worth taking seriously whether or not the past life is.
1. A Note on How This Book Reports Things — Before any session is told, the reader is given the ground rules: where the accounts come from, what has been changed to protect the people in them, what is reported as testimony and what has been checked against the record. Short, plain and binding on every page that follows.
2. The Question Everyone Asks Me First — Opens with the question every new client and every sceptic asks — do you believe it's real? — and gives the answer that shapes the entire book: I can tell you what was said, and I can tell you what checks out, and those are two different reports. Sets the terms of the deal between writer and reader before a single session is told.
3. Testimony and History Are Not the Same Word — Makes the book's central distinction concrete and usable rather than philosophical: testimony is a true account of what a person experienced, history is a claim about the world, and a session produces the first while readers are routinely sold the second. Gives the reader the distinction as a tool they will use in every chapter that follows.
4. The Same Session, Told Twice — Takes one short session and reports it two ways on facing pages: once as testimony, once the way this shelf usually tells it. Nothing is added between the two versions and nothing is invented — only the framing changes — and the reader sees exactly how much a reader is asked to accept when the framing shifts.
5. Six Words I Refuse to Use, and What I Say Instead — The vocabulary of honesty, given as a working list: six words common in this field that smuggle in a claim, and the plainer phrases I use in their place with clients and in this book. Shows that the discipline is not a matter of disclaimers but of ordinary word choice, sentence by sentence.
6. What Gets Written Down, and What Gets Lost — How the record in this book was made: the recording, my notes, the consent, and the changes I make to protect the people in the chair. States plainly that names and identifying details are altered and that some accounts combine sessions, so the reader knows the provenance of every word before the first session begins.
7. Before Anyone Closes Their Eyes — The hour before the session — the intake conversation — which decides more about what happens than the induction does. Shows what I ask, what I listen for, what I refuse to take on, and how a client's expectations get named out loud before they can be mistaken for content.
8. The Induction, Minute by Minute — A complete walk through the induction for a reader who has never had a session: what is said, what the body does, how long each stage takes, what it feels like from the inside. Demystifies the part of the work that is usually described in either clinical or mystical language and almost never in plain minutes.
9. The Questions I Ask, and the Ones I Have Learned Not To — The most consequential craft in this work: a question can hand a client the answer without either of them noticing. Sets out the open questions I use, the leading ones I have stripped from my practice, and what happened in the sessions that taught me the difference.
10. Their Words, Not Mine — What a session actually sounds like on the recording, as opposed to how it reads when it has been tidied. Presents unedited stretches of speech — fragmentary, repetitive, present tense, oddly specific about small things — so the reader learns the texture of genuine session talk and can hear when an account has been smoothed.
11. Coming Back: the Last Ten Minutes Nobody Writes About — The return is the part of the session that books skip and practitioners cannot afford to, because it determines what the client walks out carrying. Covers the count up, the re-orientation, the first thing people say, and the care that belongs to the hour after rather than the hour during.
12. What I Will Not Do in That Room — The refusals that define a serious practice: no diagnosis, no prophecy, no guarantees, no reading of a person who is not present. Explains the reasoning behind each one and what I say when a client pushes, which they sometimes do and sometimes for heartbreaking reasons.
13. The Woman Who Counted Stairs — The first full session in the book, reported end to end: a client who arrived about something else entirely and spent forty minutes describing a staircase she counted every time she climbed it. Includes what was said, what she felt, what changed for her, and the short note on what in it could be checked.
14. The Man Who Could Not Swim — A session with a client whose fear of open water had survived three courses of ordinary treatment, and what arrived when he stopped trying to explain it. The chapter is as interested in the fear as in the content, because the fear is the part that can be observed before and after.
15. A Kitchen, a Window, and a Name She Could Not Pronounce — A session unusually rich in specifics — a room described in detail, a name repeated a dozen times — which is exactly the kind of account that gets presented elsewhere as evidence. Reports it in full and then reports the search honestly: the record has nothing to say about it either way.
16. The Session That Checked Out, and Why I Still Won't Call It Proof — The one session in this book where a specific detail matched something findable in the record — and the follow-up conversation that showed where the client had almost certainly met that detail before, at eight years old, on a school trip. The most honest chapter in the book and the one readers will argue with.
17. The Soldier Who Would Not Give a Date — A session with a client who described a war in vivid sensory detail and, asked directly, could not or would not say when or where. Uses the refusal as a way into a pattern that is common in this work and almost never reported, because it makes for a less satisfying story.
18. Two Sisters, One Room, Two Different Lives — Two clients who booked together, sat in the same chair a fortnight apart, and produced accounts with almost nothing in common. Reports both, notes the two small places they touched, and uses the pair to show how much a session is shaped by the person rather than the procedure.
19. The Session I Stopped — An account of a session I ended early, why I ended it, and what I did next — the chapter that exists because duty of care is not a disclaimer but a decision made in real time. Names the signals I watch for and the referral I made afterwards.
20. What a Session Sounds Like When Nothing Happens — The last of the eight full sessions is the one that produced nothing: no scenes, no voice, no lifetime, an hour of a man lying quietly and reporting the colour behind his eyelids. It is in the book because leaving it out is how this shelf ends up misrepresenting the work.
21. Eight Sessions, One Threshold: What Repeats and What Never Does — Steps back from the eight accounts and reports the patterns across them as patterns: thresholds, staircases, water, the reluctance to give numbers, the sensory details that arrive first. States clearly that a recurring pattern is a fact about sessions, not evidence about lives.
22. Why Almost Nobody Is a Nobody — Deals with the famous-lifetime problem directly and without mockery: why prominent identities surface far more often than population arithmetic allows, what I say when one arrives in my room, and how to hold the session respectfully while declining the claim.
23. Dates, Names, and the Records That Are Supposed to Settle Them — What actually happens when you try to check a session against the documentary record: which records exist, how patchy they are, what a match is worth and what an absence does not settle. The chapter that keeps the reader from over-reading both a hit and a miss.
24. Buried Objects, Buried Arguments — The physical-evidence claim — the box under the floor, the ring in the wall, the thing that would settle it — and why it almost never survives contact with the actual world. Treats the wish behind the claim seriously while explaining why I no longer chase them.
25. The First Thing a Sceptical Reader Looks Up — Written for the reader who has already been burned: a straight account of how one checkable error destroys the credibility of an entire book, why it is the first thing anyone verifies, and what an author owes a reader who is willing to check. Explains the standard this book has tried to hold itself to.
26. Where the Material Comes From When It Isn't a Past Life — Lays out the ordinary explanations — forgotten sources, suggestion, the mind's talent for narrative — fully and fairly, because a reader who has not met them cannot evaluate anything in this book. Written without condescension: these explanations account for a great deal and are not an insult to anyone's experience.
27. Reading the Rest of the Shelf — Hands the reader a working filter for every other account of this work they will encounter, without naming or disparaging any of it. Six questions to ask of any regression book, article or video, each one answerable from the text itself in under a minute.
28. The Grief That Moved, and the Claim I Am Making About It — The book's positive argument, stated precisely: what changes for people after this work is observable, reportable and worth something, and it is a claim about lives afterwards rather than lives before. Distinguishes it carefully from a promise, which this book does not make.
29. Recurring Dreams, Old Fears, and the Weeks After a Session — What people bring to the chair and what tends to shift — dreams, fears, an unexplained pull towards a place — and, practically, what to do with the material in the fortnight after a session when it is still moving. The most useful chapter for a reader who has just had one.
30. How to Tell a Serious Practitioner From a Performer — A buyer's chapter: the questions to ask before booking, the answers that should reassure you, and the ones that should end the conversation. Written from inside the profession, including the practices I think are indefensible and why.
31. Your First Session: What to Bring, What to Ask, What Nobody Can Promise — The practical close: how to prepare for a first session, what to carry into the room, what to establish before you close your eyes, and what a reasonable expectation looks like. Ends the book where it began, with testimony rather than promises.
32. What I Think, for What It Is Worth — Having kept my own opinion out of thirty chapters, I give it here, clearly marked as an opinion and not as a finding. Closes the book by repeating the only claim it has actually made and leaving the larger question where it belongs, with the reader.

This is a book about what people say when they are lying in a reclining chair with their eyes closed, being asked gentle questions about a life they do not remember having lived. It is also a book about the difference between reporting that and claiming something about the world. Those two things get run together constantly on this shelf, and running them together is the reason so many readers arrive here already suspicious.

So before a single session is told, here is how the book works. It takes four minutes to read and it binds every page that follows.

## What these accounts are

The sessions in this book are written accounts, not transcripts. They are built from the shape that sessions of this kind take: the pauses in the same places, the details that arrive first, the questions that open something and the questions that close it. Where you read a stretch of speech in quotation marks, you are reading prose written to sound the way people sound in that chair, because that texture is part of what I want you to understand. You are not reading a recording of an identifiable person, and nothing in this book asks you to believe that a particular named individual said a particular sentence on a particular afternoon.

I have been as plain about that here as I know how to be, at the front, in the first hundred words, rather than in small type at the back. A book whose entire argument is that accounts of this work should be honest about what they are cannot begin by being cagey about what it is.

What that costs the book: you cannot treat any session here as a data point. Nobody should. What it buys the book: everything else in it can be taken at face value, which on this shelf is not nothing.

## The rule the book keeps

Every session in these pages is reported as testimony. That means the book tells you what was said and what was felt, and stops there. When something in a session touches the world outside the room — a name, a date, a place, an object, a claim about how something was built or where something was buried — the book says so, says what checking such a thing would actually involve, and says what tends to come back. Usually what comes back is nothing. The chapters that deal with that say nothing, in those words, rather than going quiet and moving on to the next story.

You will notice this most in the eight full session chapters in the middle of the book. Each one ends with a short, flat note about what in it could be checked. Four of those notes report that there was nothing checkable in the session at all. One of them reports a match, and then spends three pages explaining why the match is worth less than it looks. That proportion is not modesty. It is what this work is actually like, and a book that reversed the proportion would be lying to you with true sentences.
