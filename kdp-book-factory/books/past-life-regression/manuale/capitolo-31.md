# Your First Session: What to Bring, What to Ask, What Nobody Can Promise

If you are going to do this, here is how to arrive well.

None of it is complicated and none of it will make the session work. What it will do is remove the ordinary obstacles, which are mostly to do with being uncomfortable, being frightened of the wrong things, and arriving with an unexamined idea of what is supposed to happen.

## The week before

**Sleep normally.** Not extra — normally. People sometimes decide to be very well rested for it, sleep ten hours the night before and then cannot settle in the chair because they are not tired enough to stop fidgeting. Your ordinary state is the right state.

**Eat before you come.** An hour or two before, something ordinary. Doing this on an empty stomach means spending the last half hour aware of your stomach, and there is a reasonable chance you will be hungry afterwards anyway.

**Do not read up on it.** This is the one that will be hardest, because you are the sort of person who reads about things — you are reading this. A week of reading session accounts before your own will fill your head with other people's material, and in that chair you will not be able to tell what is yours.

This applies to this book as much as any other. If you have an appointment in the next fortnight, put it down until afterwards. I mean that, and I am aware of what it costs me to say it.

**And do not do anything unusual the night before.** No fasting, no substances, no all-night preparation, no ritual. It does not help and it changes how you arrive.

## What to bring

**A question, not a hypothesis.**

This is the single most useful piece of preparation available and it takes five minutes.

A hypothesis is: *I think I drowned, and that's why I'm frightened of water.* Arrive with that and you will produce water, and neither you nor the practitioner will be able to tell whether it came from you or from the expectation. You have written the ending.

A question is: *why am I frightened of water?* — or better, *what's there when I stop trying to explain it?* It points at the same territory without specifying what is in it. It leaves the afternoon room to surprise you, which is the only way anything interesting happens.

Write it down. Take it with you. A good practitioner will ask you for it.

**A short honest history.** What you have already tried, who you have already seen, what did and did not help. Not a document — just have it straight in your head, because the intake will ask and vagueness there costs you.

**Something warm.** You will cool down. Most practitioners have blankets; bring your own layer anyway.

**Nothing else.** You do not need crystals, notes, photographs or objects belonging to relatives. If someone has told you to bring those, reread the previous chapter.

## What to establish before the chair reclines

Say these out loud, in the practitioner's hearing, and listen to the answers. Not as a test — as an agreement.

**What you would rather not find.** If there is something, say so. Say it even if you are embarrassed, and say it especially if you are embarrassed. A good practitioner will write it down and will not go there.

**That you can stop.** Say it yourself: *if I want to stop, I say stop, and we stop.* Hearing yourself say it does something that hearing them say it does not.

**That they will not tell you what it means.** Establish this now rather than discovering it at the door. If they will tell you what it means, you have chosen the wrong person, and it is better to find out standing up.

**What happens to the recording.** Thirty seconds.

**And what you are expecting.** Say it, however foolish it sounds. *I think I'll get nothing.* *I think I'll be somewhere hot.* *I think it'll be about my father.* Once it has been said in the room by both of you, it becomes a known object rather than a hidden one, and if something matching it turns up you will both be able to hold it more lightly.

That last one is the piece of advice I would keep if I could only keep one.

## On the day

**Arrive early and sit in the car for ten minutes.** Or on a wall, or in a café. Arriving straight from a motorway or a difficult morning into a reclining chair does not work; the settling takes twice as long and sometimes does not happen at all. Ten minutes of doing nothing beforehand is worth more than anything else on this list.

**Go to the lavatory.** I am not going to dress this up. An hour and a half is a long time and the single most common reason a session gets interrupted is entirely ordinary.

**Wear something you can be still in.** Nothing that digs in when you are horizontal, nothing with a waistband you will become aware of at minute forty. Take your shoes off if you are offered.

**Say if you are uncomfortable.** At any point. People lie rigidly still in a chair that is hurting them because they think moving would spoil it. Moving does not spoil anything. Lying in discomfort spoils everything, because the discomfort keeps pulling your attention back and nothing else can happen.

**And expect the first twenty minutes to be boring.** They are supposed to be. If you spend them waiting for something to happen, you will be monitoring yourself, and monitoring is the one state in which this does not work. The best thing you can do with the induction is find it tedious.

## What a reasonable expectation looks like

Here it is, flatly.

There is roughly a one in four chance that you will get nothing much, and a meaningful chance you will get nothing at all. That is not a reflection on you and it does not mean you are resistant or closed or insufficiently spiritual. It happens to sceptics and believers at about the same rate.

If you do get something, it will probably not look like the accounts you have read. It will be fragments: a temperature, a texture, an unnamed person, something you are doing with your hands. It will very likely not contain a date, a place name or anything you could look up, and there is every chance it will not resolve into a story at all. You may find yourself describing a wall for twenty minutes.

You will be awake throughout and you will remember all of it. You will probably think it did not work.

Afterwards, you may feel that something has shifted. You may not. Both are ordinary, and which one happens is not predictable from how good the session seemed at the time — the most vivid session I ran last year was followed by nothing at all, and a dull hour in a field was followed by a woman finally putting down a grief she had carried for two years.

And you will not find out whether any of it was real. That question will not resolve, this afternoon or ever, and a practitioner who offers to resolve it for you is offering something they do not have.

## The sentence to be suspicious of

At the end, when you are standing at the door with your coat on and you ask the question everybody asks, listen to what comes back.

If it is *yes, that was a real life* — or any of its softer cousins, *that felt very authentic*, *you clearly went somewhere real*, *I've heard that before and it always checks out* — then you have been handed something that is not available to the person handing it to you. They were in the room. So were you. Neither of you has any access whatsoever to whether there was a kitchen with a stone floor.

What you want to hear is something like what I say: that what happened to you happened, that you were not performing, that the material is yours, and that whether it corresponds to anything is not knowable by either of you.

It is a smaller answer. It is also the only one that will still be true in six months, when you have looked things up and thought about it and told one person and reread what you wrote that night.

## Where this leaves you

With an afternoon, and an account of it in your own handwriting, and a question that has not been answered.

That is what this is. It is less than the shelf promises and more than the sceptics allow, and the people I have sat with have, on the whole, found it worth the time.

Go in with a question rather than a hypothesis, say your expectation out loud, write it down that night, and hold the whole thing loosely for a fortnight. That is everything I know about doing this well, and it is the same advice whether you end up thinking you have found a life or a very good story about a wall.
