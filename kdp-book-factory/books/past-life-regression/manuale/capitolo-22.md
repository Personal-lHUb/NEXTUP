# Why Almost Nobody Is a Nobody

Somebody prominent turns up in my room perhaps twice a year.

I want to handle this without mockery, because mockery is the standard treatment and it is both cruel and lazy. A person who arrives at the end of a session believing they have found something extraordinary about themselves is not a fool. They have had a vivid experience, they have reached the obvious conclusion, and the people who laugh at them have usually never lain in that chair.

But the arithmetic has to be done, and nobody does it out loud.

## The arithmetic

Estimates of how many human beings have ever lived vary, and the variation is not small — the number depends on where you start counting and what you count, and reasonable people put it anywhere from the high tens of billions upwards. Call it a very large number and leave it there; the argument does not need precision.

Now the other number: how many of those people would a person today have heard of? Not scholars — people. Names that would mean something to a client lying in a chair.

Even generously — every ruler with a recognisable name, every figure from every popular film and school syllabus, every saint and general and inventor anyone could place — you are talking about a few thousand. Perhaps ten thousand if you are being very liberal about it.

Divide one by the other. The proportion of all human lives that are recognisable to a modern person is something in the order of one in ten million, and that is being kind.

If session material were drawn at random from lived human experience, a practitioner running a session a week for a working lifetime would expect to encounter a recognisable name approximately never. Not rarely. Never, in a hundred careers.

I get two a year.

## What that tells you, and what it does not

It does not tell you the clients are lying. I want to say that clearly, because it is the conclusion people jump to and it is wrong. In my experience the clients are never lying. The material arrives the same way all session material arrives — unbidden, in fragments, with the same physical signs and the same texture.

What it tells you is that the identification is being supplied, and that it is being supplied from the obvious place: the enormously disproportionate representation of a few thousand people in everything we read, watch and are taught. Those names are available in a way that a fourteenth-century weaver's name is not available, because nobody knows the weaver's name, including the weaver's descendants.

A mind producing a life under these conditions will reach for what it has. What it has is what it has been given.

There is a second mechanism, which is subtler and which I think does more work. In most of these cases the client does not begin with a famous name. They begin with ordinary material — a room, a weight, a fear — and then, at some point, an identification arrives and fixes it. That order matters. The material is not the identification; the identification is an interpretation laid over the material, and it usually arrives late in the session or afterwards.

## What I do in the room

Nothing.

I do not contradict a client mid-session. Not ever, on any subject. A person in that state who is told they are wrong will either shut down entirely or start trying to produce acceptable material, and both outcomes end the useful part of the afternoon.

So when a name arrives, I treat it exactly as I treat a pan or a cold wall: *tell me about that.* And then I go on asking the same three questions and keep my own view completely out of the room, which is easier than it sounds because I do not have a view about what is happening, only about what it is evidence of.

What I do do, carefully, is notice whether the material changes after the identification arrives. It usually does, and always in the same direction: it becomes more general, more confident, and more like what you would find in a history book. Before the name, a client is describing an ache in a shoulder. After the name, they are describing a period.

That transition is, I think, the single most telling thing I have observed in this work. It is what supplying a frame looks like from the outside, except that in this case nobody in the room supplied it.

## The conversation afterwards

This is the part that requires care, and I have got it wrong in both directions.

I have been too blunt, early on, with a woman who left upset and did not deserve it. And I have been too soft, more than once, letting somebody walk out with an identification intact because the conversation was going to be difficult and I did not have the appetite for it.

What I do now is offer the arithmetic, without applying it. I say something close to this: *Can I tell you the thing I find hardest about this work? The numbers don't work. There have been tens of billions of us and we know the names of a few thousand, and yet that's who turns up in this room. I don't know what to do with that, and I've never known what to do with it.*

That is true, it is not a verdict about them, and it puts the problem in the middle of the table where both of us can look at it.

Most people engage with it. Some of them have already thought about it — more than you would expect. A few say, reasonably, that it is not a knock-down argument, and they are right: it is a probabilistic point, and probabilistic points do not settle individual cases.

And one or two are not interested, and take the identification away with them, and there is nothing I can do about that and it is not my business to try. They are adults. They came for an afternoon, not for a supervision.

## Honouring the session, declining the identification

These are separable, and the whole chapter turns on their being separable.

A woman describes a room, a weight of expectation, a fear of a man's footsteps, and a sense of being watched. Then a name arrives. The name is almost certainly not evidence of anything. The room, the weight, the fear and the watching are hers, they came from somewhere, and they were the most affecting thing that happened to her that year.

I can take all of that seriously and still think the name came from a television series. There is no contradiction. The mistake — made by believers and debunkers alike, in opposite directions — is treating the identification as the load-bearing part. For the believer, it is what makes the session significant. For the debunker, it is what makes the session ridiculous. Both are looking at the label rather than the contents.

What I say, in some form, to almost everyone: *the name is the part I'd hold most lightly, and it's the part that matters least. Everything else you described is yours.*

## Why the objection does not quite work

There is a standard reply to everything in this chapter and it deserves a proper answer rather than a dismissal.

The reply goes: people who are drawn to this work are not a random sample of humanity. Perhaps the sort of person who ends up in a regression chair is, for whatever reason, more likely to have had a life that left a trace. Or perhaps lives that were intense or unfinished are more likely to surface, and intensity correlates with the kind of life that gets recorded.

I have heard both versions put seriously, by thoughtful people, and neither is absurd.

Here is why I do not think either rescues the numbers. Both arguments require a selection effect of an extraordinary magnitude. To move from one in ten million to one in a few hundred — which is roughly my observed rate — you need a filter operating with a strength that has no parallel anywhere else in human affairs. It is not enough for prominent lives to be somewhat over-represented; they would have to be over-represented by a factor in the tens of thousands.

And there is a second problem, which is that the selection effect would have to be remarkably well-informed. The famous names that turn up in my room are, overwhelmingly, the ones that are famous *now*, in this country, to a person with an ordinary education. They track the school syllabus and the television schedules with a fidelity that is difficult to explain by any mechanism that is not simply availability.

That is the part I would put to anyone who wants to defend the identifications. Not "the numbers are against you," which is a blunt instrument, but: why do the names move when the culture moves?

## The one I could not place

I will end with the case that does not fit my own argument, because a chapter like this should have one.

A man in his forties produced, over about twenty minutes, a detailed and consistent account of a life that was entirely unremarkable, in a place he could not name, doing work he described in enough detail that I could tell it was skilled. No identification, no name, nothing famous, nothing to check. A completely ordinary life.

And at the very end, without prompting, he said: "Nobody knows about him. That's all right, though."

I have no idea what that was. It is the opposite of the pattern this chapter describes, it has no evidential value whatsoever, and I have thought about it more than I have thought about any of the famous ones.
