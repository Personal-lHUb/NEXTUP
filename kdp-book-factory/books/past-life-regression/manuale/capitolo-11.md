# Coming Back: the Last Ten Minutes Nobody Writes About

Accounts of this work almost always end with the material. The client describes the last thing, there is a paragraph of reflection, and the chapter closes. What happened between the last described image and the client walking out of the building is not considered to be part of the story.

It is at least a third of the job, and if it goes badly it is the part the client is still dealing with a fortnight later.

## How I bring somebody back

Slowly, and never on my own schedule.

The first move is not to move. When the material thins out — and it thins rather than stops; sessions almost never end on a beat — I do nothing for a while. A minute, sometimes two. Clients frequently produce one more thing in that minute, and it is disproportionately often the most interesting thing of the afternoon, arriving after they have effectively finished.

Then I say that we are going to come back in a little while, and that there is no hurry. Then I wait again. The announcement matters: nobody should be lifted out of that state without warning, and the gap between saying it and doing it gives them time to leave whatever they are in, in their own way.

The count up is slow, from one to five, with a sentence between each number. Not a dramatic countdown — the opposite, a sort of unhurried arithmetic. By three I am talking about the room, the chair, the blanket, the sound of the traffic. By five I am talking about their feet.

Then they open their eyes, and then I stop talking entirely for a minute or so, because the worst thing you can do to somebody at that moment is ask them a question.

I have never brought anyone back quickly and I am aware that some practitioners do, on the grounds that the state is light and the transition is trivial. The state is light. The transition is not trivial, because what has to happen is not a change of consciousness but a change of position: the person has been inside something and now has to be outside it, looking at it, deciding what it was. That takes a few minutes and it cannot be hurried by counting faster.

## The first sentence

I have been collecting these for years — the first thing a client says once they are properly back — and the collection has taught me more about this work than most of what I have read.

The commonest by a distance is some version of *that was odd*. Not profound, not shaken: odd. Said in the tone of somebody who has found a door in their house they had not noticed.

The second commonest is a question about the time. People are consistently wrong about how long they have been there, and almost always in the same direction: they guess short. An hour and fifty minutes is routinely estimated at forty.

The third is an apology. *Sorry, I don't think I did that very well.* This one is the reason I now say something before they can get to it. People arrive at the end of a session convinced they have under-performed, and it is unrelated to how much material there was; the ones who produced the most apologise as often as the ones who produced nothing.

And the fourth, which is rarer and which I have learned to pay attention to, is a flat statement about something entirely outside the session. *I need to ring my sister.* Nothing to do with anything described in the chair. Those are usually worth following.

What almost nobody says, incidentally, is anything about the content. The interpretation comes days later, if it comes.

## The ordinary physical aftermath

People are not prepared for this, and nobody writes about it, so here it is in plain terms.

They are often cold, and shivery for a few minutes in a way that is out of proportion to the room temperature. The blanket stays on.

They are frequently hungry. Genuinely, urgently hungry, the way you are after swimming. I keep biscuits in the room for no more elevated reason than that.

Some people are groggy for half an hour, in the manner of an afternoon nap that went on too long. Some are the opposite: bright, talkative, slightly overwound, and those are the ones I keep longest, because the brightness tends to drop away sharply about an hour later.

Tears are common and are not a sign of anything having gone wrong. They often arrive after the session rather than during it, sometimes without any accompanying emotion the client can identify, which alarms people considerably and should not.

And a proportion get a headache, mild, frontal, lasting an hour or two. I have no explanation. I mention it because a client who gets one and has not been told it happens will spend the evening wondering what has been done to them.

## What I say before they drive home

Four things, in the same order every time, and I keep them until the client is fully upright and has had something to drink.

That they should not decide what any of it means today. The urge to interpret is strongest in the first hour and the interpretation formed then is the one that sticks, and it is usually the most obvious reading rather than the best one.

That they should write it down before they sleep, in the way described earlier in this book — what arrived, not what it means, including the parts that did not fit.

That whatever they are feeling this evening is likely to be different tomorrow, and different again at a week.

And that they should not drive for at least half an hour. Most do not need it. The ones who do need it need it badly, and there is no way to tell in advance which is which.

## The two-day note

I send a short message two days after every session. It has three lines in it and it never varies much: that I hope they are well, that anything coming up is normal, and that they can write back if they want to.

About a third reply. Of those, most say something brief and friendly. Perhaps one in six says something substantial, and it is nearly always the same sort of thing: that something has shifted, or that a detail from the session has attached itself to something in their actual life, or that they have been sleeping differently.

The reason for the note is not therapeutic ambition. It is that the window in which a person most needs to be able to ask a question is two to four days out, and by then the session feels like a transaction that concluded, and most people will not initiate contact about a transaction that concluded. Removing that barrier costs me four lines.

It also, I will admit, gives me the only follow-up data I have. Everything I know about what this work does for people comes from those replies and from the conversations that occasionally follow them, and it is a thin and self-selected record. I would rather say so than present it as something sturdier.

## The hour after they leave

This part is about me rather than the client, and I include it because practitioners reading this will recognise it and because it bears on how reliable any of my accounts are.

I do not book two sessions in a day. I did for a while, early on, when the work was new and the money mattered more, and the second session of the day was consistently worse — not because I was tired, but because I was still holding the first one. I would find myself noticing patterns between them that were almost certainly not there.

So there is an hour afterwards, and it has a shape. I listen to nothing and write nothing for the first twenty minutes or so; I wash the cups, open the window, put the blanket over the back of the chair. Then I write the session up from the notebook while it is still close, in the present tense, attributed line by line, exactly as described earlier in this book.

The writing-up takes about forty minutes for a full session and it is the least enjoyable part of the job. It is also the part that has to happen that day. I know this because I have tested it against the recordings: a write-up done the same afternoon and a write-up done three days later are noticeably different documents, and the later one is smoother, more confident and less accurate in exactly the ways you would expect.

There is an emotional component too, and it would be dishonest to leave it out of a chapter about the last ten minutes. Some sessions stay with you. A woman crying about a child she could not name is not something you put down by washing a cup, and after a few of those in a month I am noticeably worse company at home. Practitioners in this field talk about this rarely and should talk about it more; there is no supervision structure of the kind clinicians have, and most of us are managing it privately and improvising.

What I have settled on is unremarkable: a limit on sessions per week, the hour afterwards, and one person I can describe a difficult afternoon to, anonymised, who is not a client and not a practitioner.

## The one I got wrong

Early on, I ended a session with a woman who had become distressed near the end, and I brought her back carefully, and she was composed, and we talked for twenty minutes, and she left.

I did not send a note, because the note was not yet part of what I did. She wrote to me eleven days later to say that she had been badly unsettled for most of a week and had not known whether that was expected or whether something had gone wrong, and that she had not wanted to bother me.

Nothing had gone wrong. What had happened was that I had handled the hour and neglected the fortnight, and she had spent eleven days assuming her reaction was aberrant because nobody had told her otherwise. Every practice I have described in this chapter — the delay before speaking, the four things said at the end, the note — came out of that letter.

It is the least dramatic chapter in this book and it is the one I would most want a new practitioner to read.
