# The Soldier Who Would Not Give a Date

Callum was thirty-nine and came because of a smell.

He had been having, for about four years, an intermittent experience he found difficult to describe and slightly embarrassing to raise: a smell that arrived without any source, in ordinary situations, perhaps once a month. He described it as "wet ash and something sweet underneath." It was accompanied by a strong sense of urgency and no content whatsoever.

He had seen a doctor about it, sensibly, and had been checked for the things that ought to be checked for. Nothing had come of that. He had arrived at my door as the next thing to try, with an attitude he described as "I've run out of sensible options."

## The intake

He was direct, which made everything easier.

When I asked what he expected, he said: "I expect you'll tell me I was a soldier. Everybody was a soldier."

I said that back to him, as I do, and we established it as the expectation. I also said, because he had raised it himself and it was going to sit in the room either way, that if something soldierly did turn up we would both have to hold it loosely for exactly that reason.

He said, "Noted," and lay back.

## The material

He settled quickly and the material arrived almost immediately, which usually means a poor session and in this case did not.

> — What are you aware of?
>
> — Weight. It's on my shoulders and it's pulling back.
>
> — Tell me about the weight.
>
> — It's high up, it's up here, it's not on the — *(pause)* — the straps are cutting in.

Then, over the next forty minutes, in pieces:

Mud, described at length and with considerable feeling — the depth of it, the effort of pulling a foot out, the way it holds. He came back to the mud perhaps fifteen times and was more interested in it than in anything else.

Cold on the face, warm inside the clothing.

A sound he kept returning to. He described it first as "a gate," then corrected himself: "not a gate. Like a gate. Far off, and again." He was dissatisfied with his own description of it every single time, which is a thing I have learned to notice.

Hunger. Boredom, oddly — a sense of enormous tedium that he found funny afterwards.

And somebody next to him whose name he did not give and whom he referred to consistently as "him," in the manner described earlier in this book.

## Three attempts at a when

Ruth's session produced an object. Callum's produced none — nothing manufactured, nothing with a shape, nothing that could be looked at. Which left the question of when, and I asked it three times, in three different ways, over about ten minutes.

The first way was the open one: *is there anything that tells you where you are?* He said, after a pause: "In it. I'm in it."

The second was slightly narrower, and I am not entirely comfortable with having asked it: *is there anything written anywhere?* Nothing leading in that, I think, but it does presuppose writing. He said no, and then, a while later, "there's nothing to read out here," which made me laugh afterwards.

The third was the closest I came to crossing the line: *do you have a sense of the year?* He was silent for thirty-four seconds. Then he said, flatly: "No."

Not "I don't know." No. A refusal rather than an absence, and the difference was audible.

## Why I stopped

Because the next question was the one that would have worked, and I knew what it was.

If I had said *what century is it?* — or even *is it winter?* — he would have answered. People almost always answer. He was cooperative, he was deep, and he had no reason to resist a direct question about a place he was describing in detail.

And then I would have had a date, and the date would have been mine. Everything after it in the session would have arranged itself around a number I had put in the room, and the session would have become a great deal more satisfying and completely worthless. There is a chapter earlier in this book about a question I asked for two years before I understood what it was doing; this is the same lesson applied in real time, and it is harder in real time than it reads on the page.

So I said nothing more about when, and the session went back to the mud.

## What he said afterwards

He sat up and said, "Well, I was a soldier, wasn't I. Told you."

Then, after a minute: "It wasn't a battle, though. That's the bit I didn't expect. It was mostly just awful in a boring way."

That struck me too, and it is the reason this session is in the book. The expectation he walked in with — everybody was a soldier — was met, in the most literal sense. What was not met was the shape of it. He had anticipated something dramatic and got forty minutes of mud, straps and tedium, and the tedium is the part he could not stop talking about.

The smell did not come up during the session. Neither of us noticed that until afterwards, when he pointed it out himself with some irritation.

## The sound

There is one thing I have left until now.

About two months later, Callum wrote to me to say he had worked out what the sound was. He had been somewhere with a particular kind of heavy door and had heard it and had, in his words, "gone cold."

He did not tell me what the door was, and he asked me not to include the detail if I wrote about the session, because he said it was "the only bit that's mine." So I have not.

I mention that it happened because it is the sort of thing that usually gets reported in full, and because his reason for withholding it is worth more than the detail itself would have been. He did not want it examined. He was quite clear that he thought the examination would take it away from him, and he may well be right.

## The check

Nothing. Comprehensively nothing, and this time by his own decision.

No date — asked for three times, and refused, flatly, the third. No place. No name for himself, no name for the man beside him, no unit, no commander, no side. No manufactured object: mud, straps, weather and a sound.

There is a version of this session that a writer could date. Straps cutting into shoulders, deep mud, a repeated far-off percussion: those three details would be enough to make most readers supply a specific conflict, and a writer could simply let them, without ever committing to it. I am not going to do that. Callum did not say when. He said no.

I am also aware that he described mud and weight and a noise, and that human beings have been walking through mud carrying things and hearing noises for as long as there have been human beings. The picture assembles itself because we have all seen the same films.

## The smell, and what happened to it

I have avoided the obvious question for most of this chapter, so here it is: did the smell stop?

It did not. Not at two months, not at a year. Callum still gets it, at roughly the same frequency, and as far as I know he still does not know what it is.

I am putting that in because the absence of an outcome is itself a finding about this work, and because the chapter would read quite differently without it. Every other session in this book so far has had something afterwards — sleep, a swimming pool, a woman finding the afternoon remarkable. This one has a man who came about a specific problem, had an interesting afternoon, and still has the problem.

He is not dissatisfied, which surprised me. When I asked him directly whether he felt the session had been worth doing, he said yes, without hesitation, and gave a reason I did not expect: that he had spent four years assuming the smell meant something was wrong with him, and that the afternoon had not fixed it but had made it "a thing that happens rather than a symptom." That is his phrase, not a medical judgement: a smell with no source is something to keep a doctor informed about, and he has.

I do not know what to do with that and I am not going to dress it up as a result. He arrived with a problem and left with the same problem and a different relationship to it. Whether that counts for anything is his judgement, not mine, and he says it does.

What I will not do is what the genre would do here, which is quietly reclassify the smell as a past-life echo and call the matter closed. Nothing in the session referred to a smell. He never mentioned one. The connection between an afternoon of mud and an intermittent olfactory experience is a connection I would be supplying, and I have no grounds for it whatsoever.

## What the refusal is for

I have run many sessions in which a client would not, or could not, give a date, and I have almost never seen one written up.

The reason is structural. A session without a when cannot be a story about a specific past, so it cannot be verified, so it cannot be published as a case — and so the literature of this field is quietly filtered, session by session, in favour of the ones that produced numbers. That filtering is invisible. I doubt anybody decided it. Each writer, I suspect, simply chose their most interesting material, and the aggregate effect is a body of work suggesting that sessions routinely yield datable content.

They do not. In my practice, a session yielding a date the client volunteered without being asked is rare enough that I can remember each one. What sessions yield is mud.

That is less useful for making a case. It is what the work is actually like, and Callum's afternoon is in this book precisely because there is nothing to do with it.
