# The Same Session, Told Twice

What follows is one session written out twice. It was short — forty minutes of useful material from a woman I will call Ingrid, who had come about a fear of motorway driving and got this instead.

Read the first version, then the second. Both come from the same forty minutes. The first reports them; the second is written the way this material is usually written, and everything it adds I will point to afterwards. No detail of the session itself has been changed in either. I want you to see how much a reader is asked to swallow when a writer changes the framing and lets the framing bring its own details.

## Version one: what she said

Ingrid settled quickly, which not everybody does. Eleven minutes in she reported that she could feel stone under her, cold, and that she was sitting rather than standing.

She described a wall to her left and water below. She said the water was "green and thick-looking, not clean." She said there were ropes, and that one of them was across her lap, and that she was holding it but not pulling it. I asked what she was aware of and she said, after a long pause, "waiting."

She said her hands were cold and her feet were not. She said she was wearing something over her head that was "not a hat, more like a cloth," and that it was wet at the edges. She described the light as low and said it was either early or late but she could not tell which.

She used the word "him" for the first time about twenty minutes in. She said "he's late," and then, a little after, "he's always late." I asked who, and she said "my brother," in a flat voice, and then she said it again. She did not give a name for him and she did not give a name for herself; I asked once, gently, and she said "I don't — no," and I left it.

There was a stretch of about four minutes in which she said almost nothing and her breathing changed. Afterwards she told me she had been "looking at the water and thinking about how long to wait."

The strongest single moment came near the end. She said: "If he doesn't come I have to go back up on my own, and I don't want to go up on my own." She was tearful when she said it. She was not, by her own account afterwards, sad about a brother — she said the sadness had "felt like it belonged to the going up, not to him."

That is essentially the whole session. There is no ending to it. She did not describe going up, or him arriving, or anything resolving; the material simply thinned out, and I brought her back.

Afterwards she was cheerful and a bit embarrassed. She said the cold hands had been the most convincing part, because they were still cold. She said, unprompted, that she had no idea where any of it came from and that she did not usually think about the sea.

**The check.** There is nothing in this session to check. No name, no place, no date, no object, no event. A stone quay, green water, a cloth on the head, a brother who is late: there is no record in the world that could confirm or contradict any of it, and no search that would even be a sensible thing to attempt. That is the honest note, and it is a short one.

## Version two: the same forty minutes, told the other way

Under hypnosis, Ingrid returned to a life in a northern fishing port.

She found herself sitting on the harbour wall in the late afternoon, the stone cold beneath her, looking down at the brackish green water of the inner basin. The heavy mooring ropes lay coiled around her; one rested across her lap, familiar in her hands, as it had a thousand times before. She was waiting, as she had so often waited, for her brother.

She wore a woollen head-cloth, damp with spray — the everyday dress of a working woman of the port. Her hands were chapped and cold from the work. The light was failing.

He was late. He was always late. As the minutes wore on she felt the old dread rising: if he did not come, she would have to make the climb back up to the town alone, through streets where a woman alone after dark was not safe. The fear in her voice was unmistakable, and it was not the fear of a modern woman in a comfortable chair. She wept.

Ingrid emerged from the session shaken, her hands still cold to the touch an hour later — a striking instance of the body remembering what the mind has forgotten. She had never had any interest in the sea.

## What changed

Set them side by side and count. I make it seven large changes, and a handful of small ones I will leave you to spot: *late afternoon*, when she said early or late; *woollen*; *chapped*; *an hour later*.

**One.** *Returned to a life.* She described things; the second version says a life existed and she went back to it. That is the whole claim of the field, inserted in the first five words, before any evidence has been offered.

**Two.** *A northern fishing port.* She said stone, water, ropes. She did not say north, she did not say fishing, and she did not say port. Three facts about the world have been assembled out of an atmosphere.

**Three.** *The inner basin.* A harbour with an inner basin is a specific kind of harbour. She said there was a wall to her left and water below.

**Four.** *As it had a thousand times before. As she had so often waited.* Pure invention, and the most effective sentence in the passage, because it converts a single reported moment into a life with habits in it.

**Five.** *The everyday dress of a working woman of the port.* She said it was not a hat, more like a cloth, wet at the edges. The second version has told you what kind of woman she was and what her society dressed her in.

**Six.** *Streets where a woman alone after dark was not safe.* She said she did not want to go up on her own. The reason has been supplied by the writer and attributed to her, and it is the reason a modern reader will find most plausible.

**Seven.** *The body remembering what the mind has forgotten.* Her hands were cold. She noticed they were cold. The second version has turned an observation into a mechanism, and the mechanism assumes the conclusion.

There is an eighth thing, harder to point at. In version one she says "he's late" and then "he's always late," and the second sentence arrives after a pause, and the pause is interesting. In version two the pause is gone, because prose of that kind cannot carry pauses. The tidying costs you the texture, and the texture is where the strange part lives.

## What Ingrid said when I showed her both

I sent her the two versions before asking whether I could use the session. I did not tell her which I preferred, though I imagine it was obvious.

Her response is the reason this chapter exists in the form it does. She said the second one was lovely and she would rather it were true. Then she said the thing that stopped me: *the second one isn't about me.*

I asked her what she meant, and she took a while over it. She said that the woman in version two knew things — knew the harbour, knew the danger, knew her own life — and that the whole point of the experience, for her, had been not knowing. She had been sitting on cold stone with no idea where she was or who she was waiting for, and the not knowing had been the strange part, the part she had gone on thinking about for weeks. Version two had solved it. It had told her who she was and what she was frightened of, and in doing so it had taken away the only thing about the afternoon that had actually been hers.

She also said, drily, that her hands had been cold because the room was cold and she had walked from the station.

I have thought about that last remark a great deal. It is in version one, in a fashion — I wrote that she said the cold hands were the most convincing part *because they were still cold*, which is her observation, accurately reported. But I left out that there was an obvious reason for cold hands, and I left it out without deciding to, because it was not interesting. That is the smaller and more insidious version of what version two does: not adding a claim, but declining to add a deflation. A book can be built entirely out of true sentences and still mislead, purely by what it leaves lying on the floor.

## Why the second one sells

I am not going to pretend the second version is worse writing. It is better writing, by most ordinary measures. It has momentum. It has a scene and a threat and a small ache at the end. If you read the two aloud to a room, the second one holds them.

That is precisely the problem, and it is why this is not a matter of a few careless authors. The pressure runs entirely one way. Version two is what a reader picks up in a shop and reads for thirty seconds; version one is what they can still trust on page two hundred. Most books, I suspect, are bought or put back in the first thirty seconds, so the machinery of publishing — including the writer's own ear — pushes constantly toward the version that cannot survive scrutiny.

And it does not survive it. A reader who enjoys version two and then wonders which port, and starts looking, finds that there is nothing to find, because there was never anything there: the port was assembled by the author out of a wall and some rope. The reader does not conclude that the author over-wrote one passage. The reader concludes that the whole book is like this, and closes it.

I would rather have the reader at page two hundred. That is a commercial decision as much as an ethical one, and I have made it knowingly. Every session in the rest of this book is written in the first voice, and Ingrid's session, told straight, is among the least impressive material in it. I have put it at the front on purpose.
