# What a Session Sounds Like When Nothing Happens

Ray got nothing.

Not a thin session. Nothing: an hour and five minutes of a sixty-seven-year-old man lying comfortably in a reclining chair, warm, relaxed, entirely awake, reporting on the colour behind his eyelids.

This is the last of the eight session chapters in this book, and it is here because leaving it out is how the shelf ends up lying to you. Every practitioner has these. Almost nobody prints them, and the cumulative effect is a literature in which booking a session reliably produces a life, and that is not what booking a session reliably produces.

## Ray

He was a retired engineer, widowed three years, and he came because his daughter had suggested it and he had not been able to think of a reason to say no.

That is a poor reason to come and I said so, gently, in the intake. He agreed, and then said something that made me take him seriously: "I'd like an hour where I'm not doing anything. I don't get those."

His expectation, when I asked: "I think it won't work on me. I've got that sort of head."

Anything he would rather not find: "Nothing. I've had a long life and I'm not frightened of any of it."

I have no criticism of the intake. It went well and I would not do it differently.

## The first half hour

The induction was uneventful. He settled — genuinely settled, I am confident of that — in about nineteen minutes. The swallow happened at eight. His hands opened somewhere around fourteen. His breathing dropped and stayed dropped.

By every physical sign I have, he was where people are when things happen.

> — Can you tell me what you're aware of?
>
> — *(eight seconds)* Comfortable. Very comfortable, actually.
>
> — Is there anything else there?
>
> — *(twelve seconds)* It's sort of a dark red. Behind my eyes. It's gone a bit lighter at the edge.

That is the high-water mark of the entire afternoon.

## What I tried, in order

I want to set this out properly, because a reader deciding whether to book a session should know what an hour of nothing looks like from the practitioner's side.

I waited. Four minutes, twice. Waiting is the first and best tool and it costs nothing.

I went back to the body. Weight, temperature, the chair, the blanket. Sometimes a client who is not producing images is producing sensations and has not thought to mention them. Ray reported that his left foot was slightly cooler than his right, which was true and which he found mildly interesting.

I asked about sound rather than sight. Some people produce little in response to "what can you see" and a good deal more to "what can you hear," so I ask both. He heard the traffic. He heard, he said, "a pipe somewhere in the building," which there is.

I offered a different starting point. Not an image — I do not supply images — but a different kind of question: *is there anything that feels important at the moment?* He thought about it and said, "Not really, no."

I deepened further, twice, spending about six minutes on it in total.

And then, at around minute fifty, I stopped trying. That is later than I usually leave it. Every physical sign said he was there, and I kept expecting the material to follow the signs.

## Why I stopped

Because continuing would have started to be pressure, and pressure is how sessions get manufactured.

There is a thing that happens at about the fifty-minute mark of an empty session, and I would expect any honest practitioner to recognise it. The client knows nothing is happening. You know nothing is happening. Both of you are aware that money has been paid, and that the afternoon is a failure, and that the failure is more embarrassing for the client than for you.

At that point, a question with any shape at all will be answered. Not dishonestly — most clients are not going to lie. But a person who is relaxed, cooperative, slightly embarrassed and keen for the afternoon to have been worth something will, if handed a frame, furnish it. I could have got Ray a life in about four minutes. I am fairly confident of that, and I am not proud of being confident of it.

So instead I told him what was happening, out loud, in the ordinary voice: *Ray, this isn't settling into anything today, and that happens. Do you want to keep going for a bit, or shall we stop?*

He said, "I'm quite comfortable. Can we just stay like this for a while?"

So we did, for about fifteen more minutes, and neither of us said anything.

## What he said afterwards

He sat up and said: "Well, I told you."

Then, while he was drinking his water, he said the thing I have thought about most from that afternoon, and I wrote it down immediately afterwards so I have it almost exactly:

"That's the longest I've been quiet since Eileen died. I've had the radio on for three years."

I did not know what to do with that and I still do not. I said something inadequate. He was not looking for a response; he was reporting a fact, in the way an engineer reports a fact, and then he put his coat on.

## What happened afterwards

Nothing dramatic and I am not going to make it dramatic.

He replied to the two-day note with one line: "Slept well, thank you." I heard from him once more, about four months later, when he wrote to say his daughter had asked how it went and that he had told her it was "a waste of time and I'd do it again," which I take to be the highest compliment available in his idiom.

He has not booked another session. He did, he mentioned, buy a chair.

## The check

There is nothing to check because there is nothing.

I am going to leave the section that short, because the alternative — spinning three paragraphs out of an absence — would be doing the opposite of what this chapter is for.

## The three empty sessions I did not print

Ray's is the one I have written out, and there were three others I considered, and the reason I chose his rather than theirs is instructive.

There was a woman in her thirties who got nothing and was furious about it — not with me particularly, just furious, in the way you are when something you have been looking forward to for months turns out to be a wet afternoon. She was not unkind. She asked for a refund, which I gave, and she was right to ask.

There was a man who got nothing and would not accept that he had got nothing. He spent the last half hour of his session producing material that I am nearly certain he was constructing, consciously, out of politeness or stubbornness or both — the delivery was completely different, quick and fluent and in his ordinary voice — and the difficult part was afterwards, when he wanted to talk about what it meant and I had to find a way of saying that I did not think it had come from the same place as the rest.

And there was a woman who got nothing in ninety minutes and then, standing at the door with her keys in her hand, said one sentence about a staircase that was more vivid than anything in any session I ran that year. She had no interest in pursuing it. She said it the way you mention the weather and left.

I chose Ray because his was the ordinary case: no drama, no complaint, no last-minute reversal. The other three all have something in them that a writer could use, which is exactly why they are the wrong illustration. An empty session that turns out to be interesting is not an empty session.

## How often this happens

About one session in eight produces nothing at all. Another one in eight produces so little that it does not amount to anything: a colour, a temperature, a single image that goes nowhere.

That means roughly a quarter of the afternoons I run do not give the client the thing they came for. I tell people this in the intake, in those words, and I would encourage anyone booking with anybody to ask the question directly: *what proportion of your sessions produce nothing?*

If the answer is none, or if the answer is a vague reassurance about everyone getting something, you are talking to somebody who is either not counting or not telling you. The rate is not a mark against a practitioner. It is a fact about the work, and the practitioners I respect most are candid about their own number.

## Why this is the eighth session

I had a choice about what went in this slot. I have sessions that are more vivid than Ray's, more emotionally complete, more quotable. Any of them would have made a better chapter and a better book, in the short and obvious sense of better.

But eight session chapters, all of which produced a life, would be making a claim about the base rate without ever stating it. The claim would be implicit, undefended and false, and it would be delivered not by any sentence I had written but by the shape of the table of contents.

So: eight sessions that produced something, and Ray, who produced a dark red colour behind his eyes and fifteen minutes of silence and, by his own account, the quietest hour he had had in three years.
