# Reading the Rest of the Shelf

You are going to read other things about this. Books, articles, forum posts, videos, the account a friend gives you over dinner. Most of it will be sincere and some of it will be very good, and you will have no easy way of telling which is which.

So here are six questions. Each one can be answered from the text itself, usually in under a minute, usually from a single page picked at random. None of them requires you to know anything about the subject, and none of them assumes bad faith on anybody's part.

I have deliberately not named a single book, author or practitioner anywhere in these pages, and I am not going to start now. The questions work without examples; that is rather the point of them.

## One: does it separate what was said from what is being claimed?

Open at any session account and find a striking sentence. Ask which kind it is: a report about a room, or a claim about the world.

*She described a staircase and counted forty-one steps* is the first kind. *She had climbed those stairs every day of a life in service* is the second, and nobody present could know it.

A well-made account keeps them visibly apart, sentence by sentence, without needing to explain that it is doing so. A poorly made one runs them together in the same paragraph at the same speed, and the transition is invisible because the writer did not notice making it.

This is the single most informative question of the six, and it takes about twenty seconds.

## Two: where did the record come from?

Look for any statement about how the account was made. Was there a recording? Were there notes? Were they written during the session, the same day, or years afterwards?

Many of the books I have read do not tell you, which is itself an answer. Of those that do, several of the cases I have seen quoted most often appear, on their own account, to rest on write-ups made long after the event, by someone recalling a session rather than consulting one.

That does not make them worthless. It makes the *specific wording* of the crucial sentence unreliable, and in a case that turns on exactly what somebody said, the specific wording is the entire evidence.

## Three: are the client's words distinguished from the writer's?

Read a page and ask a single question: could you list which of the concrete details came out of the client's mouth? If the account is properly made, you can — there will be quotation marks, or a consistent attribution, or a change of layout doing the work.

If the answer is yes, the account is worth something even where it is unconvincing. If the whole thing flows as a single narrative voice, you cannot separate the testimony from the writing, and the testimony was the only part with any evidential standing.

Watch particularly for nouns, for the reason given in the chapter on questions: a practitioner who supplies them has put them in the session, and an account that does not print the questions has hidden it.

## Four: what does it say about the checks?

Find a place where something was checked. Now ask what happened when it was not found.

An account that reports only successful checks did not run any failed ones, or ran them and left them out. Both are the same problem. If you check a hundred details, a great many come back empty, and a book containing no empty results is not a book in which a hundred things were checked.

This is the cleanest test in the list, because it asks for something that should be there rather than looking for something that should not. A writer who genuinely checked will have a sentence somewhere saying that a search found nothing, and the presence of that sentence tells you more about the book than any number of matches.

Then ask what counted as a match. Is there a record of the description made before anyone went looking, and how many candidates would have satisfied it? If neither question is addressed, a reported match tells you nothing, however striking it reads.

## Five: what is promised?

Read the back cover, the introduction and any page describing what the reader will gain.

There is a spectrum here. At one end: *this book describes what happens in these sessions.* At the other: *this book will change how you understand your life.* In between there are softened forms, which are the ones to watch, because they are promises with the grammar removed. *Many readers find.* *It is not unusual for.* *This work often.*

Those constructions are doing the work of a guarantee while remaining deniable. They are also, almost always, unaccompanied by any statement of how often the thing does not happen — which is the number a reader would actually need.

A serious account tells you the failure rate. Ask any book what proportion of sessions produce nothing; in my reading, few have an answer.

## Six: who is not in the room?

Look for claims about people who were not present: the dead, the absent, the client's relatives, historical figures.

Anything a session produces about an absent person was produced by the client, and no account can responsibly present it as coming from anywhere else. When you read that a session established what somebody's mother felt, or that a departed person passed on a message, the honest report would be that the client, in that chair, said those words.

This is not a technicality. It is where I believe the greatest harm in this field is done, because it is where the material is least checkable and most load-bearing for the person receiving it.

## What the six questions do not measure

Before I turn them on this book, a caution, because a test used carelessly is worse than no test.

None of these six measures whether an account is true. They measure whether it is *assessable* — whether the writer has given you enough to form a view. Those are different properties and confusing them will make you unfair.

A book can fail every question and contain entirely accurate sessions. A practitioner with no interest in record-keeping, who writes up from memory in flowing prose and never checked anything, may nonetheless be reporting exactly what happened in their room. The failure is that you cannot tell. You are being asked to take it on trust, and the questions measure how much trust is being asked for, not whether it is deserved.

Equally, a book can pass all six and be wrong throughout. Scrupulous method is not a guarantee of anything; it is a guarantee that the errors will be visible.

The other thing the questions do not measure is whether a book is worth reading. Some of the accounts in this field that fail hardest on question three are the ones I have learned most from, because the writer was a superb observer of people even when they were a careless custodian of records. I would not want a reader to finish this chapter and start discarding books on a checklist.

Use the six to calibrate, not to convict. When a book scores badly, do not stop reading — read it as testimony about the writer rather than about the world, which is a legitimate and often rewarding way to read.

## Applying all six to this book

It would be cheap to hand you a test and exempt myself, so here is this book scored on its own six, including where it comes off worst.

**One:** I think it holds. The whole book is built on it and there are six words I do not use in order to keep it.

**Two:** It is stated at the very front, in the note about what these accounts are. That note is the most important page in the book, and I would rather you re-read it than take my word for anything.

**Three:** It holds, and at some cost. My questions are printed alongside the answers, which makes the session chapters clumsier than they would otherwise be.

**Four:** This is the book's strongest suit. Six of the eight session chapters have "nothing" as their check, in that word; one reports a search that came back empty; and the one match in the book is dismantled by the chapter that reports it.

**Five:** I think it holds, but this is where I would be most suspicious if I were you. A book that spends two hundred pages saying it promises nothing is still a book being sold to you, and the abstinence is itself a sales position. I have noticed that about my own manuscript and I have not found a way out of it.

**Six:** The weakest. I have quoted clients' accounts of what changed for them afterwards, and those people are not in the room while you read. They are composed, as the note at the front says, and they are still being characterised by me. The chapter that follows rests on those accounts, and this is the right moment to say that you are receiving them at second hand, from someone with an interest in how they sound.

Six questions, about ninety seconds of work, and they will serve you on everything else you read about this — including the next thing I write.

## Where the field's own literature actually stands

Since I have declined to name anything, let me at least characterise the landscape honestly, because "read it all critically" is not useful advice on its own.

There is a body of work in this area that is carefully reported. It exists, in my reading it is a minority, and it tends to share certain features: the practitioner's questions are printed, the failures are included, the checks that came back empty are stated as having come back empty, and the claims made are about what people said rather than about what happened. You can identify it by question four, reliably, in under a minute.

There is a much larger body that is sincerely written and methodologically careless. The record is a retelling, the client's words and the writer's have merged, and the checkable claims were handled in an afternoon by somebody with no training in handling them. In my reading this is not a fringe — it is most of what I have come across — and the people writing it are not charlatans. They are practitioners who are excellent at the central thing and have never been told that the peripheral thing has standards.

And there is a third category, whose size I cannot estimate, which makes claims it knows it cannot support. I have no interest in identifying members of it and no confidence that I could tell it apart from the second category from the outside, which is rather the problem.

What is missing from all three is the boring work: counts, denominators, follow-up collected by somebody with no stake. Outside the field, a small number of academic psychologists have run regressions under laboratory conditions and counted what came out; as far as I know, nobody without a stake has followed up what happens to clients afterwards. The absence is not a conspiracy. It is that the people with access to clients are practitioners, and practitioners have clients rather than subjects — a position I described from the inside in the chapter on the sisters, and will again in the next one, both times as an explanation for something I did not do.
