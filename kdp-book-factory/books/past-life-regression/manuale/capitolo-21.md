# Eight Sessions, One Threshold: What Repeats and What Never Does

Eight afternoons, eight people, eight sets of material with nothing linking them except the chair and the questions. Now the part that comes after: what the eight have in common, and what none of them contain.

I want to be exact about what kind of statement this chapter makes. A pattern across sessions is a fact about sessions. It is not evidence about lives. If everybody who comes to my room describes a threshold, the thing I have learned is something about what people describe in that room — and there are several explanations for that, of which "they are all remembering thresholds" is one and not obviously the best.

## What repeats

**Thresholds.** Doors, gates, entrances, the edge of something. Maureen had a door at the bottom of the stairs she would not go through. Ellen had a door that led "through." Callum's most insistent detail was a sound like a gate. Ray, who produced nothing at all, produced a colour that had "gone a bit lighter at the edge."

Across my practice generally, thresholds appear in something over half of sessions that produce any spatial content. They are usually mentioned rather than crossed. In a great many sessions the client stands at one and does not go through, and — this is the part I find interesting — very few of them are troubled by this. Not going through is presented as unremarkable.

**Small domestic objects.** A pan. A rocking floor stone. A cloth over the knees. A rope. Sessions are full of the sort of thing you would not bother mentioning if you were making up a life: objects with no narrative function, described with an odd practical familiarity.

**Weather and temperature.** Almost universal, and almost always the first thing to arrive. Cold on one side, heat that produces a physical flush, a draught from behind. If a session produces anything at all, it produces temperature before it produces anything else.

**A person who is not described.** Ellen's "she." Callum's "him." Jo's figure ahead she was following. Bernadette's "them," going on. Clients use definite pronouns for unintroduced people and are visibly puzzled when asked to identify them.

**Bodily discomfort.** Backs, hips, feet, hands. An enormous proportion of session content is somebody being uncomfortable.

**Waiting.** Not action. Waiting, and the small habits people develop to get through it.

## What almost never arrives

Here is the list that matters, and it is short.

**Dates.** In fourteen years I can count on my hands the sessions in which a client volunteered a year, unasked. Asked, they will usually produce one, which is precisely why I stopped asking.

**Surnames.** First names, occasionally — Ellen's *Mirushka*, and perhaps a dozen others across my whole practice. Surnames essentially never.

**Place names.** Almost never. Clients will describe a place in detail and be entirely unable to name it, and unbothered by that.

**Anything institutional.** No parishes, no regiments, no ships' names, no trades with formal titles. Callum described mud and straps for forty minutes and produced nothing that would identify an army.

**Events.** Nothing that a history would record. Nobody in my practice has ever described being present at something that happened.

**Numbers of any kind**, apart from Maureen's stairs, which she counted rather than recalled.

## The asymmetry

Put the two lists side by side and you have the single most interesting thing in this book.

Sessions produce, in abundance, exactly the class of material that cannot be checked: sensations, textures, unnamed people, domestic objects, emotional states, the experience of waiting. And they produce, almost never, the class of material that could be: names, dates, places, institutions, events.

That asymmetry is extremely sharp and it is not what you would predict if you had only read the literature. It is, I think, the finding a serious study of this material would start from.

It is also something a practitioner can accidentally erase. Ask "what century is it?" often enough and your sessions will start producing centuries, and your records will show an entirely different asymmetry, and you will believe your records.

## Three readings, none of which I am picking

I have three explanations and I am not going to choose between them, because I cannot, and because a practitioner who announces a conclusion here is announcing a preference.

**The first is about memory.** Whatever this material is drawn from — forgotten sources, imagination, something else — the retrieval is sensory rather than semantic. It produces texture and not facts because texture is what it has. On this reading the asymmetry is a fact about how minds generate material under these conditions, and it would be predicted by anyone who studies how people confabulate, which they do richly and specifically and without names.

**The second is about the nature of the source, taken at face value.** If this material came from lived experience, the asymmetry is what you would expect. Most people, for most of history, did not know what year it was. They did not know the name of the country they lived in. They knew the stone that rocked and which pan was the good one. On this reading the absence of dates is not a weakness in the material, it is the most realistic thing about it, and a session full of dates would be the suspicious one.

**The third is about the room.** I do not ask for dates. I do not supply nouns. I ask three questions in rotation and they are all about awareness rather than information. It is entirely possible that the asymmetry I have described is an artefact of my own method — that I have built a procedure which produces texture and excludes facts, and then been struck by finding texture and no facts.

The third reading is the one I like least and take most seriously. It is testable, at least in principle: another practitioner with a different method, counting the same categories, would get a different distribution or the same one. As far as I know nobody has done that count, which is a sentence that recurs in this book more often than I would like.

## What the patterns are not

They are not evidence of a shared source.

I want to be blunt about this, because it is the step almost every account in this field takes and it is taken silently. The argument, unstated, runs: many unconnected people describe thresholds, therefore something outside those people is supplying thresholds.

That does not follow, for a simple reason. Human beings are alike. We have the same bodies, the same architecture of attention, the same handful of deep image-schemas that structure how we represent space — inside and outside, up and down, through and not-through. A threshold is not an exotic detail. It is one of the most basic ways a mind can organise a space, and a room full of unconnected people asked to produce spatial material will produce thresholds for the same reason a room full of unconnected people asked to name a colour will produce red.

The same applies to weather, to bodily discomfort, and to waiting. These are not obscure. They are the substrate of ordinary experience, and their recurrence tells you about the substrate.

Where a pattern would mean something is if it were arbitrary and specific: if eight unconnected clients produced the same unusual name, the same peculiar object, the same distinctive detail that had no reason to be common. That has not happened in my practice. The closest I have is that two clients, years apart, described a particular kind of hinge — and when I looked at my notes properly, the descriptions were not the same, and I had made them the same in my memory.

## What does not repeat at all

It is worth saying what the eight sessions do *not* share, because the differences are larger than the similarities and nobody ever lists them.

They are not the same length. Ray's produced nothing in sixty-five minutes; Ellen's thickened for fifty and had to be stopped for time.

They are not in the same posture. Bernadette sat still for an hour and ten minutes. Jo walked for most of hers. Callum stood in mud. Maureen climbed.

They do not share an emotional register. Douglas was terrified. Bernadette was bored and amused by being bored. Ruth was mildly interested throughout and never moved from that. Martin was in trouble from the first four minutes.

They do not share a period, a place, a climate or a social setting — insofar as any of them can be said to have those things, which mostly they cannot.

And, most importantly, they do not connect to one another in any way at all. No recurring figure, no shared location, no detail from one turning up in another. Nothing that would suggest a common world.

If these eight afternoons were drawing on one source, that source is producing radically different material for each person and no cross-references between them. That is a real constraint, and any explanation has to account for it — including the ordinary ones, which handle it easily, and the extraordinary ones, which handle it less easily than is usually acknowledged.

## What I would want counted

If anyone with the right training ever does this properly, here is the count I would want.

Not a study of whether sessions are accurate. That question is unanswerable with the records that exist.

A study of the distribution: across several hundred sessions by practitioners with different methods, what proportion contain a volunteered date, a surname, a place name, an institution, an event? And how does that proportion move with the questions asked?

That is an answerable question. It requires no position on what any of this is. And it would tell us, for the first time, whether the asymmetry I have described here is a property of the material or a property of me.
