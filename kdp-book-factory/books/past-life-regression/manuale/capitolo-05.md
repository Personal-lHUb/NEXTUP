# Six Words I Refuse to Use, and What I Say Instead

The discipline this book is trying to keep is not maintained by disclaimers. A disclaimer at the front of a book is a receipt: it proves the author knew, and it changes nothing on page ninety. The work is done sentence by sentence, in ordinary word choice, and most of it comes down to six words.

None of the six is a lie. That is what makes them difficult. Each one is a perfectly good English word doing a perfectly ordinary job somewhere else, and each one, used here, quietly asserts something nobody in the room is in a position to assert.

I have struck all six from my practice and from these pages, with one exception I owe you an account of, because it is on the cover. *What They Remembered* is the clients' word, not mine. It is the word nearly everyone uses about their own session afterwards, and this is a book about what they say, so the title quotes them. Inside the covers I use it in my own voice only for ordinary memories of this life, which are what it was made for. Here are the six, with what I say instead.

## Remembered

The commonest, and the one that does the most damage.

In ordinary English, "remembered" is a success verb. In that sense you cannot remember something that did not happen; if it did not happen, you did not remember it, you imagined it or confabulated it or produced it. Psychologists would add that people can feel they remember things that never happened, which is exactly why the word should not be doing the deciding. Built into the word is the claim that there was an event and that this is retrieval. Write *she remembered a stone quay* and you have settled the entire question in a subordinate clause, before the reader has been shown anything at all.

Instead I write: *she described*. Or *she said*. Or, when I want to keep the strangeness of how the material arrives, *she reported being aware of*. Those are all flat, and flat is the point. They report the room.

There is a cost, and it is worth naming. "Described" is a colder word than "remembered," and a book written in it reads cooler than the alternative. I have decided I would rather be cool than wrong. But I understand exactly why the other choice gets made, and I do not think the people who make it are frauds.

## Recalled

The same problem in a smarter suit. "Recalled" sounds more careful than "remembered" — more clinical, more like a report — and it smuggles the identical claim.

Worse, it tends to appear in the passive: *details were recalled*. The passive removes the person entirely, which is extraordinary when you think about it, because the person is the only thing in the room we have any evidence about. A session has exactly one witness to what is happening inside it, and a sentence in the passive voice deletes her.

Instead: *she said*, with the subject restored. Always the subject. In fourteen years I have not found a single sentence about a session that is improved by taking the client out of it.

## Returned to

*She returned to a life in the north.* The word does two things at once: it asserts the life, and it asserts that she had been there before and is going back. It is the vocabulary of travel, and travel presupposes a destination that exists independently of the traveller.

Instead I use nothing at all. That is the honest replacement: there is no verb of motion that will do, because no motion has been established. The client did not go anywhere. She was in a reclining chair in a room with a radiator that ticks. What happened is that she began to describe things, and *she began to describe* is the accurate sentence.

Clients themselves use the travel language constantly, and I do not correct them — it is their experience and they may describe it however they like. When I quote them saying "I went back," I quote them, and the quotation marks do the work.

## Proved

Nothing in this field proves anything, and the word appears in it constantly.

Its favourite habitat is the near miss: *which seemed to prove*, *appearing to prove*, *proof, if proof were needed*. Those constructions are worse than the bare assertion, because the hedge makes the writer feel careful while the reader takes away the noun. The reader remembers "proof." Nobody has ever finished a paragraph and remembered "seemed to."

Instead I say what actually happened: *the detail matched the record*, or *the detail could not be checked*, or *the search found nothing*. Those sentences are about a search, which is a thing I did, on a date, and can describe. They are also falsifiable, which is the property "proof" is pretending to have.

## Confirmed

The one that most often turns up attached to a real, checkable fact, which is what makes it the most dangerous of the six.

*Records confirmed that a family of that name lived in the parish.* Perhaps they did. There may well have been such a family, and you may well have found them. What the sentence does is attach that finding to the session as though the session had predicted it — and in almost every case, the session produced a common surname, the parish contained a great many people, and the match means very little. The word "confirmed" carries all of that weight silently.

Instead I write out the whole thing, at length, in the flattest way I can manage: *she gave the name as X. I searched Y. There were N households of that name in the area in the relevant period. That is roughly what you would expect from any common name, and I do not think it tells us anything.* It takes three sentences instead of one. There is a chapter later in this book that does very little except this, slowly, because the arithmetic of matching is where honest people most often go wrong without noticing.

## Verified

The last one, and the one I hear most often from other practitioners rather than from writers.

"Verified" means checked and found true, and in my experience, in this work it is usually used to mean *checked and found not to be contradicted*, which is a completely different thing. A session detail that the record does not contradict has not been verified. It has been found unfalsifiable, usually because the record for that time and place is too thin to falsify anything at all.

Instead: *I looked, and the record has nothing to say about it either way.* That is what happened. It is not an exciting sentence. It is, in my experience, the true sentence about four cases in five.

## The words that are not on the list

The list stops at six, and what it leaves off matters as much as what it contains.

It does not contain "soul." It does not contain "past life," "between lives," "karma," or any of the field's other furniture. I am asked about this fairly often, usually by people who assume that a practitioner with my views must be working her way round to striking those too.

I am not, and the reason is the distinction this book keeps returning to. "Soul" is a word for something a client believes in and is describing her experience with. When a woman tells me her soul chose this life, she is not making a checkable claim about the world; she is telling me how the afternoon felt from inside, in the vocabulary she has. Quoting her is honest reporting. Using the word in my own voice would be different, and I generally do not — not because it is forbidden, but because it is not mine.

The six words are on the list for a narrower reason: each one asserts a matter of fact that could in principle be wrong and that nobody present can establish. "Remembered" claims an event occurred. "Confirmed" claims a search succeeded. Those are the sort of thing a reader could, in another context, go and check — and being uncheckable here is exactly what makes them dishonest here.

"Soul" is not that kind of word. Nobody reads "she felt her soul was old" and goes looking for a record. The reader knows perfectly well what kind of sentence they have been handed, and no one has been deceived. A rule that struck it would not be more rigorous, only colder, and it would tell the people who come to me that their vocabulary is unwelcome in a book that is supposed to be about them.

There was very nearly a seventh word. I spent a long time over "revealed" — *the session revealed* — which does everything "confirmed" does and adds a suggestion of concealment, as though something had been waiting to be found. In the end I decided it was a variant of the other two rather than a case of its own. If you want to make it seven, I will not argue.

## What the list is actually for

Here is a short passage of my own, from my notebook, written before I had any of this straight.

*Client remembered a childhood near water, recalled a brother, returned to the moment of the accident. Details of the boat seem to confirm the period.*

Twenty-five words, and there is not one thing in it I could defend. Every verb is doing work the evidence cannot pay for. Struck of those words, the same note reads:

*Client described a childhood near water and a brother. Described an accident. Gave details of a boat; I have not checked them and would not know where to start.*

Longer, duller, and true. The second version also happens to be more useful to me a year later, because it tells me what I actually have.

That is the test I would offer. Take any paragraph in any book on this shelf, strike the six words, and read what is left. If the passage still says something — if there is a stone quay and cold hands and a brother who is late — then the writer had material and chose to overstate it, which is a fixable fault. If the passage collapses into nothing, then the six words were the content, and there was never anything underneath them.

I apply this to my own drafts. It catches something in roughly one page out of five, and it has caught things in this chapter.
