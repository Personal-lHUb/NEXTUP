# The Session I Stopped

I have ended four sessions early in fourteen years. This is one of them, and it is the one I got closest to handling badly.

Martin was fifty-two. He worked in logistics, he had a dry sense of humour, and he came about what he called "a low patch" that had lasted, on inspection, about two years. He had not seen anybody about it. He had not, he said, wanted to "make a thing of it."

That sentence should have slowed me down more than it did.

## What I missed in the intake

I did the four questions and he answered all four, briefly and pleasantly, and briefly and pleasantly is not the same as fully.

*What brought you here?* The low patch, plus a general interest, plus his sister had done one.

*What are you expecting?* "No idea. Whatever turns up."

*Have you done anything like this before?* No.

*Is there anything you would rather not find?* A pause — a short one, perhaps two seconds — and then: "Not that I can think of."

Two seconds. I noted the pause at the time, in the notebook, because I note pauses. What I did not do was go back to it. There is a chapter earlier in this book in which I describe the fourth question as the one that gives people a sanctioned way to gesture at something without naming it, and I wrote that with Martin in mind: he gestured, I recorded the gesture, and I carried on.

I have thought a great deal about why. The best answer I have is that he was likeable and easy and the conversation was going well, and going back to a two-second pause would have made it less easy. That is not a good enough reason.

## The first fifteen minutes

He settled in eleven and began producing immediately, which as I have said usually means a shallow session and in this case did not.

The material was not unusual in content. A room, low ceiling, not much light. A sense of being somewhere he should not be. Cold.

What was unusual was the rate. He was talking almost continuously, which people in that state rarely do, and the sentences were getting shorter rather than longer.

> — There's not a lot of room in here.
>
> — Tell me about that.
>
> — It's low. You have to keep your head down. *(no pause at all)* It's low and it's low all the way along and you can't stand up in it.
>
> — What are you aware of?
>
> — I can't stand up.

And then, about four minutes later, without any question from me:

> — I can't stand up. I can't stand up. I need to stand up.

## The signals

Three things happened at once and I want to name them precisely, because this is the part that is useful to other practitioners and the part that gets left out of books.

His breathing went wrong. Not fast — shallow, and out of rhythm, with a catch at the top of the inhale. That is different from the breathing of someone who is frightened in the material, which is fast and regular and settles when you speak.

His hands did not close. This is the one I would emphasise. In most of the distress I have seen, hands close; people grip the arms of the chair or each other. Martin's hands stayed exactly where they were, palms up, completely still, while the rest of him was in trouble. The disconnection between the two was the thing that made me sit forward.

And he did not respond to my voice. I said his name, and then I said it again, and there was no change at all in what he was saying or how fast he was saying it. In my experience a client in an ordinary difficult moment orients to the practitioner almost at once, and a great deal of the safety of this work depends on it. He did not.

Eleven seconds, from the first signal to my decision. It felt considerably longer.

## How you stop

Not with a count. The count is a return from a settled state and it takes several minutes, and several minutes was not what was needed.

What I did was change the entire register of my voice — from the slow, low session voice to ordinary conversational speech, at normal volume, and I said something that had nothing to do with anything:

*Martin, we're in the room, it's about half past two, there's a lorry going past outside.*

Then I said it again, differently. Then I asked him to open his eyes, plainly, as a request rather than a suggestion. Then — and this is the part I would not have predicted about myself — I put my hand flat on the arm of the chair near his, not touching him, because I wanted him to have something in the ordinary world to find.

He opened his eyes about forty seconds in. He was disoriented for perhaps a minute and then he was entirely present, and the first thing he said was "sorry."

## Afterwards

We sat for an hour and a half. I made tea twice.

He told me, in that hour and a half, that the two-year low patch was not a low patch. He told me what it actually was, which is his and is not going in a book. He told me that he had not said any of it to anybody, including his wife, and that he had booked the session because it seemed like a way of dealing with it that did not involve telling anybody.

I want to be careful how I put the next part. I did not do therapy in that hour and a half. I am not qualified to and I did not try. What I did was sit there, and say very little, and at the end of it ask him — directly, twice — to go and see his GP that week.

He said he would. I asked whether I could send him a couple of numbers as well, and he said yes, and I sent them that evening: his GP's, a helpline, and the details of somebody I know who is properly trained and whom I have sent people to before.

If you recognise yourself in Martin, you do not need a session to begin: your doctor is the right first call. If it is worse than that, and you are thinking about harming yourself, the contacts in the chapter on the weeks after a session are there for today, not for later.

## What he did with it

He did not go to his GP that week.

He went about five weeks later, and he told me so himself, in a message that said "you were right, I was annoyed about it for a month, I've been." He did not take up the referral to the person I know. He did go, eventually, to somebody else, found through his GP.

I have not seen him since and would not offer him another session. He has asked, once, and I said no, and gave the reason, which is that what he needs is being done by somebody qualified and I would be an interference.

That is the outcome. It is not a happy ending and it is not a sad one; it is a man who got to the right place five weeks later than he should have, via a route that included an afternoon that went wrong in my room.

## The three signals, written out

If you do this work, or intend to, this is the part of the chapter worth keeping.

**Breathing that is out of rhythm rather than fast.** Fast breathing, in my experience, is usually somebody having a big feeling, and it settles when you speak; if it does not settle, treat it as you would the other two. The catch at the top of the inhale is different and I have never seen it in an ordinary difficult session.

**Hands that stay still while everything else does not.** Distress that recruits the body is distress the person is in. Distress that leaves the hands lying palm-up while the voice goes is something else, and I do not know what it is, but in my own small number of cases it is the one that mattered most.

**No orientation to your voice.** Say their name. If nothing changes, stop. Not soon — now.

Any one of the three is enough. I would not wait for two, and the reason I would not is that I waited about eleven seconds for two and it was eleven seconds too many.

These are my signals, drawn from a handful of afternoons, not a clinical protocol. Anyone doing this work needs proper training in recognising and handling acute distress, and a plan for it that does not depend on them.

## The check, and why there is one

It would be easy to leave this section out, on the grounds that the chapter is about something else. I am including it because every session chapter in this book has one, and a book that suspends its own rule when the rule is inconvenient does not have a rule.

Martin's session contained a low room, a ceiling he could not stand up under, and cold. No name, no place, no date, no object, no other person. There is nothing here that could be looked up and nothing I would want to look up.

I also want to say plainly what this session is not. It is not evidence of anything about a past life. It is evidence of a risk this work carries and should say out loud: a session can take down the guard of someone carrying something heavy, and a person in a long low patch may need a GP before they need a hypnotist. What happened to Martin in that chair was, as far as I can tell, what happens to a person carrying something very heavy who is invited to stop managing it for an hour. The low room may have meant something and it may have meant nothing; he has never offered an interpretation and I have never asked for one.

The temptation in this genre is to read a session like his as the material breaking through — the past life so distressing that it overwhelmed the process. That reading is available, it is dramatic, and it would be an invention. The simpler account is that a man in trouble relaxed his guard and the trouble arrived, and the trouble was from this life, and he told me what it was over two cups of tea afterwards.

## What this chapter is for

Duty of care in this field is too often a paragraph on a website. It is not a paragraph. It is a decision made in about ten seconds by a person who is not a clinician and who has, at that moment, no one to consult.

There is a chapter near the end of this book about how to tell a serious practitioner from a performer, and one of the questions it suggests asking is what they would do if a session went wrong. The answer you want is not reassurance. The answer you want is specific: signals, an actual method for stopping, and what happens in the hour afterwards.

If a practitioner tells you it has never happened to them, I would guess they have either done very few sessions or not been watching.
