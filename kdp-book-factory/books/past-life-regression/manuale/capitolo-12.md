# What I Will Not Do in That Room

A practice is defined by its refusals. Anyone can list the things they offer; the list is marketing. What tells you who you are dealing with is the list of things they decline, and whether the declining costs them anything.

Mine costs me a certain amount of money every year and one client in particular that I still think about.

## No diagnosis

People arrive with symptoms. A man with pain in his shoulder that four doctors have not explained. A woman with a compulsion she is ashamed of. A parent whose child is not eating.

The session will produce material. It always does, and the material will attach itself to the symptom, because that is what the mind does with a symptom and an afternoon of free association: it joins them. He will describe a fall, or a weight, or a blow. She will describe a room she could not leave.

And then the two of us will be sitting in a room with a story that explains the shoulder, and the story will be satisfying, and it will be the only explanation on offer. That is a dangerous object to hand somebody who has been in pain for two years.

So I say, before and after: *this is not a finding about your shoulder. It is something you described. Your shoulder is a matter for your doctor and I would go back to them.*

I have had that received badly exactly twice. Both times the client's view was that I was hiding behind a formula, and I understand why it looks like that. It is a formula. It is a formula because the alternative is improvising medical opinion on the strength of a metaphor, and there is no version of that which ends well.

What I do instead, if the material seems to be pointing somewhere, is tell them what they said and let them take it to somebody qualified. Twice that has led to a useful conversation with a clinician. That is the whole of my ambition in this area.

## No prophecy

The most requested thing I decline, and the requests are not frivolous.

People want to know whether the relationship will last, whether the move is right, whether the treatment will work, whether they should have another child. They ask directly, and they ask obliquely, and they ask in the intake conversation dressed as a general enquiry about what the session covers.

The answer is always the same: nothing in this can tell you that, and if I let the afternoon produce something that sounded like an answer, you would act on it.

The last part is the point. Clients are not fools and would not, in ordinary circumstances, restructure their lives around an image that came to them in a chair. But the material from these sessions carries a peculiar authority, precisely because it does not feel authored. It arrives from somewhere that is not quite you, and that gives it weight that a thought does not have. I have watched people hand that weight to decisions it cannot bear.

There is a version of the request I do accept, which is a person wanting to sit with a decision rather than resolve it. The difference is audible in the intake and I have been wrong about it perhaps a dozen times.

## No third parties

I will not put an absent person in the chair by proxy.

*Can you find out whether my mother forgave me? Can you ask about my son?* The answer is no, and it is the refusal people find hardest to accept, because it looks like an arbitrary rule standing between them and something they need.

The reason is straightforward. Whatever the session produces about an absent person will be produced by the client, out of the client, and it will be indistinguishable from an answer. If a woman describes her dead mother saying something kind, she will carry that for the rest of her life as something her mother said. She did not. The most I could honestly report is that the woman, in that chair, produced those words — and in that moment, no disclaimer I offer will survive contact with how much she wants it to be true.

The absent person has also not consented to anything, which is a smaller point but not nothing.

What I do offer is the ordinary version: she can describe her mother, her feelings about her mother, a conversation she wishes had happened. All of that is hers and all of it is legitimate and it is sometimes the most useful afternoon I am involved in. What I will not do is let it be reported as contact.

## No guarantees

There is a sentence I say in the intake and repeat on the invoice, and I have never had anyone object to it: *I can't tell you what will happen in the session, and I can't tell you that anything will change afterwards.*

Both halves matter. About one session in eight produces nothing at all, and a client who has not been told that in advance will conclude, wrongly, that they have failed. And of the sessions that do produce something, what follows varies enormously and unpredictably — some people describe a substantial shift, some describe nothing, and I cannot tell in advance which will be which.

There is a whole chapter later in this book about what does seem to change for people, and the claim it makes is deliberately narrow: that people report changes, that the reports are worth taking seriously, and that this is a statement about what people say afterwards rather than a statement about what I deliver.

Anything stronger would be a promise, and promises in this field are made constantly, usually in the softened form: *many clients find*, *it is not unusual for*, *this work often*. Those constructions are promises wearing a hat.

## What I say when somebody pushes

They do push, and occasionally it is heartbreaking.

The conversation I think about most involved a man whose daughter had died eighteen months earlier. He did not want a regression. He wanted me to ask her something. He had a specific question and he had rehearsed it, and when I said no he asked me three more times, in three different ways, over about fifteen minutes, and then he cried, and then he apologised for crying.

I said no all four times. What I said, in the end, was: *if I do this, you will get an answer, and it will come from you, and you will spend years not knowing whether it was hers. I don't think I'd be doing you a kindness.*

He did not agree with me. He was not unpleasant about it; he simply thought I was wrong, and he said that he would rather have something he could doubt than nothing at all, and that is not an unreasonable position. I have turned it over many times since and I am still not certain I was right — only that the alternative was worse, and that a practitioner who bends this particular rule for the most sympathetic case available will not have a rule for very long.

He left and I did not charge him. He sent a card at Christmas for two years, which I did not expect and did not deserve.

## The refusals I decided against

There are three things I considered adding and did not, and the reasoning matters as much as the list itself.

I considered refusing to work with anyone who had read extensively in the field. The argument is reasonable: a client who knows what a session is supposed to look like is more likely to produce one, and the more they have read the more specific their expectations. But the rule would have excluded a large share of the people who most want this, on the grounds that being interested in something disqualifies you from experiencing it. That is not a defensible position, and the problem it addresses is better handled by naming the expectation out loud in the intake.

I considered refusing to tell clients anything about what other clients have produced. There is a version of this that is obviously right — I do not describe a session to a client beforehand, ever, because it would furnish the room. But a blanket rule would mean refusing to answer perfectly ordinary questions about what the work is like, and a person is entitled to know what they are buying. So the line falls between general and specific: I will say that people often describe sensory details before scenes, and I will not say that three clients this year described a staircase.

And I considered refusing to work with anyone twice, on the grounds that a second session with me is a session with somebody who now has expectations about them. I decided against it because some people genuinely need a second afternoon, and because the risk is manageable if I re-read nothing beforehand — which I do not, deliberately, going into a repeat session with only my memory of the person and none of my notes.

Each of those three would have made me look more rigorous. None of them would have made a single client safer, and two would have made the work worse. That is the test I try to apply: does the refusal protect somebody, or does it protect my reputation for carefulness?

## Why the list is short

Four refusals. Not a code of ethics, not a charter, just four things I will not do.

I have seen much longer lists, and I am suspicious of them, because a long list tends to be about the practitioner rather than the client. These four exist because each one names a specific way this work can hurt somebody: by substituting for medicine, by impersonating foresight, by manufacturing contact, by selling an outcome.

They are also the four questions I would ask a practitioner before booking, phrased as questions rather than as statements. A chapter near the end of this book puts them in that form, along with the answers that should make you comfortable and the ones that should make you put the phone down.
