# Buried Objects, Buried Arguments

Three times, a client has told me where something is buried.

The claim has a particular hold on this field, and on the people who come to me, and I understand exactly why. It is the one thing that would settle it. Everything else — a name in a register, a room described accurately — can be argued about, attributed to a forgotten source, explained by the base rate. But a box under a specific floor, found where nobody could have known to look, would be something else entirely.

The wish behind that is not silly. It is the wish for the question to be answerable, and I have it too.

## The three times

The first was a woman who told me, in the last ten minutes of a session, that there was "a tin under the third step." She was quite specific about the step and entirely unable to say where the staircase was.

The second was a man who described burying something in a wall — not under it, in it, in a cavity — and said it was "at the corner where the two roads are." He gave no town, no country, no period.

The third is the only one that had an address attached, and it is the one worth going through properly.

## The one with an address

A woman I will call Sandra described, over about half an hour, a garden. She described it in considerable detail: a wall, a fruit tree, a stone trough. And she said that she had put something at the base of the wall "where the tree is," and she named a street.

Not a full address. A street name, in a city, which she volunteered without being asked, and which is the only volunteered place name I have had in fourteen years.

I am not printing it. This is the exception to my own rule that I promised earlier. A street name is not an entry in an archive; it is people's homes, and printing it would send readers to stand outside a stranger's wall, or worse. So on this one detail you will have to take me on trust, which is exactly the position this book tries never to put you in, and I would rather say so than pretend otherwise.

She wanted me to go and look. She was quite insistent about it and, when I hesitated, mildly hurt — she had produced the one thing the field says it wants and the practitioner did not seem interested.

So let me explain what "going and looking" actually involves, because the phrase does a great deal of work in accounts of this kind and almost none of it is examined.

## What it takes to dig somewhere

**Somebody owns it.** Land belongs to a person, a company, a council, a church or a trust. Digging on it without permission is trespass, and depending on where you are and what you disturb it can be a great deal more than trespass.

**Getting permission means explaining why.** This is the part people do not think through. You must go to a stranger — a homeowner, a landlord, a housing association — and say that a woman in a reclining chair in another city has told you something is buried in their garden, and ask them to let you excavate it. I have made this sort of approach once, in a different context, and I would encourage anyone who thinks it sounds straightforward to imagine receiving the call.

**Then there is the law about what comes out of the ground.** Many countries have specific rules for finds, especially old ones: in some, anything archaeological must be reported and belongs to the state; in England and Wales, anything that counts as treasure must by law be reported to the coroner, and not doing so is an offence. And even where no such rule applies, what you lift out of a stranger's garden is generally theirs, not yours. An amateur digging a hole and lifting something out has, in the best case, destroyed the context that would have made the find meaningful, and in the worst case committed an offence.

**And it costs money.** Doing it properly — which means an archaeologist, a method, a record — is not a weekend activity.

Sandra's street exists. There are houses on it and some of them have gardens and at least one, from what I could see, has a wall. That is as far as I went, and I told her so, and she was disappointed.

## Why I no longer chase them

Not because of the practical obstacles, which are real but could be overcome by someone determined.

The reason is that I worked out what the result would be in each of the two possible outcomes, and neither is worth what it would cost.

**If nothing is found**, we learn nothing. The thing may have been removed a century ago; the wall may have been rebuilt; the tree may be a different tree; the garden may have been dug over ten times. An absence establishes nothing at all, for exactly the reasons set out in the previous chapter.

**If something is found**, we learn less than it seems. A garden wall that has been standing for a long time will have things at the base of it, because gardens accumulate. Broken pottery, a coin, a buried bottle, the buckle off a boot — these are what soil in an inhabited place contains. Finding an object would produce a spectacular anecdote and would not be evidence, because the prediction was not specific enough to be surprising.

To be worth anything, a prediction of this kind would have to be very tight indeed: a specified object, in a specified place, at a specified depth, described before the dig, in a location with no reason to contain such a thing. Sandra said "something." The first woman said "a tin." Neither is that.

## The stories that do not survive the question

You will read accounts of successful finds. I have read several, and I want to be careful here, because I am not calling anyone a liar and I have not investigated any of them.

What I will say is what I ask when I read one, and what happens to most of them when I ask it.

*Who found it, and was the client present?* If the client, or someone who knew the client's account, did the finding, the chain is broken at the first link — not because of dishonesty but because knowing where to look is exactly what was supposed to be tested.

*Was the description written down before the search?* If the only record of what was predicted was written after the object was in hand, there is no prediction. Human memory will helpfully adjust the description to fit, and it will do so invisibly.

*What was the object, and how unusual is it in that kind of ground?* A coin is not unusual. A bottle is not unusual. The specificity has to exceed the background rate.

*Who else knew?* A local story, a family legend, a line in a guidebook — any of these can reach a person and be forgotten.

Most of the published accounts I have read cannot answer the second question, and the second question is the one that matters. In the absence of a contemporaneous record of the prediction, there is nothing to assess.

## What I would accept

It seems only fair, having spent a chapter explaining why none of this convinces me, to say what would.

A prediction made and recorded before anybody goes anywhere. Written down, dated, witnessed by someone with no stake in it, and specific enough to be wrong: not "something," but an object of a stated kind, of a stated material, at a stated depth, in a location the client could not have visited or read about.

A search conducted by people who have not seen the prediction. This is the step I have never seen taken in any account I have read, and it is not difficult: the person digging should not know what they are looking for, and the person who knows should not be there.

A location with no local tradition attached. If there is a story in the village about something buried by the church, the whole exercise is worthless, and the check for that starts with an afternoon in a local archive.

And a client whose access to the site can be ruled out. Not just "she says she has never been" — a genuine effort to establish it, which usually means somebody other than the practitioner asking the question.

I want to be clear that I am not setting an impossible standard as a way of dismissing the whole category. Every element of that list is ordinary. It is how you would test any claim about the physical world, and it costs money, time and the goodwill of a landowner, none of them in impossible quantities.

The reason it has not been done — or has not been done in a form that survives inspection — is not that it is hard. I suspect it is that the people best placed to do it are the people least keen to find out, and I include myself in that. I have had one street name in fourteen years and I did not go.

## The honest version of the claim

Here is what I think is actually true in this area, and it is smaller and more interesting than the dramatic version.

People in that chair sometimes produce extremely specific spatial claims — a step, a corner, a wall — with a confidence they do not apply to anything else in the session. Sandra was vague about who she was and certain about the base of a wall. The first woman could not say where the staircase was and was completely sure about the third step.

That asymmetry is odd and I do not have an explanation for it. It might be that spatial memory and autobiographical memory are differently organised and that whatever produces this material has more access to the former. It might be that specificity about places is simply easier to generate than specificity about people, because a step is a step and a person needs a name.

Either way, the thing to notice is not "she knew where it was buried." It is "she was certain about a location and uncertain about everything else." That is a real observation about sessions, it costs nothing to make, and it does not require anyone to dig up a stranger's garden.

Sandra, incidentally, went herself. She stood on the street and looked at the wall and did not knock on the door, and she told me afterwards that she had felt "completely ridiculous, and also quite glad I went." I think that is the right outcome, and I did not arrange it, and it cost nobody anything.
