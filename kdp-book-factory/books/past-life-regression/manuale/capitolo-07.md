# Before Anyone Closes Their Eyes

The hour before the session decides more about what happens in it than the induction does. I did not believe that for the first few years. I thought of the conversation as admin — consent forms, medical questions, a chat to settle the nerves — and I got through it as quickly as courtesy allowed so that we could get to the real work.

The real work starts the moment they sit down. What is said in that hour sets the terms, names the expectations, and determines, more than anything I do afterwards, whether the session produces something that belongs to the client or something that belongs to me.

## The four questions

I ask everyone the same four, in the same order, and I write the answers down in front of them.

*What brought you here?* Open, and deliberately not "what do you want to get out of this?", which invites a specification. I am listening for the difference between a person with a problem and a person with a project. Both are welcome. They need different afternoons.

*What are you expecting to happen?* This is the one that changes the session, and I will come back to it.

*Have you done this before, or anything like it?* Meaning hypnosis of any kind, meditation with any depth to it, or — the answer people give most often and least expect to matter — long periods of being read to as a child. The people who settle fastest are frequently the ones who spent their childhood being talked into a story.

*Is there anything you would rather not find?* The bluntest of the four and the one people appreciate most. A surprising number have something specific in mind, and almost none of them would have raised it unprompted. A woman who is frightened of finding out something about her mother needs me to know that before she is in the chair, not after.

The whole thing takes forty minutes if it takes any time at all. I have never regretted the forty minutes.

## Why the second question changes everything

Expectation is, in my experience, the largest contaminant in this work, and the reason is not that people fake things. Almost nobody fakes anything; I will come back to that too. The reason is that a person in a suggestible state who has a clear idea of the sort of material that is wanted will very often produce it, without meaning to and with complete sincerity.

So I ask what they are expecting, and I ask it neutrally, and then I say it back to them out loud.

*So you're expecting something from the past — a place, a person, a time. Possibly something that explains the dream. You're half expecting it to be somewhere hot.*

That sounds like a small thing. It is the most useful ninety seconds of the afternoon. Once an expectation has been said out loud by both of us, in the room, before anything begins, it becomes a known object. If something hot and southern turns up an hour later, neither of us has to pretend to be surprised, and both of us can hold it more lightly. If something turns up that is nothing like the expectation — a cold room, a corridor, an animal — then that is genuinely interesting, and it is interesting precisely because we established the baseline in advance.

I do not tell clients their expectations will contaminate the session. That is both rude and unhelpful, and it sets up a kind of performance anxiety in which they start trying to produce unexpected material, which is its own contamination and harder to see. I simply name what they came in with, and leave it named.

## Who I turn away

Not many, and for two reasons only.

The first is medical. There are conditions for which an afternoon of intense, unstructured emotional material is a poor idea, and there are people currently under the care of someone else for whom my involvement would be an interference. If a person is in treatment, I ask them to tell the person treating them that they are coming, and to come back if that person has concerns. Perhaps one in thirty does not come back. That seems to me about right.

The second reason is the one that takes judgement. Some people arrive needing an answer to a question that this cannot answer, and needing it badly enough that they will take whatever the afternoon produces as that answer.

A man whose wife has died in the last few months, wanting to know she is all right. A woman in the middle of a custody dispute, wanting to know whether her instinct about her child is right. Somebody deciding whether to leave a marriage. In each case the session will produce material, because sessions almost always do, and the material will be vivid, and they will take it as instruction. I will not be there when they act on it.

I say no to those, and I say it in the room, at the time, with a reason. *What you're carrying is too heavy for what this is. If you come here today you will leave with something that feels like an answer, and I don't think it would be one, and I think you'd act on it.* Most people take it well. A few do not, and one of those conversations is the worst half hour I have had in this job.

## The terms, agreed in advance

Before the chair goes back, we agree four things, and I say all four out loud even when a client is visibly impatient to get on with it.

That I will not tell them what any of it means. The interpretation is theirs, afterwards, in their own time, and any meaning I supplied would be mine.

That I do not know whether it is real and will not pretend to. This is the one that occasionally produces a flicker of disappointment, and it is much better to have that flicker now than at the door on the way out.

That they can stop at any point, that stopping is not a failure, and that they will not be "stuck" — a fear a remarkable number of people carry in and almost none of them mention until asked.

And that nothing said in the room goes anywhere. They were told when they booked that I write about this work; if I want to write about their session, I will ask afterwards, showing them what I have written, and a no is a no with no explanation required.

## What is actually on the form

There is a form, and it is one side of paper, and I have rewritten it perhaps a dozen times.

It says what the session is: a conversation conducted while the client is deeply relaxed, lasting between one and two hours, recorded unless they ask otherwise. It says what it is not: not therapy, not treatment, not a diagnosis of anything, not advice. It says in one sentence that I am not a clinician and that nothing said in the room should replace anything said by one.

Then there is a line I added after about three years, which is the only part anybody ever comments on. It says: *the practitioner cannot tell you whether the content of the session refers to real events, and will not offer an opinion on this during the session.*

Clients read that line and look up. Several have asked, not unreasonably, why they are paying me if I will not tell them what it means. The answer is that they are paying me for the afternoon and for the care taken over it, in the same way you pay somebody to take a photograph without expecting them to tell you what the photograph signifies.

The form also says they can withdraw consent to being written about at any time, including years later, with no reason given. That clause has been used once. I did not enjoy it and I would not remove it.

## When somebody is not telling me the truth

It happens, rarely, and almost never in the way you would expect.

As far as I can tell, nobody comes in intending to fake a session; there is no advantage in it, nobody is watching, and the whole thing costs money. Once or twice a client with nothing arriving has started supplying something to fill the silence, which is a different thing, and you will meet it later in the book. What happens far more often is that people are not straight with me in the intake, and it is nearly always for the same reason: they are embarrassed about why they have come.

A man says he is curious, generally, intellectually, and it emerges three-quarters of the way through the afternoon that his brother died eight months ago. A woman says she has no particular expectations and turns out to have read a great deal on the subject and to have a very specific idea of what ought to happen.

I do not think of this as lying. It is what people do in first conversations about things that matter to them, and the remedy is not to press. The remedy is the fourth question — *is there anything you would rather not find?* — which gives a person a sanctioned way to gesture at the thing without naming it. Quite often the answer is a long pause and then "no, not really," said in a tone that tells me there is, and that is usually enough. I have it now. I do not need the content. I learned once, the hard way, how much work "usually" is doing in that sentence, and the chapter about the session I stopped is about what it cost.

What I do need is to have noticed, because the material that arrives in the session will be shaped by whatever they came in carrying, and I would rather know what it is before it turns up wearing a costume.

## What I listen for while they talk

Underneath the four questions I am doing something else, which is listening to how a person tells a story.

Do they give me detail or summary? A person who says "we went to my grandmother's every August and the hall smelled of coal" is going to have an easier time in that chair than a person who says "I had a normal childhood," and I adjust accordingly — more time on the induction, more patience, fewer questions.

Do they check my face? Some people tell a story while watching to see how it is landing. Those clients need more reassurance from me during the session and more silence, because they will otherwise spend the hour producing material for my benefit.

Do they correct themselves? A person who says "it was blue — no, it was green, I'm thinking of the other one" is a good sign. It means they are reporting rather than composing, and they will go on doing that with their eyes closed.

None of this is a diagnosis and I would not defend any of it as a method. It is the pattern-matching of somebody who has had this conversation several hundred times, and it is wrong often enough to keep me honest. But by the time a client puts their head back, I have a fair idea of what kind of afternoon we are going to have, and almost everything that happens next was set up in the hour before.
