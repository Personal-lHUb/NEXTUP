# Testimony and History Are Not the Same Word

A courtroom separates these two things with a single question, and it separates them so routinely that nobody in the room finds it remarkable. A witness is asked: *are you telling us what you saw, or what happened?*

The witness says she saw a blue car turn left at eleven o'clock. That is testimony. It is her account of her own experience, and it can be completely honest and completely accurate as an account of her experience while still being wrong about the world. The car might have been green under a sodium lamp. The clock she glanced at might have been slow. She is not lying. She is doing exactly what a witness is supposed to do, which is report what reached her.

Whether a blue car turned left at eleven o'clock is a different question with different evidence: the traffic camera, the ANPR record, the car's own telematics. That is the world. The court keeps the two apart because it has learned, expensively, over centuries, what happens when they are allowed to merge — when a sincere account of an experience is treated as a finding of fact simply because the person giving it is honest and certain.

Everything that happens in a regression session is the first kind of thing. All of it. Without exception.

## What the distinction actually buys you

I want to make this useful rather than philosophical, because in the abstract everybody agrees with it and then goes straight back to writing the other way.

Take a sentence of the kind that appears in a hundred books:

*Under hypnosis, she recalled a childhood in a coastal village, where she had worked mending nets until she drowned at nineteen.*

Read it again slowly. There are four claims in there, and only one of them is about the session.

The claim about the session is: *she described a coastal village, net-mending, and drowning at nineteen.* True or false, it is a report about a woman lying in a chair in a room in the present day, and the person who wrote the sentence was in that room and is well placed to report it.

The other three claims have been smuggled in by the grammar. *Recalled* asserts that there was something there to recall — that an event occurred and this is retrieval rather than production. *A childhood* asserts a life took place. *She had worked* puts a person in a village doing a job, in the actual past, as a matter of fact. None of those three things is available to anybody who was in the room, and all three arrive free of charge with a verb.

Now the same session, reported as testimony:

*She described a coastal village. She said she was mending nets, and that her hands hurt. Later she said she was in the water and that it was very cold, and she gave her age as nineteen.*

Nothing has been taken away. Every concrete detail survives. The reader knows precisely as much as before about what happened in that session — more, actually, because the second version tells you which details came in the client's own words and roughly when. What has gone is a set of assertions about the world that the writer was never in a position to make.

That is the whole technique. It is not difficult. It costs about four extra words per sentence and it is the difference between a book you can check and a book you cannot.

## Where the slide happens

In my experience it happens in three places, and they get progressively harder to catch.

The first is the transcript itself. Someone makes notes during a session and writes *drowned at 19* rather than *says she drowns, gives age 19*. By the time those notes are typed up, the hedge is gone and nobody remembers removing it. I have done this. Everyone doing this work has done this. The only defence I have found is mechanical: I write in the present tense and I attribute every line, even when I am the only person who will ever read the notebook.

The second is the retelling. A practitioner describes the session to a colleague over coffee, and in speech the hedges fall away because speech is compressed. Within a couple of tellings, *she said she was in the water* has become *she drowned*, and it stays that way, because that is now the version in the practitioner's own memory. This is not dishonesty. It is what happens to any story that gets told out loud a few times.

The third is the cover. Whatever discipline survives the first two is usually finished off by the need to describe the book in nine words. Testimony does not fit on a cover. *What she said she saw* is not a title. *Her nine lives, recovered* is. The pressure at that end of the process is enormous and almost entirely one-directional, and I have some sympathy for writers who hold the line for three hundred pages and lose it on the jacket — though the jacket is where most readers meet the book, so losing it there is losing it.

## What it does not mean

Keeping the two apart is not the same as saying the sessions are worthless, and I want to head that off now because it is the reading people expect.

Testimony is not a consolation prize. In most of human life it is the primary evidence we have about anything. Medicine runs on it: a patient's description of their own pain is not downgraded because it cannot be independently verified, it is treated as the central datum and everything else is arranged around it. History runs on it. Any account of a battle, a famine or a childhood is somebody's testimony, weighed against other testimony.

What testimony cannot do is settle questions about the external world on its own, and what it must never do is be quietly promoted into something else while the reader is not looking. A session is extraordinary testimony. It is people producing detailed, emotionally coherent, internally consistent accounts of lives they have no ordinary access to, in a state they cannot easily fake, often to their own considerable surprise. That is a genuinely strange thing and it deserves to be reported accurately, which means reported as what it is.

The irony is that the honest version is more interesting. *She described the cold of the water and gave her age as nineteen, and afterwards could not say where the number had come from* is a stranger sentence than *she drowned at nineteen*, because the second one resolves and the first one does not. Resolution is the enemy here. The material is peculiar and unresolved, and dressing it as history does not make it stronger, it makes it ordinary and checkable and, when somebody checks, false.

## Using it on your own session

If you have had a regression, or if you are going to, the distinction has a very practical use, and it is not the one people expect.

In the days after a session, the material moves. It starts as a set of impressions — a smell, a floor, a sense of being smaller than you are — and within about a fortnight it has become a story with a beginning and an end. That consolidation is normal and it happens to every kind of memory, including the ones from last Tuesday. What is particular to this material is that there is nothing to hold it down. An ordinary memory gets corrected by the world: you thought the pub was on the corner, you go back, it is three doors along, and your memory quietly updates. Session material never meets that friction. Nothing contradicts it, so it firms up unopposed, and the version you have at a month is smoother and more certain than the version you had at a day.

The single most useful thing you can do is write it down within twenty-four hours, in the first kind of sentence. Not *I was a fisherman on the west coast* but *I saw a boat, and my hands were cold, and I had a strong sense of being needed somewhere.* Write what arrived, not what it means. Put in the bits that did not fit, because those are the first things to go and they are often the most interesting — the modern word that turned up in the middle of an old scene, the detail you are fairly sure you got from a film.

Then leave it alone. If you come back to it in six months, you will have something rare: an account of the session rather than an account of the account. Almost nobody has this, which is part of why the field's own literature is weaker than it should be. The record is nearly always a retelling, and by the time anyone writes it down it has been through the process described above at least twice.

I ask every client to do this. Perhaps a third of them actually do. The ones who do almost always tell me later that the written version and the remembered version had drifted apart more than they expected — and, in more than one case, that the written version was the more interesting of the two.

## The tool, in your hands

You now have it, and you will use it for the rest of this book.

When you read any sentence in these pages, ask which kind it is. *He said the pack was heavy and kept coming back to a sound like a gate* — testimony. *There was a pack, and a gate* — a claim about the world, and if you find one of those in here without a plain statement of what it rests on, I have failed at the thing I said I would do.

Use it on me. That is not a rhetorical flourish; it is the only way the arrangement in the opening pages means anything. A writer who asks you to trust him has given you nothing. A writer who hands you the test he is asking to be judged by has at least given you the test.
