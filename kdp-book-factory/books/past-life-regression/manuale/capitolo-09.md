# The Questions I Ask, and the Ones I Have Learned Not To

A question can hand a person the answer without either of you noticing. That sentence is the whole of the craft in this job, and everything else — the chair, the blanket, the breathing — is arrangement around it.

The problem is not that clients are gullible. It is that a person in that state has stopped filtering, and a question arriving from outside does not land as a question. It lands as material. Ask a woman who has just described a floor what she can see through the window, and there is now a window, and there was not one before.

## Two questions, one silence

Here are two questions I could ask at the same moment of the same session, after a client has fallen quiet.

*What are you aware of?*

*What century is it?*

The first is almost empty. It asks for a report and specifies nothing: not a sense, not a place, not a time, not even that there should be anything to report. "Nothing" is a permitted answer, and about one time in six it is the answer I get, and that is fine.

The second contains an entire world. It asserts that there is a century, which asserts a past, which asserts that the client is somewhere in it. It asks for a number, which means a number is expected, which means one will usually be produced — and once a number has been said out loud, everything afterwards arranges itself around it. The client is not lying. She has been handed a frame and, being deeply relaxed and cooperative, she has furnished it.

I have listened to recordings of other practitioners asking that second question, and I have listened to recordings of myself asking it, and in every case you can hear the session change shape in the following ninety seconds. It becomes more specific, more confident, and less interesting, because from then on it is answering rather than reporting.

## The question I used for two years

I want to be concrete about my own version of this, because it is easy to write a chapter like this as though the errors belonged to other people.

For about two years I ended a stretch of description by asking: *and how did you die?*

It is a standard question. It is in the training. There is a reasonable rationale for it — the end of a life is where the emotional material tends to sit, and a great many clients come specifically for that, and asking cleanly is better than circling.

What I did not notice, until I listened back to a session with a client I will call Priya, is what the question does before it gets an answer. It establishes that there was a death, which establishes that there was a life, which converts the whole preceding twenty minutes from a set of impressions into a biography with an ending. Priya had described a room and a smell and a feeling of being hurried. Then I asked how she died, and there was a pause of about six seconds, and then she produced a death — quite a detailed one — and for the rest of the session she was narrating a story that now had a shape.

On the recording you can hear her take that six seconds. I had been in the room and had not registered it at all.

I stopped asking. What I ask now, if a client seems to be near something and has gone quiet, is *is there anything else there?* — and then I wait, and the waiting is longer than is comfortable. Perhaps a third of the time the client goes to an ending on their own, in their own words, and those endings are very different from the ones I used to get. They are vaguer, more partial, and they do not resolve. I take that to mean they are closer to whatever this material actually is.

## Never supply a noun

That is the rule, and I have not found a case where it needs an exception.

I do not say "window," "door," "house," "father," "village," "ship," "child," "war." I do not say "man" or "woman." I do not say "the room" unless the client has said room.

What I say instead is built almost entirely out of the client's own last few words. She says "it's cold and there's a sort of edge." I say: *tell me about the edge.* She says "the edge of the — where it drops." I say: *where it drops.* Repeating her phrase back, flat, as an invitation.

This sounds mechanical written down and it does not sound mechanical in the room. It sounds like being listened to, which is what it is. And it means that every noun in the transcript came from the client, which means the transcript is worth something. When Ingrid said "not a hat, more like a cloth," I had not said hat. When she said "my brother," I had not said brother, or family, or anyone. I can go through that session line by line and account for the origin of every concrete term in it, and that is the only reason I am willing to print it.

The cost is that my sessions are slower and produce less. A practitioner supplying nouns gets a richer transcript in half the time. I have seen it done, competently and in good faith, by people who would be horrified to be told they were shaping the material — and they were, and the shaping is right there on the recording if anyone listens.

## The three questions I do use

*What are you aware of?* Opening and re-opening.

*Tell me about that.* The workhorse, used perhaps thirty times in an hour, attached to whatever the client last said.

*Is there anything else there?* For gaps, and for endings.

That is close to the whole repertoire. There are a few practical additions — checking comfort, checking whether they want to continue — and there is the returning sequence at the end, which is a different thing. But the substantive questioning is those three, in rotation, for an hour.

People find this disappointing when I describe it. They expect a technique.

## Silence is also a question

The thing nobody warns you about is that not speaking is not neutral either.

If a client says something and I say nothing, the silence has a shape. Held too long after a particular sentence, it says: *that was interesting, go on.* Broken too quickly after another, it says: *that was not.* A client in that state is exquisitely attuned to the person in the room, far more than in ordinary conversation, and they will read the rhythm of my silences as feedback and adjust.

I know this because of a session in which I was distracted. My father was in hospital; I was there, doing the work, and about forty minutes in I lost a stretch of perhaps two minutes to worrying about something else entirely. When I listened back, the client's material had gone flat and repetitive in exactly that stretch and picked up again when I did. She had not noticed anything consciously. She told me afterwards it had been a good session.

So the discipline extends past the words. I try to keep my silences even — four or five seconds after almost anything, regardless of how interesting it was — and I do not make encouraging noises. No *mm*, no *yes*, no intake of breath. The one exception is when somebody is distressed, where warmth matters more than cleanliness, and I let the warmth win without pretending it is methodologically tidy.

## When the client asks me something

It happens in perhaps one session in four, and it is the sharpest test of all this.

A woman stops mid-description and says: *is this real? Am I making this up?*

Everything in me wants to reassure her, and reassurance would be an answer, and an answer would shape the next forty minutes. So I have a form of words that I use nearly verbatim: *You don't have to decide that now. Just tell me what's there.*

It sounds evasive on the page. In the room it works, because it is not a refusal, it is a postponement, and it takes the weight off. The question she is really asking is whether she is allowed to keep going, and the answer to that is yes.

The other thing clients ask, usually much later, is what they should do about something they have just described. A man who has found himself frightened of a door will ask whether he should open it. I do not decide that either. I say: *you can, or you can stay here, or we can stop. All three are fine.* Three options, none recommended, and the choice stays where it belongs.

## How to spot a leading question in any transcript

You can do this without any training, and it will change how you read this genre.

Find a striking detail — a name, a place, a date, an object. Now look backwards up the page for where that word first appears. If its first appearance is in the practitioner's mouth, the detail is worth nothing, however emphatically the client confirms it afterwards. Confirmation in that state is not evidence; agreement is cheap and the client has no reason to resist.

Second test: count who is talking. In a well-run session the client's words outnumber the practitioner's by something like ten to one. Transcripts where the practitioner is doing a third of the talking are not sessions, they are collaborations, and the material in them belongs to both people.

Third: look for the closed questions. *Was it summer? Were you alone? Was he angry?* Each one offers a yes, and a yes costs nothing. Open questions cost something to answer, which is why the answers are worth reading.

Apply all three to the sessions in the middle of this book. I have printed my own questions in the transcripts rather than only the answers, which is not the usual practice and makes the accounts clumsier to read, and this is why. A session account that hides the questions is asking to be taken on trust, and there is no reason on earth to extend it.
