# The First Thing a Sceptical Reader Looks Up

You know how this goes, because it has probably happened to you.

You read something in this genre. It is good — vivid, warm, the sessions are interesting, the writer clearly cares. Somewhere around a third of the way in, there is a detail you can check. A date, a place, a claim about how something was built or when something happened. You are enjoying the book, you are not out to catch anybody, and you look it up for the entirely innocent reason that you have become interested.

And it is wrong. Not arguably wrong, not a matter of interpretation. Wrong in a way that took forty seconds to establish.

## What happens next

What happens next is disproportionate and completely rational, and every writer in this field should understand it.

You do not think: that author made a small factual error on page ninety-four. You think: *this person did not check.*

And that thought is correct, and it is not confined to page ninety-four. If the writer did not check the one thing you happened to test, they did not check the others either. Every claim in the book is now in the same condition — not disproved, but unverified by anybody, including the person who wrote it down.

Then it goes further, and this is the expensive part. The sessions themselves are now suspect. Not because a wrong date makes a session false, which it does not, but because your only access to those sessions is through this writer's care, and you have just measured their care. If they were casual about a fact anybody could check, what were they like with a fact nobody could?

The book is over. You may finish it. You will not recommend it, and you will not believe it.

## Why the checkable bits are always the weakest

This is the cruel part, and it explains why the error is so common.

The checkable material is, almost by definition, the part the writer is least equipped to handle. They are a practitioner. They know the chair, the questions, the aftermath; they are the world expert on what happened in their own room. What they are not is a historian, an archivist or an archaeologist, and the checkable claims are precisely the ones that require those skills.

So a writer with genuine expertise in the central thing writes forty pages on it, competently. Then a session produces a date, and they spend twenty minutes on a search engine, and they write a sentence about a period. The sentence is the weakest in the book and it is the only one a reader can grade.

It is also, and this is the injustice, the sentence most likely to be quoted — in reviews, in arguments, by people who have not read the book. The checkable claims are the handles. They are how a text gets picked up and carried around, and if they break, the whole thing goes on the floor.

## The standard I have tried to hold

I want to state it as a rule you can test, rather than as a promise about my intentions.

**Every factual claim in this book about the world outside my room is either something I have checked, or is stated as unchecked.** There is no third category. Where I do not know, the text says I do not know, in the sentence itself and not in a note.

**No session detail has been presented as corresponding to anything unless I went and looked**, and the account says where I looked and for how long.

**Where a search found nothing, the book says nothing** — in that word — rather than going quiet.

**I have not supplied a period, a place or a conflict that a client did not name.** Callum described mud and straps and a sound; the book does not tell you which war, because he did not, and because you supplying it yourself is not the same as me telling you.

**Where an ordinary explanation exists, it is in the text**, not left to a critic.

You can test all five. That is the point of writing them down. If you find a place where I have broken one, I have failed at the specific thing this book claims to do, and no amount of the rest being good makes up for it.

## What I am not claiming

I am not claiming there are no errors. There will be. A book this length written by one person contains mistakes, and some of mine will be in the checkable parts because those are the parts I am least qualified for.

The distinction I would ask you to hold is between an error and a practice. An error is a wrong fact that somebody tried to get right. A practice is a book in which nothing was checked, and in which the wrong facts are wrong for the same reason the right ones are right, which is chance.

You can usually tell the difference from the shape of the text. A book that checked things will contain, somewhere, a sentence saying that a check came back empty — because if you check a hundred things, some come back empty, and reporting only the successes is the tell. A book with no failures in it did not look.

## The four errors I nearly made

It seems useful to show the near misses, because they are more instructive than the rule.

**The war.** In writing up Callum's session I had, in an early draft, the phrase "the kind of mud that comes up in accounts of trench warfare." Every word of that is hedged and defensible. It also plants a specific conflict in the reader's head, from a session in which the client explicitly declined to say when he was, three times. I cut it. What is left is thinner and it is the only version I can defend.

**The name.** I had written, about Ellen's *Mirushka*, that the name "is found in several eastern European traditions." That is true as far as I could establish. It is also completely irrelevant, because names being found in traditions is not a finding about Ellen, and its only function in the paragraph was to make the reader feel that something had been located. It went.

**The latch.** My first draft of the gate-latch chapter described the motif as "regionally distinctive," which is how one source characterised it. When I looked properly, "distinctive" was doing more work than the evidence supported — the form appears across a wider area than that phrasing implies. The chapter now says "a documented style" and "a recognised motif," which is duller and accurate.

**The statistic.** I had a sentence giving a specific figure for the number of human beings who have ever lived, quoted to three significant figures, which I had picked up somewhere years ago and could not source. The chapter on famous lifetimes now says "a very large number" and explains why the argument does not need better than that. Losing the figure cost the paragraph some force and gained it the property of being true.

All four survived at least one draft. Three of the four were caught by the same method, which is reading my own sentences and asking what they are *for*.

## What to do when you find one

In any book, including this one.

Write it down and keep reading. One error does not license throwing out the testimony, and the testimony is a different kind of claim with a different kind of support. The writer's account of what happened in their own room does not become false because they got a date wrong.

Then look for a second. The pattern matters more than the instance. One wrong fact is a mistake; three in the same book, all in the same direction, all making the case stronger than it is, is a practice.

Notice the direction. This is the most useful test I know. Errors that are random — some helping the argument, some hurting it — are the signature of ordinary carelessness. Errors that all point the same way are the signature of something else, and it need not be conscious. A writer who checks the claims that trouble them and waves through the ones that please them will produce a book with directional errors and will not have decided to deceive anyone.

And tell them. Most writers would rather know. If I have got something wrong in here, I would like to correct it, and there is no edition so final that it cannot be fixed in the next one.

## Why I have written this chapter at all

Because it is the chapter that explains the other twenty-nine.

The care taken over the checking in this book is disproportionate to what the checking found. I searched for six hours for a name and found nothing. I spent four days on a gate latch and then dissolved my own best case with a phone call. I have reported four sessions that contain nothing to check, and said so four times, in the same flat words.

That is a poor return on effort if the purpose was to build a case. It is not why I did it.

I did it because you are going to check. Not every reader, but enough — and the reader who checks is the reader worth having, because they are the one who will still be here at the end, and the one who might hand the book to somebody else. Everything in this book that is worth anything depends on that reader finding, when they look, exactly what I said they would find.
