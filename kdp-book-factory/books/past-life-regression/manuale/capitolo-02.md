# The Question Everyone Asks Me First

There are two versions of the question and they sound identical.

The first version gets asked in the intake chair, usually about twenty minutes in, usually by someone who has been circling it since they sat down. They have told me why they came. They have told me about the dream, or the fear of the stairs, or the sister who died in March. Then there is a pause, and they look at the carpet, and they say: *do you believe it's real?*

The second version gets asked at a dinner party by a man holding a glass of wine who has just found out what I do. Same five words. Entirely different transaction. He is not asking a question, he is opening a negotiation about whether I am a serious person, and the answer he is listening for is the one that tells him which box to put me in.

For a long time I answered both the same way, and I answered both badly.

## Why it is the wrong thing to hand a practitioner

Here is the problem with the question. It asks me to certify something I have no way of certifying, using the authority of having been in the room — and being in the room gives me no such authority at all.

What being in the room gives me is this: I know what was said. I know how long the pause was before she said it. I know that her hands were open on the blanket and then they were not. I know the exact wording of the question I had asked forty seconds earlier, because it is on the recording and I have listened to it eleven times. On all of that I am the best witness available, and I take that seriously.

What being in the room does not give me is any information whatsoever about whether there was ever a house with those stairs, or a woman who climbed them. I was not there. Nobody was there. The room contains two people, a chair and a recorder, and nothing that happens inside it can tell you anything about any century but this one. This is so obvious when you write it down that it is strange how much of this field is built on pretending otherwise — on the idea that proximity to the experience confers some kind of standing on claims about the past.

It does not. A nurse who sits with a dying man and hears him describe a room full of light is the best possible witness to what he said. She is not, by virtue of that, an authority on what is on the other side of the door.

So when a client asks me, I no longer answer the question I was asked. I answer a better one.

## What I say instead

I say: *I can tell you what people say in that chair, in detail, and I can tell you which parts of it could be looked up and what happens when you look them up. Those are two different reports, and I will keep them separate for you. What I can't do is combine them into a verdict, because there isn't one.*

Most people find this a relief. That surprised me at first. I had assumed — I think most of us in this work assume — that a client wants the practitioner to believe, that belief is part of what they are paying for, that hedging would feel like being short-changed.

It is the opposite, in my experience. A great many of the people who come to me are intelligent, careful, slightly embarrassed to be here, and braced for someone to start performing certainty at them. When I decline to perform it, they relax. The session goes better. They stop trying to produce the sort of material they imagine I want, which is, in my experience, the largest contaminant in this work and the subject of a later chapter.

The dinner party version I answer differently, and more shortly. I say that I sit with people while they describe things, and that the descriptions are interesting, and that the people are usually better than the descriptions. If he wants a fight he goes and finds someone else; I have run out of appetite for having the same argument at parties.

## The third version, which is the hard one

There is a third place the question gets asked, and it is the only one that still gives me trouble.

A woman has been in the chair for an hour and ten minutes. She has described a kitchen with a stone floor and a child she was frightened for, and at one point she cried in a way I have rarely seen anyone cry about something they knew they were making up. She has sat up, drunk a glass of water, sat quietly for a while. She is putting her coat on. And at the door, not looking at me, she says: *was that real?*

The dinner party answer is useless here. The clinical answer — that I cannot say, that the material is hers to interpret — is technically correct and, delivered at that moment, unkind to the point of cowardice. She has just been through something, she is asking a person she trusts, and what she needs is not a disclaimer.

What I have settled on, after getting it wrong a great many times, is to answer the question underneath the question. Almost nobody at that door is asking for a metaphysical ruling. They are asking whether what just happened to them counts — whether they are allowed to take it seriously, or whether they have spent an afternoon and a fair amount of money making things up in a nice room.

So I say: *what happened to you in that chair happened. You didn't perform it. Nothing in the last hour looked like performing to me. Whether there was a kitchen with a stone floor, I don't know and I can't find out. But you weren't pretending, and whatever that was, it's yours.*

That is as true as I can make it, and it is the most I can honestly hand her. Sometimes it is not what she wanted. Twice, in fourteen years, a client has been angry with me at that door, and on both occasions they were entitled to be, because they had come looking for certainty and I had let them get all the way to the end before making clear I did not sell it. I now say it at the beginning instead. That is the reason for the conversation described later in the book, the one that happens before anyone closes their eyes.

## The two reports

Everything in this book is one of two kinds of statement, and I have tried never to let them blur.

The first kind is testimony. *She said the stairs turned twice before the landing. She counted them out loud, and she got to forty-one, and then she said "forty-one, always forty-one" in a voice that was not the voice she had been using with me.* Those sentences are reports about a session. They can be right or wrong the way any account of an event can be right or wrong — I could have misheard, I could have misremembered, my notes could be poor — but they are claims about a room, and a room is the sort of thing a person in it can speak about.

The second kind is a claim about the world. *There was a house with forty-one stairs. A woman lived in it. She died in the winter of a named year.* Those sentences are not about the room at all. They are about the world outside it, and no amount of sitting in a reclining chair produces evidence for them.

The trouble is that the two kinds of sentence look the same on the page, and in many of the accounts of this work I have read they are printed in the same paragraph, in the same tone, at the same speed. A reader moves from one to the other without noticing the border has been crossed, because the writer has not marked it. Often, I think, the writer has not noticed either.

Once you can see the border you cannot unsee it, and you will start seeing it everywhere, including in conversations about things that have nothing to do with hypnosis. It is one of the more useful things this work has given me.

## What I want from you in exchange

Six of the eight session chapters in this book contain nothing that anyone could check. Not one date, not one surname, not one place name, not a single fact that could be put into a search box. That is typical. It is more typical than the alternative, and it is the first thing that surprises people who come to this expecting the genre's greatest hits.

I am asking you to stay for those sessions anyway.

I could have left them out. There were eight places to fill and a great many sessions to fill them from, and I could have chosen differently, filled the middle of the book with the ones that glitter, and let you conclude that this is what the work is like. Every one of us who writes about this faces that choice, and the shelf is the shape it is because of how the choice usually goes.

What you get for staying is a book you can check. If I am willing to report the empty ones, you have some reason to trust me when I report a full one — and there is one session here that did produce a match, further in, and I spend most of that chapter explaining why the match is worth less than it appears.

That is the arrangement. I keep the two reports separate; you read the ones that go nowhere. Neither half works without the other.
