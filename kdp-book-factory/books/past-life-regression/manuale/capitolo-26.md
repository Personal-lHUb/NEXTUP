# Where the Material Comes From When It Isn't a Past Life

A reader who has not met these explanations properly cannot evaluate anything in this book, including the parts I want them to take seriously. So here they are, laid out at their full strength rather than waved at.

I want to say in advance what I am not doing. I am not presenting these as the answer and then adding a polite paragraph at the end about mystery. Nor am I building them up in order to knock them down. I think they account for a great deal — more than most people in this field allow — and I think they leave some things unaccounted for, and I will say which.

## Forgotten sources

The strongest of the three, and the one with the clearest demonstration in this book.

Human beings absorb enormous quantities of material and retain almost none of it consciously. A documentary watched at eleven. A book on a grandparent's shelf, opened once. A film half-watched in a hotel room. A museum display. A leaflet on a dresser. A conversation overheard on a bus.

Almost all of that is inaccessible to ordinary recall — you cannot retrieve it by trying — but some of it leaves traces, and fragments can resurface detached from any memory of where they came from; psychologists call this cryptomnesia, or source amnesia. What resurfaces is not a recording. It comes back partial and reshaped, and nothing about a relaxed or hypnotic state has been shown to make it more accurate; if anything, it makes people more confident in it. That detachment is the crucial part: you do not experience it as a memory of a documentary. You experience it as something you know, arriving from nowhere.

Ruth's gate latch is exactly this, and it is the reason her chapter is in the book. A woman with no interest in ironwork produced a close description of an object she had seen at eight, and very probably on a leaflet on the family dresser for years afterwards. She could not have told you she had seen it. The leaflet came back in a five-minute phone call, and if I had not made the call I would have had a miracle.

The strength of this explanation is that it is sometimes testable — once, in this book, a phone call turned up a plausible source — and that everybody has an enormous stock of such material. The weakness, which is not small, is that it is very nearly unfalsifiable. Any specific detail can be attributed to a forgotten source, and the attribution cannot be checked unless you get lucky, as I did once.

That cuts both ways, and honest people should notice it cutting the second way. An explanation that can absorb any evidence is not doing scientific work. It is a bucket. I use it because in the one case where it was testable it produced the most plausible answer available, and because the sheer volume of material people absorb makes it prior-plausible — but I try to notice when I am reaching for it because it is convenient.

## Suggestion

Less of it is needed than people imagine, and it does not require anything as crude as a leading question.

There is a chapter earlier in this book about a question I asked for two years — *how did you die?* — which does its work before any answer is given, by establishing that there was a death and therefore a life. Few people hearing that question think of it as suggestion. It was in the training I received.

Beyond questions, suggestion arrives through the room itself. A person books a past-life regression. They pay for it. They travel to a house and sit in a chair and are asked to relax by a practitioner who does this for a living. Everything about the situation says that something is expected to arrive, and something arriving is what the afternoon is for. That is an enormous amount of framing before a single word is exchanged.

Then there is the practitioner's manner, which cannot be neutralised. My silences have shapes, as described earlier; my attention is legible; a client in that state is unusually attuned to the person in the room. I try to keep my responses even and I do not believe I succeed completely.

And there is the client's own expectation, named in the intake for exactly this reason. Callum expected to be a soldier. Callum said afterwards that he had been one.

The strength of this explanation is that the mechanism is visible and documented and I can show you my own version of it on tape. The weakness is that it does not obviously account for material that surprises the client — the sessions that go somewhere the person did not want and did not anticipate, like Jo's, who expected music and got heat.

## The narrative instinct

The third explanation and the one that is hardest to argue with, because it describes something every one of us does continuously.

A mind presented with fragments builds a story out of them. Not occasionally: constantly, automatically, and below the level of choice. It is the same kind of work that makes a dream feel coherent while you are in it, that fills in the gaps in a half-heard conversation, that produces a plausible reason for a decision you made for no reason at all.

Give that machinery an hour, a quiet room, no external input, and an instruction to report what comes, and it will very often produce a coherent something. Not always: about one session in eight of mine produces nothing, and many produce only fragments. But when it produces anything, it tends towards coherence, because producing coherence is what it is for.

This explains a great deal about the texture of sessions, including features that look like evidence. It explains the internal consistency — a person who has described a kitchen will keep the kitchen consistent, because that is what narrative machinery does. It explains the emotional appropriateness. It explains why the material feels found rather than made, since the construction happens below the level at which you could observe it.

The weakness is that it is a description rather than a mechanism. Saying that the mind makes stories is true and almost unhelpfully general. It does not tell you why *this* story, with these particular details, for this person.

## What they do not account for

Three things, and I put them here because a chapter of this kind that ended with "and so it is all explained" would be doing exactly what I have criticised in the other direction.

**The asymmetry.** If this material is constructed, why does it construct so poorly in one specific category? A mind building a life could easily supply a year, a name, a place. One might expect those to be the easiest things to generate, and they are the things that almost never arrive unasked. The ordinary explanations can reach it — as I said in the chapter on patterns, it may be an artefact of my own method, since I never ask for dates — but I did not expect it before I counted, and it is where I would most like someone with a different method to count the same categories.

**The voice.** Perhaps one session in ten contains a few seconds in which the delivery changes — speed, rhythm, a vowel. I do not know what it is. It is not evidence of a past life; it is not evidence of anything. The obvious ordinary reading is role enactment — people telling a story in a character's voice shift their delivery without meaning to — and that may be all it is. I list it because it is the part of sessions I understand least, not because nothing could explain it.

**The reluctance.** Clients decline. They stop at doors, they will not look at things, they refuse dates. I would not have predicted refusal from a process that is making things up, though an ordinary reading exists: a person uncomfortable with where a scene is going, or with nothing to offer, may stop rather than invent.

None of those three is an argument for anything. They are three places where I can see the edge of what I understand, and I would rather mark them than paper over them.

## The fourth explanation, which nobody likes

There is one more, and it gets left out of accounts in both directions because it satisfies nobody.

It is that the question is badly formed.

"Where does the material come from?" presupposes that it comes from somewhere — that there is a source, and the job is to identify it. That is how we think about memories, because memories have sources, and the whole vocabulary of this field is borrowed from memory.

But a great deal of what minds produce does not have a source in that sense. A tune you find yourself humming that is not any tune. A face in a dream belonging to nobody. The specific shade of a colour you imagine when someone says a word. None of those is retrieved whole from any one place; they are assembled out of the general capacity to generate, and asking which single experience they came from is probably the wrong question.

If session material is more like that than like memory, then both camps in this argument have been asking the wrong question with great energy for a long time. The believer wants the source to be a previous life. The sceptic wants it to be a documentary at eleven. Both want a source, and both are using the model that comes free with the word "remembered" — which is on the list of six words this book does not use for anything said in a session, and which I struck for exactly this reason.

I find this explanation the most persuasive of the four and the least satisfying to write about, because it does not produce chapters. There is nothing to check and nothing to trace and no story to tell. It is also the one that best accounts for the asymmetry: a generative process would produce texture in abundance and facts hardly at all, because texture is cheap to generate and a verifiable name is not.

I hold it loosely, along with the other three, and I would not build anything on it.

## Why this chapter is not an insult

Because being offered an ordinary explanation is not being called a fool.

The material in these sessions is real material. It arrives, it has force, it does things to people. The question of where it comes from is genuinely open, and the ordinary answers are not dismissals — they are proposals about a mind that turns out to be far stranger than the folk version. Ruth's line is still the best summary anyone has given me: a brain that kept a leaflet for forty-eight years and would not give it back until she lay down is not a smaller story than the other one. It is a different one, and it is frightening in its own way.

What would be an insult is presenting you with only one side of this and letting you think it was the whole. You can now weigh the sessions in the middle of this book against these explanations yourself, which is what I would have wanted from a writer, and which few writers in this field have offered me.
