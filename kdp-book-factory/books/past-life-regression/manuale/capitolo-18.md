# Two Sisters, One Room, Two Different Lives

Bernadette and Jo booked together, which I do not normally allow.

My reason for not allowing it is straightforward: if two people who know each other do this a week apart, the first one talks to the second one, and the second one arrives with the first one's session in her head. It is the cleanest way I know to contaminate an afternoon.

I made an exception because they proposed a condition I had not thought of. They would not discuss it. Not a word, either way, until both sessions were done and both of them had written their accounts. Jo suggested it, and she is a person who keeps to things, and they kept to it — I asked both of them afterwards, separately, and I believe them.

What came out of it is the closest I have to a controlled comparison, which is to say not very close at all, but better than nothing.

## Two people, one upbringing

They are sisters, sixteen months apart, raised in the same house by the same parents, schooled together, and close.

Bernadette, the elder, is a nurse. Practical, sceptical in a good-humoured way, and came, she said, "to see what Jo makes of it." Her stated expectation was "nothing, probably."

Jo teaches music. She had read a fair amount on the subject and said so, which I appreciated; people who have read a great deal usually minimise it. Her stated expectation was much more specific: she thought she would find "somewhere warm, with a lot of people, and music."

Two women with a shared history, shared references, shared childhood television, shared holidays. If session material comes out of what a person has absorbed, these two have absorbed a very large amount of the same material.

## Bernadette's afternoon

She took thirty-one minutes to settle, which is at the slow end, and then produced one of the flattest sessions I have recorded.

> — What are you aware of?
>
> — I'm sitting down and I'm waiting for someone to stop talking.
>
> — Tell me about that.
>
> — They're going on. *(pause)* There's a lot of them and they're all going on.

Her entire session took place in one position. She was seated, indoors, in a room with other people in it, for an hour and ten minutes, and she never moved and never went anywhere.

What she gave me: a hard seat, an ache in one hip, a draught from behind, cloth over her knees that she kept smoothing. A great many people, none of them described. A sense of enormous impatience. One moment, near the end, when she said "I could just go. I could just get up and go," and laughed — actually laughed, in the chair, which is rare.

She did not go.

Afterwards she said it had been "an hour of being in a meeting," and that it was, on the whole, exactly what she would have expected her own subconscious to produce.

## Jo's afternoon, a fortnight later

Jo settled in nine minutes and produced a great deal.

Outdoors, moving, most of the time. A road, then not a road. Water crossed at some point — she was unclear about how. Heat, described repeatedly and with obvious physical discomfort; she was flushed in the chair. Something carried on the head, which she mentioned twice and could not elaborate on.

No music. Not one mention of it, in an hour and a half, from a musician who had walked in expecting music.

She did produce people, and unlike her sister she produced them in some detail: a man with a bad leg, a group of children who were "not mine but they're with me," and somebody ahead she was following without knowing why.

Her session had a shape, which Bernadette's did not — a sense of a journey being undertaken for a reason, though the reason never arrived.

## The two places they touched

Both sessions contained, independently, a piece of cloth that mattered.

Bernadette had cloth over her knees that she kept smoothing — a repeated, absent gesture, mentioned four times. Jo had something on her head, twice, which she could not describe in the chair and which was, when I asked her afterwards, "folded cloth, I think — something you put there so the load sits right."

Both sessions also contained a strong sense of waiting for or following a person who was not described. Bernadette waiting for people to stop talking; Jo following somebody ahead without knowing why.

That is the whole overlap. Two elements, both extremely general.

## The arithmetic of that

I want to do this properly, because "the sisters both described cloth" is the kind of sentence that sounds significant until you count.

Cloth appears in a large proportion of sessions. So does waiting. So does following. Between them, Bernadette and Jo produced perhaps a hundred and twenty discrete details across two afternoons, and two of those details rhymed at a level of generality that would let almost any two sessions rhyme.

The honest way to put it is this: if you gave me any two of the sessions in this book and asked me to find two overlapping elements, I could do it every time. I have just tried it, with Maureen and Callum, who have nothing in common: both sessions contain cold arriving early and something returned to again and again — her stairs, his mud. That is the same quality of match as the cloth, and nobody would think it meant anything.

What would have meant something is a shared specific. If Jo had produced a backward-looking bird on a gate latch, or Bernadette had said *Mirushka*, we would have something worth a chapter of its own. Neither did, and in two sessions by two sisters raised in the same house, that absence is more interesting than the presence of cloth.

## What they said to each other

I let them compare notes, which meant breaking a second rule in one case: the first was booking them at all, and the second is that I do not discuss one client's session with another.

They did it in front of me, at their request, and it took about twenty minutes. Bernadette was disappointed by her own session and said so cheerfully. Jo was more affected than she let on and got slightly defensive about it.

The exchange I remember was Bernadette saying, "But you always were the one who got the good dreams," and Jo saying, "I'm not making it up," and Bernadette saying, "I didn't say you were," and both of them stopping.

They are sisters. The session content was almost the least interesting thing in the room at that point.

What both of them independently said afterwards — Jo in writing, Bernadette on the phone — was a version of the same thing: that the afternoon had told them more about themselves than about anybody else. Bernadette said she had been "sat in a room being annoyed, which is the story of my life." Jo said hers was "me going somewhere with no idea why, which is also the story of my life."

Neither of them offered that as an explanation. Both offered it as an observation. I have quoted them accurately and I am leaving it there.

## The check

Nothing, in both.

Bernadette: no name, no place, no date, no object beyond cloth and a hard seat. Nothing whatsoever.

Jo: no name, no place, no date. Heat, a road, water, something carried on the head, a man with a bad leg. Every one of those is either unfalsifiable or so general as to be meaningless as a search term. There is no record anywhere of a woman walking somewhere hot, and there never will be.

## The experiment I did not run

There is an obvious next step and I did not take it, and I want to say why, because it bears on what kind of book this is.

The obvious step is to do this deliberately and at scale. Recruit pairs — siblings, spouses, strangers matched for age and background — run them through an identical protocol, record everything, and have somebody who was not in the room score the overlaps against a pre-agreed list of categories. Do it a hundred times. Publish the rate.

That would be worth something. It would be the first thing anyone asked for, and as far as I know nobody has a good answer to it — which is not because the question is difficult.

I have not done it for three reasons, and only one of them reflects well on me. The first is that I am a practitioner, not a researcher, and I have neither the training to design that study nor the statistical competence to analyse it, and a badly designed version would be worse than nothing because it would produce a number people would quote.

The second is that the people who come to me are not subjects. They arrive with something, usually something difficult, and turning their afternoon into a data point changes what I am doing in the room — and it would change it in the direction of my own interests rather than theirs.

The third, which is the honest one, is that I have not wanted to enough. I could have found a way. Somebody with the right training would work with me if I asked properly. I have been doing this for fourteen years and I have not asked, and when I examine why, the answer is not flattering: I am not certain I want the number.

I am reporting the sisters as an anecdote, then, and labelling it as one. Two sessions is not a study and a coincidence of my own noticing is not a finding. What I can say is that the comparison was available to me, I looked at it as carefully as I could, and what I found was two dissimilar afternoons and a piece of cloth.

## What the pair demonstrates

One thing, and it is worth the two afternoons it took to establish.

The procedure is the same. The room is the same, the chair is the same, the induction is the same, the practitioner is the same, and the questions are the same three in rotation. Two women with much of their genetics, a childhood and a cultural diet in common went through an identical process a fortnight apart and produced sessions with almost nothing in common — different settings, different postures, different emotional registers, different lengths, different depths.

Whatever this material is, the method alone does not seem to decide it: the method was a constant here and the output varied enormously. Two sessions cannot tell you more than that. What they suggest is that it comes from the person, and from something quite deep in the person, because both of them recognised their own session as characteristic of themselves the moment it was over.

That does not tell you what the source is. It tells you where to look.
