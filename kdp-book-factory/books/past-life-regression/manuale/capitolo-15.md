# A Kitchen, a Window, and a Name She Could Not Pronounce

This is the session that is rich in specifics, and it is here because that is exactly the kind of session that ends up being presented elsewhere as evidence.

Ellen was thirty-four, a veterinary nurse, and she came for the most ordinary reason anybody comes: curiosity, plus a friend who had been. She had no symptom, no grief, no decision pending. In the intake she said she expected "probably nothing," and when I asked whether there was anything she would rather not find, she laughed and said "spiders."

You have already met four minutes of her session, earlier in this book. What follows is the rest.

## The room

The first material was the wall, the window and the pan, as set down earlier. From there it thickened considerably, which is unusual — most sessions thin out rather than the reverse.

Over about fifty minutes she gave me, in fragments and out of order:

A floor of flat stones, uneven, with one that rocked. She mentioned the rocking stone three separate times, widely spaced, and on the third occasion said "you get used to which one it is."

A window set high in the wall, too high to see out of, with light coming through but no view. She was quite insistent about this and slightly frustrated by it.

A door that led somewhere she described only as "through" — never out, never in, just through.

A shelf with things on it she did not itemise. When asked, she said "the things," in a tone that suggested the question was odd.

A heavy pan with a burnt base she was scraping. Later, a different pan, or possibly the same one, that she describes as "the good one."

Cold hands. Cold feet. A back that hurt from standing.

And a person, referred to throughout as "she," who might come in, whose arrival was not exactly feared but was something to be ready for.

## The name

About forty minutes in, unprompted, she began repeating a word.

I have written it here as it sounded to me on the recording, which is the practice described earlier in this book: *Mirushka*. Possibly *Merushka*. The stress is on the second syllable and the first vowel is unclear.

She said it eleven times over the course of about twelve minutes. She said it as an address — the way you say a name across a room to get somebody's attention — and once she said it twice in quick succession, impatiently.

I asked, once, who that was. She said: "She's — " and then nothing for nineteen seconds, and then, "she's little."

I asked nothing else about it. I did not ask whether it was a daughter, a sister, a servant or a cat, because all four of those nouns would have been mine.

Afterwards Ellen was frustrated by this above everything else. She said she had been "trying to get hold of it" and could not — that the name had been completely clear while she was saying it and had gone almost immediately afterwards, and that her own recording of herself saying it did not sound the way it had sounded in her head.

## What I searched, and how

Ellen asked me to look. That is worth saying: I do not usually, and I did this because she asked.

I spent, in total, about six hours over three evenings.

I looked for the name as a personal name, in the spellings that seemed plausible from the recording and in several that did not, across the obvious general sources and two specialist name databases. Names of that shape exist. They exist across a wide geography and a long period, and there are diminutive forms of several common names that could produce something sounding like it.

I looked for the room. This is where the exercise becomes absurd, and I want to walk you through the absurdity, because the absurdity is the finding. A kitchen with a stone floor, a high window and a heavy pan describes millions of rooms at the very least, across several centuries and a great deal of the world. There is no search. There is no database of rooms. The only thing that would narrow it is a place name or a date, and she gave neither.

I looked for the combination — the name plus anything else in the session — which is the only search that could possibly have meant anything. There was nothing to combine it with.

## What came back

Nothing.

Not "nothing conclusive." Not "nothing definitive, though there are suggestive parallels." Nothing. Six hours of searching produced no fact about the world that is any different for Ellen's session having happened.

I am labouring this because the alternative version of this paragraph is very easy to write, and I have read it many times in other people's books. It goes like this: *the name is attested in the region from the fourteenth century, and stone-floored kitchens with high windows are characteristic of the period.* Both halves of that sentence could be true and it would still be worthless, because it would be telling you that Ellen produced a name that exists and a room that existed. So does everyone. The set of things that have existed is extremely large.

A match only means something if it was unlikely. An unremarkable name in an unremarkable room is the most likely thing a session could possibly produce.

## What this tells us, and what it does not

Here is the part I want to be careful about, because "nothing came back" is not the same as "there was nothing there."

The record for ordinary people in most times and places is thin to the point of non-existence. Most human beings who have lived left no trace that could be searched: no register entry that survived, no document, no stone. If Ellen's session were a perfectly accurate account of an actual woman in an actual kitchen, the overwhelmingly likely outcome of a search would be exactly what I got.

So the empty result does not tell us the session was fiction. It tells us the session is unfalsifiable, which is a statement about the record rather than about the session.

That cuts both ways, and the second way is the one people forget. An unfalsifiable account cannot be confirmed either. If nothing could have come back regardless of the truth, then nothing coming back is not evidence, and nothing coming back would not have been evidence either. The session sits entirely outside the reach of the question, and no amount of searching was ever going to change that.

This is the ordinary condition of almost everything in this field. It is not a scandal. It is only a scandal when somebody pretends otherwise.

## Ellen afterwards

She was disappointed, which I had expected, and she was disappointed in a specific way I had not.

She was not disappointed that the name did not check out. She said she had assumed it would not. What bothered her was that she could not get the name back — that the clearest thing in the whole afternoon had been available to her for twelve minutes and was now gone, and that she had spent weeks trying to retrieve it and had ended up with a worn-down version she no longer trusted.

She said, about three months later: "I know I've reshaped it. It's like a word you've said too many times."

She has not had another session and says she probably will not. She describes the afternoon as "the most interesting thing that's happened to me in years" and does not believe any of it was a past life. She holds both of those at once without apparent difficulty, which is more than most of us manage.

## The three things I keep returning to

Having said all that, there are three details in this session I have not been able to put down, and it would be dishonest to leave them out because they complicate the tidy account above.

The first is the rocking stone. Ellen mentioned it three times, at intervals of roughly twenty minutes, with no prompting from me and no apparent awareness that she had mentioned it before. On the third occasion she added something: "you get used to which one it is." That is not a description, it is a habit, and habits are a peculiar thing for an invented scene to contain. I would not expect somebody constructing a room to put in a floor stone that rocks, forget about it, and then come back to it with a practical note about living with it.

The second is the high window. She was frustrated by it in the session and remained mildly frustrated by it afterwards — a window you cannot see out of is an obstruction, and an imagined room does not usually obstruct its own occupant. When people furnish a scene, they tend to furnish it with things they can use.

The third is "the things." I asked what was on the shelf and she answered as though I had asked something faintly stupid. That flicker of impatience is not in any of my notes as a quotation because it was in her tone rather than her words, and it is the thing I remember most clearly about the afternoon.

None of that is evidence. All three are exactly what you would predict from a mind that is good at generating coherent scenes and has been given an hour and nothing to do. I am reporting them because they are what a practitioner actually notices, and because a book that reported only the parts supporting its own argument would be doing the thing this book was written against.

## Why it is in the book

Because it is the session I would have written up dishonestly, if I were going to write one up dishonestly.

It has everything the genre wants: an unusual name, repeated; a room with tactile specifics; a rocking stone; an emotional undertow about a person who is "little." Given six hours and a willingness to write *appears to correspond*, I could have made this chapter sing.

What it actually is, is a woman who spent an afternoon describing a kitchen, produced a name I could not trace, and found the experience remarkable and unresolved. That is the whole of it, and putting it in the middle of the book unresolved is the most useful thing I can do with it.
