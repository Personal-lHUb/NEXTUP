# The Man Who Could Not Swim

Douglas was forty-eight and had been frightened of open water since before he could remember being frightened of anything.

Not water generally. He showered, bathed, could stand in a swimming pool if the pool was small and he could see all four sides of it. What he could not do was go near a lake, a river, an estuary or the sea. He had not stood on a beach in twenty years. He had missed a brother's wedding because it was on an island.

He had done two courses of treatment for it, one of them reasonably intensive, and he was careful to tell me that both had been useful — he had learned a great deal about his own physiology and had a set of techniques he used. What he had not been able to do was go near the water.

## Why this chapter is about the fear

Most session chapters, including the previous one, ask you to take the content seriously on the strength of how it arrived. This one is different, and it is the reason I put Douglas second.

The fear is observable. It existed before the session, it was described by him in specific terms, it had a history that other people could confirm, and it had a measurable consequence: a man who does not go to beaches. Whatever happened in that chair, there is something outside the chair that can be looked at before and after.

That is not evidence about a past life. Nothing here is. But it is evidence about something, and it is a rarer thing in this field than it should be, because most of what people bring to these sessions is interior and unmeasurable.

## The intake

He was not a believer and said so at some length, in the slightly apologetic way of a man who does not want to insult the person he is paying.

When I asked what he expected, he said: "I expect to find out that I nearly drowned when I was two and nobody told me." That is a reasonable expectation and I said so. I also said it back to him, out loud, twice, because it was the most specific expectation anyone had brought into that room in months and I wanted it named before we started.

Nothing resembling it turned up.

His answer to the fourth question — is there anything you would rather not find — was immediate: "That it's my father's fault." He and his father had a difficult relationship. Nothing about his father turned up either.

## The session

He took twenty-six minutes to settle, which is slow, and for the first stretch he produced almost nothing.

Then:

> — What are you aware of?
>
> — Rope. *(pause)* There's rope round my hand, it's round here.
>
> — Tell me about the rope.
>
> — It's wet and it's swollen up. *(eleven seconds)* You can't get it off when it's like that.

That is the whole of the physical detail for the next ten minutes. Rope, wet, swollen, wound round the hand and wrist. He came back to it perhaps eight times.

Then a shift:

> — I'm not in it. I'm looking at it.
>
> — Tell me about that.
>
> — It's flat. It's flat and it's grey and it's — *(pause)* — it's flat all the way.
>
> — Is there anything else there?
>
> *(thirty-one seconds)*
>
> — There's something in it.

And then he stopped talking, for about two minutes, and then he said, in a voice that had gone quite small: "I'm not going to look at that."

## The two minutes he could not speak

That is the centre of the session and I have very little to put in it.

His breathing changed — faster, shallower, the kind of thing you would notice across a room. His hands closed. He did not speak for two minutes and eleven seconds, which is the longest silence I have recorded in a session that did not end.

I said, twice, that he did not have to look at anything, and that we could stop whenever he wanted. Both times he shook his head, slightly, which is a thing people do in that state and which always slightly startles me.

Then he said, "It's all right, it's in the water, it's not in here," and his breathing settled, and about a minute after that he said, "I don't want to do any more of this," and I brought him back.

Total session content: a rope, a flat grey expanse, and something in it he would not look at. Twenty minutes of material in an afternoon.

## Afterwards

He was shaken and irritated, in roughly equal measure, and the irritation was directed at himself.

He said the thing that a great many people say: "I don't know if I made that up." Then he said something less common, which I have thought about since: "I don't think it matters, does it. It was still the worst two minutes I've had in a while."

He did not want to talk about the content and I did not ask. He asked me twice what I thought it meant and I told him, twice, that the meaning was his and that I was not going to supply one.

## Eleven weeks later

He booked a swimming lesson.

He told me about it in an email, and the email is worth quoting because of its tone, which is not the tone of a man who has had a revelation:

*Went to the pool on Tuesday, the big one, not the little one. Got in. Stayed about twenty minutes. Didn't enjoy it particularly but I wasn't frightened, which was odd. Wife cried, which was embarrassing for everyone.*

At about eight months he wrote again to say he had been to a beach. He walked on it. He did not go in the sea and said he had no plans to.

I want to be careful here, and the care is the whole point. I do not know that the session did that. Douglas had been working on this for years with other people, and it is entirely possible that he was ready and that my afternoon was the last thing that happened before something that was going to happen anyway. He said himself, in the eight-month message, that he thought the previous treatment "did the work and this shook it loose," and his guess about his own life is better than mine.

What I can report is the sequence: a man who had avoided open water for two decades, an afternoon in which he described a rope and a grey expanse and could not speak for two minutes, and a swimming pool eleven weeks later.

## The check

There is nothing to check, and this time the nothing is unusually complete.

No name. No place. No date. No period — he gave no indication of when any of it was supposed to be, and there was nothing in the material to date it by: rope is rope, and water is water, and a man looking at both could be standing anywhere in human history.

No object with distinguishing features. No event, even: nothing happened in this session. A man held a rope and looked at some water and declined to look at something in it.

I have seen accounts of sessions like this one written up with a shipwreck in them. There is no shipwreck here. There is no boat. He never mentioned a boat, and I never mentioned a boat, and the temptation to supply one is considerable because it would make the paragraph work.

## The ordinary explanations, applied here

Before I say what I think this session is for, it is only fair to run it past the explanations that do not require a past life, because a reader who has not seen them applied to a specific case cannot judge how much work they do.

A man frightened of open water, asked to relax and report what comes, produces images of water. That is not mysterious. It is close to the least surprising possible outcome, and any account that treats it as remarkable is not counting the alternatives. Had he produced a kitchen, that would have been strange.

The rope is a better candidate for something, but only slightly. Rope is strongly associated with water in the ordinary furniture of the imagination — boats, harbours, every film about the sea anyone has ever watched — and a man reaching for water imagery will reach past it.

The wet rope swollen around the wrist so it cannot be removed is more specific, and I notice that I want it to be more specific than it is. It is the kind of detail that feels like it came from somewhere. It may have come from a documentary he saw at fourteen. He would not know; nobody knows the contents of their own forgotten sources, which is exactly what makes that explanation so hard to argue with and so easy to over-apply.

And the two minutes? A man in a suggestible state, approaching the edge of a fear he has organised his life around, becomes very frightened. There is nothing there that requires anything unusual at all.

I am not saying these explanations are correct. I am saying that they cover most of this session comfortably, that I cannot rule any of them out, and that a writer who skipped this section would be presenting Douglas as stronger evidence than he is.

## What this one is for

It is in the book as the honest version of the most commercially attractive story in this field: the phobia that lifts.

The story as usually told has a mechanism in it. A man is frightened of water; under hypnosis he discovers he drowned; the discovery resolves the fear. Three steps, all of them satisfying, and the middle one is an assertion about the world that nobody present can support.

What actually happened has the first step and the third step and a hole where the middle should be. Douglas described a rope and some water and was badly frightened for two minutes, and some months later he could go in a swimming pool. Between those two facts there is no mechanism I can give you. I do not know whether he drowned, whether anyone drowned, whether the material was about drowning at all.

The fear moved, as far as he and the people around him can tell. That part is real, it is his, and it is the only thing in this chapter I would defend.
