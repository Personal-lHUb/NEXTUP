# What Gets Written Down, and What Gets Lost

Every account of this work rests on a record of some kind, and few of the books about it that I have read tell you what the record is. You are handed a session as though it had arrived intact, when in fact it has been through at least three transformations before it reaches the page, each one lossy, each one with its own characteristic way of going wrong.

Since I am asking you to take these accounts seriously, you are owed the provenance.

## The three records, and what each one is good for

There is a recorder. It sits on the windowsill, it is switched on before the client sits down, and it is switched off when they stand up. A client who does not want it running gets a session without it; about one in twenty asks, and I never ask why.

The recording is the only part of the record that does not degrade, and it is a great deal less useful than people assume. Speech in that chair is slow, quiet, full of half-words and long gaps. An hour of session is perhaps two thousand words of actual speech, much of it fragmentary. Listening back takes the full hour and yields less than you expect, because the recorder captures what was said and nothing about what was happening — the hand that opened, the moment the breathing changed, the fact that she had been still for four minutes before she spoke.

So there is also a notebook. I write in it during the session, badly, without looking at it. It is the only place the non-verbal record exists, and it is where the timestamps come from: a line down the margin every time I glance at the clock, so that when I listen back I know which four minutes of silence were the significant ones.

And there is the third record, which is my memory, and which I trust least of the three and cannot do without. Memory is where the shape lives — the sense of a session as a thing with a movement in it. It is also, reliably, wrong about details, and wrong in a consistent direction: it tidies. Every time I have checked a remembered session against the recording I have found that my memory had smoothed the hesitations, moved the best line earlier, and supplied a resolution the session did not have.

That last tendency is worth dwelling on, because it is not a personal failing. It is what memory does to any story, and it means that a practitioner writing up a session from memory five years later is not reporting the session. They are reporting the fourth or fifth telling of it. I suspect a good deal of this field's literature is exactly that, written in good faith, and its details cannot be relied on for reasons that have nothing to do with hypnosis.

## What I change, and why

Names are changed. Jobs are changed. Towns are changed, or removed. Ages of children are moved by a year or two. The month of a bereavement is moved. If a client has an unusual illness, it becomes a common one. If a client has an unusual job, they get a common one.

The reason is simple and I would rather state it plainly than dress it up as ethics. People tell you things in that chair they have not told their husbands. They cry in front of a stranger about a child. Some of them are dealing with something they would lose work over, or custody over. A woman whose sister would recognise her from a book has not consented to being in it, whatever she signed.

Consent, since I have raised it: everyone I write about is told when they book that I write about this work, and is asked afterwards, in writing, having read what I intend to write, with the changes already made. Two people declined and are not here. One agreed and then withdrew a year later, and that account came out of the manuscript, which was annoying and correct.

There is one category I never touch in my notes, and the whole value of a write-up depends on it.

## The detail that never moves

If a session contains something checkable, it goes in exactly as it came.

A date given by a client is the date she gave. A name is spelled the way it sounded, with a note if I am guessing at the spelling. A place name is the place name, not a nearby one. An object described in detail is described in the same detail. A number is the number.

This is not a small commitment and it constrains everything else. It means I cannot move a session from one county to another for anonymity if the client gave a place name, because moving it would falsify the only part a reader could test. In practice, when the two obligations collide, the checkable detail wins and the account either runs with thinner anonymity — with the client's specific agreement — or does not run at all. I have kept more than one account out of print for exactly that reason, and there is one place later in this book where I break the rule, for a reason I give there.

I want to be clear about what this rule does and does not achieve. It does not make the sessions verifiable; most of them contain nothing to verify. What it achieves is narrower and, I think, more important: it means the honest part of a write-up is actually honest. If I were prepared to nudge a date for narrative convenience, then every date in here would be worthless, including the ones I did not nudge, because you would have no way of knowing which was which. A single permitted exception destroys the whole category.

## The composites

Some accounts in this genre combine material from more than one session, or from more than one person, and present the result as a single afternoon. In the accounts I have read it is rarely declared, and that has a specific and corrosive effect: the reader assumes that any given account is one afternoon, and so assumes a density of remarkable material that no single afternoon produces. The genre's characteristic session — vivid, continuous, resolving, full of specifics — is, I suspect, partly an artefact of undeclared composition. Real sessions are patchier than that.

The accounts in this book are composed, as the note at the front says, so this is a distortion I could very easily have added to. What I have done instead is keep the density of an afternoon. The long stretches where nothing arrives are left in, the material thins out where sessions thin out, and no account is given more than a single afternoon produces.

## What to ask of anybody's record

You can apply all of this to any account of this work, including the ones that do not volunteer it, and the questions are short.

Was there a recording, or is this from notes? If from notes, were they written during, immediately after, or later? "Later" is the answer for a good deal of the field's material, and where it is, it should change how much weight you give to any specific detail — not to the general shape of the account, which is probably reliable, but to the exact wording of the exact sentence that everything turns on.

How long between the session and the writing? A year is a different document from a week. The difference is not honesty, it is smoothing, and smoothing runs in one direction: towards coherence, towards resolution, towards the version that makes a better paragraph.

Are the client's words distinguished from the writer's? In a well-made account you can always tell. There are quotation marks, or a change of typeface, or at minimum a consistent *she said*. In a poorly made one the two voices merge into a single flowing narrative, and by the third page you have no idea which of the vivid details came out of a person's mouth and which were supplied to join them up.

And has anything been combined? You will rarely get an answer, because the question is rarely asked. But you can sometimes tell from the density: an account in which every minute produces usable material is not one afternoon, because afternoons are not like that.

None of these questions is hostile, and none of them requires you to doubt the writer's good faith. They are the same questions you would ask about any second-hand account of anything — a court case, a war, a family argument. The field has simply got out of the habit of being asked.

## What is lost

Three things go, and they go in the gap between the room and the page.

The first is pace. A session runs at about a fifth of the speed of conversation. There are gaps of forty seconds between a question and an answer, routinely, and the gaps are not empty — you can watch someone working in them. On the page, forty seconds of silence is a paragraph break, which takes a tenth of a second to read. Everything in this book reads roughly five times faster than it happened, and the compression makes the material seem more fluent and more confident than it was.

The second is the voice. People do not sound like themselves in that chair. They speak more slowly, often more simply, sometimes with an odd flatness, and now and then with a shift of register that is genuinely disconcerting to sit next to. None of that survives transcription. What reaches you is words on a page in a uniform typeface, and the single most striking feature of the material — that a person is talking in a way you have not heard them talk — is invisible.

The third is the breath before the answer. I have come to think this is the most informative thing in a session and there is no way at all to put it on paper. There is a difference between a client who answers immediately, a client who breathes in and then answers, and a client who breathes in, holds it, and then says she does not know. Sitting there, those are three completely different events. Written down, they are all just an answer.

I have tried to compensate by saying so when it mattered — *after a long pause*, *without hesitation* — and it is a poor substitute. When you read the sessions in the middle of this book, slow them down in your head, and put four times as much silence in as the punctuation suggests. You will be closer to what happened than the prose can get you.
