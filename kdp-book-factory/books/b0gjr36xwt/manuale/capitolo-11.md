# The Delayed Scan and the Bed That Never Opens

## A scanner out of service

The scenario that follows is constructed. Bianca Ellison spent eight years as an oncology nurse and has now worked seven months as an NP in the emergency department of a community hospital with one CT scanner. At 09:25 on a Friday she sees Victor Langley, sixty-seven, who has had cramping abdominal pain since 06:00. He has vomited four times and has passed no stool or gas since the night before. In his thirties he had open surgery for a ruptured appendix. His abdomen is distended and tender all over, without guarding or rebound.

Bianca suspects a small bowel obstruction. At 09:40 she orders blood work and a CT of the abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast, and starts IV fluids, an antiemetic and morphine. At 09:55 the CT technologist calls: the scanner is out of service, and the repair should be done by noon. It is back at 12:40.

The scan, read at 14:05, shows an obstruction without signs that the bowel is losing its blood supply, and the general surgeon recommends managing it without an operation. The hospitalist accepts Mr. Langley at 15:20, but there is no medical bed. In this hospital, by policy, the emergency clinician keeps writing orders for an admitted patient until a bed is assigned. His is assigned at 20:35.

## What the record will be asked

The order and the scan each carry a timestamp, so the gap between them is on the timeline before anyone writes a word. The questions start there. What did you know about the delay, from whom and when, and what estimate were you given? What did you do in the meantime, for the condition you suspected rather than the one the scan later confirmed? And how often did you look again, and what did you find each time?

Behind them sits the standard-of-care question from the chapter on what a chart becomes after a bad outcome, asked now of the wait itself. Waiting is a decision whenever another path exists, such as a scanner elsewhere or a surgeon at the bedside before the images. A reviewer will ask whether a reasonable NP, knowing what you knew at each hour, would have kept waiting. *Boarding*, holding an admitted patient in the emergency department because no inpatient bed is free, adds one more question: who was responsible for him while he waited? Your organization's policy decides; the note says what it decided.

## The note as written

At 20:40, with the bed assigned, Bianca writes:

> 67M abd pain, vomiting, prior abd surgery. CT delayed, scanner down. CT: SBO, no ischemia. Surgery saw, recs nonop mgmt, NGT. Admitted to hospitalist, boarding in ED awaiting bed. Pt resting comfortably, VSS. Continued to monitor.

Nothing in it is false, yet more than eleven hours fit into one clause and two reassurances. "CT delayed, scanner down" does not say when Bianca learned it, what she was told or whether she looked for another way to get the scan. "Resting comfortably" and "VSS," for vital signs stable, carry no time, and the nursing flowsheet shows a heart rate of 112 at 12:20; a reviewer will look for her response to that number. "Continued to monitor" gives no interval, no findings and no limit that would have ended the wait. Had his bowel perforated at 18:00, nothing here would show that anyone examined him between the scan and the bed.

## The rewrite

Here is the same day as it could have been written, one timed entry at a time:

> 67M, cramping abdominal pain since 06:00, vomiting x4, no stool or flatus since last night. Open surgery for ruptured appendix, age 34. Seen 09:25: T 98.9°F, HR 98, BP 138/84. Abdomen distended, diffusely tender, no guarding or rebound. Assessment: suspected small bowel obstruction, likely adhesive; compromised bowel not excluded. Plan: NPO, IV fluids, ondansetron, morphine; labs and CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast ordered 09:40.
>
> 09:55. CT technologist reports scanner out of service, repair expected by 12:00. Nearest other CT 40 min by ambulance; transfer for imaging would not be faster than the repair estimate. 10:10 Dr. Marisol Gaines, general surgery, told by phone of suspected SBO and CT delay; will see after CT, sooner if called. RN Theo Chandler to call me for HR above 110, systolic BP below 100, T 100.4°F or higher, worsening pain or more vomiting. Will call Dr. Gaines for guarding or rebound, constant pain or fever.
>
> 11:00. Pain 4/10 after morphine 4 mg IV at 10:05; no further vomiting. HR 92, BP 132/80. Abdomen unchanged. WBC 11.8, lactate 1.4. Continue to wait for CT.
>
> 12:25. Called by RN for HR 112, pain 8/10. Pain still cramping and intermittent. Abdomen unchanged, no guarding or rebound; T 99.1°F. Morphine 4 mg IV 12:30; at 12:50 HR 101, pain 4/10. Scanner back in service 12:40, one trauma scan ahead of him. Continue to wait.
>
> 14:05. CT: small bowel obstruction, transition point in right lower quadrant; no sign of ischemia, no free air. 14:40 seen by Dr. Gaines: nonoperative management, NG tube, surgery to follow. NG placed 14:55, 400 mL bilious output.
>
> 15:20. Accepted by Dr. Naomi Harrow, hospitalist. No medical bed; house supervisor: third in line, no estimate. Per hospital policy, ED orders and reassessment continue until a bed is assigned; RN call limits as at 09:55. Patient and wife told: admitted, no bed or estimate yet.
>
> 17:15. HR 88, pain 2/10. Abdomen softer, less distended. NG 300 mL since placement. Plan unchanged.
>
> 19:10. HR 86, pain 2/10. Abdomen soft, mildly tender. NG 150 mL since 17:15. Plan unchanged.
>
> 20:35. Bed assigned. 20:40 report by phone to Dr. Harrow: course, NG output, exam at 19:10.

## Times, findings and decisions

Each reassessment has the same three parts: a time, the findings that bear on the danger named at 09:25, and a decision. The findings are chosen, not complete: at 17:15 Bianca records the pulse, the pain, the abdomen and the tube output, which are what would show the bowel getting worse. The decision is written even when it is to keep waiting, because "continue to wait" can be judged against the findings beside it, and "continued to monitor" cannot.

The 09:55 entry does the most work. It records the delay as a fact, with its source, its time and its estimate. It shows the alternative Bianca weighed and the surgeon who now knew. And it turns the sentence about what would change your mind, from the chapter on what you don't know yet, into limits set in advance, with a nurse to watch for them. At 12:25 the limits work as intended: the nurse calls when the pulse crosses one, Bianca comes, and the entry shows why she does not yet call the surgeon.

How often to look again is your clinical judgment, set by how fast the danger you named could move; a reviewer will set your entries beside the flowsheet and look at what happened between them. An entry written after the fact is labeled as the chapter on templates and the late addendum describes. "Scanner out of service, repair expected by 12:00" belongs to the timeline; a remark about radiology is commentary, which the chapter on facts and quotes keeps out of the chart.

## The transfer that stalls

The second scenario is also constructed. Corinne Abbott, a former flight nurse, is in her second year as a hospital medicine NP at a forty-bed rural hospital. Elaine Burke, fifty-eight, was admitted on Sunday with right upper abdominal pain and jaundice. Her ultrasound shows a dilated bile duct, her bilirubin is rising, and the plan is an ERCP, an endoscopic procedure to clear the duct, which this hospital does not offer. By Monday evening the chart could say "Accepted at Regional, awaiting bed. Pt and family aware." Some of Corinne's entries show what that line would hide:

> 10:15. Transfer requested for ERCP, not available here. 10:50 accepted by Dr. Isaac Farrow, gastroenterology, Regional Medical Center, pending bed; no estimate. 13:40 Regional: no bed yet. University Hospital called as an alternative: no bed.
>
> 14:00. T 98.8°F, HR 84, BP 126/78, alert. RUQ tenderness unchanged. Bilirubin 4.1 (3.6 at 05:00). Waiting remains reasonable; reassess every 2 h. For fever, rigors, systolic BP below 100 or confusion: blood cultures, antibiotics, call Dr. Farrow about priority and air transport. RN to call me for any of these. If no bed by 20:00, will call both centers again.
>
> 16:30. Patient and daughter Megan Burke told: accepted, no bed yet, checks every 2 h; tell her nurse at once for fever, shaking chills, feeling faint or confusion. Megan asked, "What if she gets worse while she waits?" Told: antibiotics here and a call to Regional about moving her sooner.
>
> 18:20. Bed assigned. Ground ambulance earliest 22:00 per dispatch, crew on another transfer. 18:35 Dr. Farrow by phone: air transport not needed while stable. Patient told 18:45; she said, "Fine, as long as I get there tonight."

Acceptance is not a bed. Who accepted, when, and what the acceptance is pending are separate facts, and the bed gets its own time when it appears. Transport is a second delay, with its own source and estimate, and how she travels is a decision; Dr. Farrow's view is recorded as his, with the time he gave it.

The alternatives, a second center and air transport, each have an answer, so a reviewer asking whether there was a faster route finds that Corinne looked for one. If Mrs. Burke worsens on the road, Megan will be asked what the hospital told her, and the 16:30 entry is the record's side of that answer. The 14:00 entry also holds the plan for the bed that never opens: an hour at which Corinne stops waiting and calls again, written before anyone knows it will be needed, like the plan for the unreachable patient in the chapter on closing the loop on the abnormal result.

## What the note must contain

- **The delay as a fact.** What is delayed, who told you and when, the estimate and each change to it, with no comment on the department or facility.
- **The interim plan and its owner.** What you did for the condition you suspected, and who was responsible for the patient while they waited.
- **The limits that end the wait.** The findings that would make you stop waiting, who watches for them and what you will do.
- **Each reassessment.** Its time, the findings that bear on the danger and the decision, including the decision to keep waiting.
- **For a transfer.** The accepting facility and clinician, when they accepted and what it is pending, transport and its estimate, the alternatives considered, what the patient and family were told, and when you will act if the bed does not open.
