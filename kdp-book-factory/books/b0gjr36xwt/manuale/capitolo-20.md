# Handoffs, Cross-Cover, and the Night Call

## A pressure of 84 at 02:10

Thirty-eight patients become Claire Yamada's at 19:00 on a Tuesday, five months into her work as a night hospital medicine NP, and she has never met any of them. One is Leonard Vogel, seventy-two, admitted at 14:30 by the day NP, Deepa Natarajan, with right pyelonephritis. The night is invented, and so is everyone in it.

His sign-out takes ninety seconds. Ceftriaxone has run since 13:10, and blood cultures from the emergency department are pending. His blood pressure, Deepa says, is "a little soft, 104 over 62 at five, after a liter in the ED. Probably dry. I'm holding his lisinopril." A repeat lactate is due at 21:00; the first, at 12:50, was 2.4. "If his systolic drops under 90, give him 500 of LR and recheck." Claire writes it on her list.

At 21:40 she sees the repeat lactate: 1.6. At 02:10 his nurse, Ruby Whelan, pages her: blood pressure 84/50, temperature 101.3°F, and "he's sleepier than he was at midnight." Claire is at the bedside at 02:20. Before ordering anything, she opens his medication record and finds lisinopril 20 mg, his usual evening dose, given at 21:00; the order to continue it was still active. At 02:45 she calls Dr. Kofi Mensah, the night hospitalist.

## What the record will be asked

A clinician on *cross-cover*, caring overnight for patients another team admitted, inherits them without the reasoning behind the plan, and a reviewer knows it. Suppose that on Thursday Mr. Vogel is in intensive care with septic shock. The night's entries will be read against one question in two parts: what did Claire know, and what did she do with it? Some of what she knew she was told at 19:00, and some she could have found in the chart, and a reviewer will want to know which was which: a sign-out can be wrong, and relying on one without looking is judged differently from checking.

The second part asks whether the decisions after the page were hers: did she see the patient, form her own view of a man who had changed since the day team saw him, and decide whether their plan still fit? Behind both parts sits a question of ownership: who was responsible for each pending item, and when did it pass from one clinician to the next? Most of a handoff is spoken; the chart holds the answers only if someone wrote them down.

## The note as written

Her 02:55 note, typed between pages, reads:

> 02:55. Cross-cover. Called for hypotension. 72M w/ R pyelo on ceftriaxone, BCx pending. BP soft since admission, lisinopril held. Pt seen, mentating. Hypotension likely sepsis vs meds. 1 L LR bolus, labs. Dr. Mensah aware. Will monitor. Day team to f/u in AM.

Almost nothing in it is Claire's. "BP soft since admission, lisinopril held" is the sign-out in her voice, with no values and no source. It fits the day team's plan and the order Claire stopped at 02:30, but not the 21:00 dose, and beside a medication record that shows the dose, it reads like a clinician who never looked.

"Pt seen, mentating" has no time and no findings, so the nurse's "sleepier" goes unanswered. "Sepsis vs meds" names two causes without saying which she favored, or why the bolus was twice the size the sign-out planned. "Dr. Mensah aware" does not say when, of what, or whether he saw the patient. "Will monitor" sets no limit, and "day team to f/u" hands nothing to anyone.

## What you were told, and what you accepted

The written part of a handoff is the sign-out, a list or an electronic tool that in many hospitals sits outside the medical record, is updated every shift and is sometimes discarded at the end of it. Whether yours is kept, and for how long, is a question for your risk manager. Either way, do not count on it to show later what you heard.

So when a decision of yours rests on something you were told, put it in your note with its source and its time, as the rewrite does with "Per 19:00 sign-out." It is the attribution the chapter on facts and quotes asks of a nurse's report. You do not need a note for every patient on the list. You need one when you act, and when what you were told shapes what you do.

Pending items need the same care, because a handoff moves ownership along with information. An item you accept at 19:00, such as the repeat lactate, is yours until you close it or hand it back by name, as a result belonged to its owner in the chapter on closing the loop on the abnormal result. The 21:40 entry is short, and it shows the item received, seen and closed. The same holds in clinic: a result that posts while you cover a colleague's inbox is yours, and the addendum carries your name.

## Your assessment, not the sign-out's

The sign-out carries the day team's working diagnosis, and as the chapter on what you don't know yet put it, a working diagnosis has a date on it. "Probably dry" described a man at 17:00. The man Claire saw at 02:20 had a fever, slow answers and a dose of lisinopril nobody had planned. Recorded as what you were told, the day team's view is a fact. Carried into your assessment, "per day team" or not, it is a judgment you did not make about a man who has since changed, with your signature under it.

The if-then plan in the sign-out deserves the same look. "Give him 500 of LR" was advice written before the event, by someone picturing it. When the event comes, decide whether the advice still fits, and write which it was: followed, changed or set aside, with the reason in a few words. The last line of Claire's assessment does that, and it turns a bolus twice the planned size from a deviation into a decision.

Keep the note brief and timed. The background is in the admission note, so point to it rather than retell it, and spend the words on what only you can supply: what you found, what you checked and what you made of it. The alternatives fit the short form from the chapter on the differential. Where the sign-out and the chart disagree, write both as facts, with no word on whose error it was. If the discrepancy needs review, it goes through your organization's reporting channel, not the chart, as the chapter on the consultant who never comes advised.

## Handing the patient back

At 07:05 the handoff runs the other way. What Deepa Natarajan hears is not in the chart unless Claire puts it there, so at 07:10 she adds one entry:

> 07:10. Cross-cover summary for day team. Hypotension 02:10, see 02:55 note. After LR 1 L: SBP 102–112 since 03:25, 108/64 at 06:15, HR 86; alert and oriented at 05:05 reassessment. Lactate 1.5 at 05:00. Changed overnight: lisinopril order discontinued. Pending: ED blood cultures. Open for day team: whether to restart lisinopril, and antibiotic choice when cultures result. RN call limits from 02:55 note still in force. Verbal handoff to D. Natarajan, NP, 07:05.

The entry tells Deepa what changed, what is still waiting and which decisions are now hers, so nothing rests on a conversation in a hallway. A reviewer will look there for the moment each open item passed to a named clinician. Deepa's own note starts from her own exam: copying the night entries forward would put her signature under findings she did not make, the trap described in the chapter on templates and the late addendum.

## What the note must contain

- **What you were told.** The source and time of the sign-out, and the items your decisions rest on.
- **The pending items.** Each item accepted, its result and what you did, or the clinician you handed it back to.
- **The call.** Who called, when, and what they reported.
- **Your own look.** When you saw the patient, the findings that bear on the problem, what you checked in the chart, and any disagreement with the sign-out, as facts.
- **Your assessment.** The working diagnosis and alternatives in your words, and whether the inherited plan still fit.
- **What you did and whom you told.** Orders with times; the physician you called, when, whether they saw the patient and what they advised.
- **The limits and the next look.** What the nurse will call you for, and when you will see the patient again.
- **The handback.** What changed overnight, what is pending, which decisions now belong to the day team, the limits still in force, and who took over and when.
