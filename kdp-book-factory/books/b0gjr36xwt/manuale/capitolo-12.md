# The Consultant Who Never Comes

## Two pages and a cold foot

The scenario that follows is constructed. Jasmine Holloway spent eight years as a cardiac catheterization lab nurse and has now worked three months as a hospital medicine NP. Gloria Ainsworth, seventy-six, was admitted on Tuesday with pneumonia. She has peripheral artery disease and a stent in the main artery of her left thigh. At 13:50 on Thursday her nurse, Diego Fuentes, calls Jasmine: the left foot is cold, and Mrs. Ainsworth says it has hurt since lunch.

At 14:00 the foot is pale and cool to the ankle, with no pulse Jasmine can feel and only a faint signal on Doppler; sensation and movement are intact. She suspects the stent has closed, orders heparin and at 14:05 pages the vascular surgeon on call, Dr. Stefan Kirby. No call comes back. At 14:20 she pages him again, and at 14:25 she calls Dr. Omar Bashir, the attending hospitalist on her service.

By 14:40 the toes are numb. Dr. Bashir learns from the operating room desk that Dr. Kirby is scrubbed in a case, and through the house supervisor he reaches the chief of surgery, Dr. Helen Eastman, who is at the bedside at 15:25. Dr. Kirby never comes. Nobody need have failed for that to happen, and that is what makes the note hard to write.

## What the record will be asked

If Mrs. Ainsworth loses her leg, a reviewer will spend longest on the eighty minutes between the first page and a surgeon at the bedside. Who was called, and when? What did each call say, and what did it ask? What came back, and what was done while nothing did? And when the silence went on, who decided it had lasted long enough, and what did they do?

These questions turn the treatment allegation from the chapter on where claims begin, "a consultant not called, a worsening patient not escalated," into a timeline. Asking for a consult does not hand the patient over: until the specialist arrives, her care rests with her own team, and the reviewer will look to their notes for the time in between. Paging systems often log each page with its time and number; only your note shows what you did when nobody answered.

## The note as written

At 16:40, with Mrs. Ainsworth in the angiography suite, Jasmine writes:

> 76F PAD s/p L SFA stent, L foot cold and painful this afternoon, concern for acute limb ischemia. Heparin started. Vascular paged multiple times without response; attending aware. Escalated per policy. Eventually seen by surgery, to IR for angiogram.

Nothing in it is false, and almost none of it can be checked. "Paged multiple times" gives no times and no content: a reader cannot tell how far apart the pages came or what they said. "Attending aware" hides that Dr. Bashir came to the bedside, examined the patient and made the next calls himself. "Escalated per policy" names no person and no time. And the numb toes at 14:40, the finding that made waiting unsafe, are missing, so the escalation has no reason on the page.

"Without response" and "eventually" are not findings but complaints. They set Jasmine's note against Dr. Kirby, who may answer that he was in an emergency operation and returned the page as soon as he could: the argument between clinicians that the chapter on facts and quotes warned against.

## The rewrite

The same afternoon, written as it happened:

> 14:00. Called by RN Diego Fuentes 13:50 for cold, painful L foot. 76F admitted Tues with pneumonia; PAD, L superficial femoral artery stent 2 yrs ago. L foot pain since ~13:15, 7/10. L foot pale and cool to ankle, capillary refill 5 s; no palpable DP or PT pulse; Doppler: faint PT signal, no DP signal. R foot warm, pulses palpable. Light touch and toe movement intact L. Assessment: suspected acute L limb ischemia, possible stent occlusion. Plan: heparin per protocol, bolus 14:15; NPO; urgent vascular surgery consult. RN to check foot q15 min, call me at once for numbness, weakness or color change.
>
> 14:05. Paged Dr. Stefan Kirby, vascular surgery on call per operator: "Urgent. 76F, 4 East bed 12, cold pulseless L foot since 13:15, prior SFA stent, heparin ordered. Need evaluation for revascularization. Call NP Holloway x4417."
>
> 14:20. No callback. Operator confirmed Dr. Kirby on call, same pager; second page, same text. 14:25 called Dr. Omar Bashir, attending hospitalist: gave exam, onset, heparin and both pages. He is coming to bedside.
>
> 14:40. Reassessed with Dr. Bashir, who examined her. New numbness L toes; light touch reduced over toes, movement intact. PT Doppler signal now absent. Worse than at 14:00; heparin running since 14:15. Decided with Dr. Bashir not to wait for a callback.
>
> 14:45. Dr. Bashir called OR front desk: Dr. Kirby scrubbed in a case, expected to finish ~16:30; message left with circulating RN. 14:50 Dr. Bashir called house supervisor Lydia Coates to reach a second vascular surgeon.
>
> 15:05. Dr. Helen Eastman, chief of surgery, by phone with Dr. Bashir and me; told of 14:00 exam and 14:40 change. Has not seen patient. Her recommendations, read back: continue heparin, keep NPO. Will be at bedside by 15:30.
>
> 15:25. Dr. Eastman at bedside, examined patient. Her plan: angiography with possible intervention now; consent obtained by her. To angiography 16:10.
>
> 16:35. Dr. Kirby returned page from OR; told of Dr. Eastman's plan. Plan unchanged.

## The call log inside the note

Each call in the rewrite has four parts: the time, who was called and by what route, what was said, and what came back. A page can go astray, and one sent to the wrong number looks, from your end, exactly like a page ignored. "Operator confirmed Dr. Kirby on call, same pager" shows that Jasmine checked before she paged again.

Most notes leave out the text of the page, which does the most work. "Please call 4 East" gives whoever reads it no way to judge urgency. Jasmine's message gives the finding, its onset and the question, so it shows that the urgency traveled with the request. Not every paging system keeps the text, so put the words in your note.

Answers get the same care. "No callback" is an answer, written with the time you noted it. A phone recommendation is recorded as the consultant's, with name and time, read back and matched by your orders. The note also says whether the consultant has seen the patient, as the chapter on the provider note asked of an attending's role: at 15:05 Dr. Eastman's advice rests on Jasmine's report, and at 15:25 the plan is hers, made at the bedside.

Between the calls, the entries about Mrs. Ainsworth herself follow the method of the chapter on the delayed scan: a time, the findings that bear on the danger, a decision. Here each reassessment carries one more decision, whether to escalate. The 14:40 entry is that decision on the record, and it ties every call after it to a limb that was getting worse.

## Escalation, step by step

The first step is the repeat page. How long to wait before it is a clinical judgment set by how fast the danger can move; some hospitals also set an expected response time for on-call consultants, so find out whether yours does. With the repeat, try any other route the schedule lists, such as a backup number or the operating room desk.

The second step is the physician you work with. Whether Dr. Bashir is Jasmine's collaborating or supervising physician, or simply the attending on her service, depends on her state and her employer, as the chapter on the provider note explained. Call that physician when the finding worsens, when waiting could cost the patient something, or when the next step is one your policy reserves for a physician. The trigger is the patient, not a number of unanswered pages. On the record the call is a name, a time, what you told them and what they did.

The third step is the **chain of command**: the route your organization sets for taking a clinical concern past the people who have not resolved it. Who comes next differs between organizations; a common path runs through the house supervisor or the administrator on call to the chief of the consultant's department, then to the chief medical officer. Read your policy before you need it. Use it when the consultant cannot be reached in a time the patient can afford, or when a consultant's answer leaves the patient at risk and a direct conversation has not changed it. In the note each step is a role, a name, a time, what was asked and the answer.

Had Dr. Kirby called back at 14:30 and said he would see Mrs. Ainsworth after his last case, the note would carry that plan as his, with its time, then the numb toes at 14:40, the call telling him of them, his answer and what Jasmine and Dr. Bashir did next. If no one with the needed skill can come, the escalation becomes a transfer, worked in the chapter on the delayed scan and the bed that never opens.

## A time and an action

The chapter on facts and quotes already settles the hardest line in this note: facts with times, and no account of anyone else's failure. An unanswered page is a time, a fact and your next step, as at 14:20. "Despite multiple pages," "unresponsive," "failed to call back" and "finally" turn the same facts into a complaint no reader can check against the log. The callback, when it comes, is written as plainly: the 16:35 entry has no "finally." Leave out reasons you were not given, such as "probably in surgery"; one you were given goes in with its source, as at 14:45. If the delay needs review, raise it with your manager or your organization's reporting channel, not in the chart.

## What the note must contain

- **The trigger.** The finding that made the consult urgent, with its time and onset.
- **Each call.** The time, the person or role, the route, and the message or question as sent.
- **Each answer.** The reply or its absence, with the time and with no adjective or guessed reason; a phone recommendation as the consultant's, read back, with whether they had seen the patient.
- **The interim care.** What you did while waiting, and each reassessment with its findings and its decision, including the decision to escalate.
- **Each escalation step.** The physician you work with and each chain-of-command contact: name, role, time, what you told or asked them, and what they did.
- **The handover.** Who took over, when, and with what plan.
