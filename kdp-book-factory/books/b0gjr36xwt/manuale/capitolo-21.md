# The Telehealth Visit and the Portal Message

## A peak flow held up to the camera

The scenario that follows is constructed. Maya Thibodeaux spent seven years as an outpatient infusion nurse and has now worked ten months as an NP at Northgate Primary Care, where Thursday afternoons are video visits. At 14:40 she connects with Colleen Pacheco, forty-six, who has had asthma since childhood. A cold began on Saturday; since Monday she has been wheezing and short of breath, and since Tuesday she has needed her albuterol inhaler every four hours.

Maya cannot listen to her lungs, so she asks for what a camera can show. Ms. Pacheco talks in full sentences while she walks to the kitchen and back, lowers her collar, holds her pulse oximeter up to the lens, blows three times into her peak flow meter and turns the laptop toward her calves. At 14:58 Maya decides on home treatment and sets the point at which Ms. Pacheco must be seen in person.

## What the record will be asked

Suppose that on Saturday Ms. Pacheco reaches the emergency department with pneumonia. Beyond what is asked of any visit, a reviewer will ask three things of this one. What could Maya assess through a camera, and what could she not? Which findings came from her own eyes, which from the patient's devices and which from the patient's account? And given what the screen could not show, why was remote care reasonable, and what would have brought her in?

The chapter on what a chart becomes after a bad outcome showed that the setting counts toward the standard of care, but only the setting the reader can see. A video visit is part of the circumstances, and it is also a choice. The record has to show that it was chosen for reasons, not inherited from the way the visit was booked.

## The note as written

At 15:10, with the next video visit waiting, Maya writes:

> Telehealth visit. 46F hx asthma, SOB and wheeze x 3 days after URI, albuterol q4h. Gen: NAD. Resp: no respiratory distress, lungs clear to auscultation bilaterally. SpO2 95%. A: asthma exacerbation. P: prednisone 40 mg daily x 5 days, continue albuterol. ED if worse.

Every line but one is true, and Maya never typed that one. "Lungs clear to auscultation" came from the clinic's default exam. It is the finding nobody could have made, from the chapter on templates and the late addendum, and it costs the rest of the exam its weight, "NAD" included. "Telehealth" does not say whether there was a picture or only a voice, and "SpO2 95%" has no device and no source. "ED if worse" gives a woman with a peak flow meter on her counter no number to watch. Nor does anything say an in-person exam was weighed, so the lungs nobody heard read as an oversight rather than a limit.

## The rewrite

Here is the same visit, written from what Maya saw, heard and decided:

> Video visit, audio and video throughout, 14:40–14:58. Patient at home; address on file confirmed for emergency use. Identity confirmed by name, date of birth and chart photo. Alone. Telehealth consent per clinic process, confirmed at start.
>
> Reported: cold since Sat; wheeze, chest tightness and shortness of breath since Mon; albuterol q4h since Tue, relief ~3 h. Home temp 99.0°F this morning. No chest pain, leg pain, hormone use or sputum change. Two prior flares, settled at home.
>
> Observed on video: full sentences, also while walking to kitchen and back. RR 22, counted twice over 30 s. No neck muscle use or retractions, collar lowered. Calves symmetric, no visible swelling. Measured on camera, her devices: peak flow 300, best of 3 (personal best 430 per action plan in chart: 70%); pulse oximeter 95%, steady, finger warm. Not assessable by video: lung and heart sounds, temperature, oxygen on exertion.
>
> Assessment: asthma exacerbation after viral URI, like her prior flares. Pneumonia not excluded without auscultation; against it, near-normal temperature, no sputum change. PE: gradual onset with URI, no hormone use, calves symmetric. Remote care reasonable today: full sentences, SpO2 95%, peak flow 70% of best. In-person exam offered today 16:30; she prefers home treatment.
>
> Plan: prednisone 40 mg daily x 5 days; albuterol as before. Peak flow and oximeter at 20:00 and 08:00, readings by portal by 09:00. In-person visit held Fri 10:20, to keep if peak flow below 340, tightness not easing or temperature 100.4°F or higher. ED or 911 for: cannot finish a sentence, blue lips, oximeter below 92%, peak flow below 215, or albuterol relief under 2 h. She said: "Under 340 or a fever, I come in. Under 215, or I can't talk, I go to the ER."

## Observed, measured, reported

The rewrite sorts the remote exam by source. What Maya saw or heard through the connection is *observed*, and it counts as her exam. A number from the patient's own device, read on camera at Maya's request, is *measured*: data with a source, so the note names the device and how the reading was seen. A number the patient only reports is history. And the line on what was not assessable names what the modality ruled out.

That line keeps the assessment honest. Pneumonia stays "not excluded" because nobody heard the lungs, and the plan answers it, as the chapter on what you don't know yet requires: a fever is one of the triggers for the morning visit. The reasons for remote care point to findings already in the note, so a reviewer can test the decision against them. The thresholds open two doors, the clinic for slow progress and the emergency department for danger, each with numbers Ms. Pacheco can read on her own devices. If the video fails partway, the note says when, and what was seen before it.

## Advice by phone and portal

At 08:15 on Friday a message reaches Maya's inbox from Ms. Pacheco's portal account: "Peak flow 320 this morning, feel a little better. Do I still need to come in? I'd have to miss work." A reply that tells a patient what to do is a clinical decision, by phone or by portal, and the record will ask four things of it: who asked, what they asked, what was advised and how the answer reached them.

Who asked is not always the patient: a daughter writing through her father's proxy access receives the advice, and the note names her. Record the question in the note, since a portal thread may not be filed with the chart; ask health information management what your system does. When a nurse or medical assistant sends the reply, the note says whose advice it carries. The method has to fit the urgency. A portal reply can sit unread until evening, and this answer cannot wait:

> 08:45. Portal message 08:15 from patient: peak flow 320, "a little better," asks whether to keep visit. Below in-person threshold of 340 set yesterday. Called cell 08:35, reached patient, identity confirmed. Advised visit today for lung exam; offered 12:40 instead of 10:20, to fit work. She chose 12:40. ED thresholds reviewed, unchanged. Portal reply 08:42 confirming 12:40.

## The call after hours

The second scenario is also constructed. Jonah Soto, a former wound care nurse in his second year as an NP, covers Northgate's after-hours line from home. At 23:02 on a Wednesday, paged by the answering service, he reaches Gail Rinaldi, thirty-nine, on day three of amoxicillin for strep throat, with an itchy rash since 21:00. The answering service keeps a log of its own, but the day team will open the chart, so at 23:20 Jonah writes a short note there:

> After-hours call 23:02–23:12. Caller: patient, identity confirmed by name and date of birth. Chart reviewed remotely: amoxicillin started Mon by Dr. Miriam Voss for strep pharyngitis; no allergies listed. Itchy raised rash since 21:00. No swelling of lips, tongue or throat; no trouble breathing or swallowing; no faintness. By phone: full sentences, voice not hoarse. Assessment: hives, likely reaction to amoxicillin; no features of anaphylaxis. Advised: stop amoxicillin; cetirizine 10 mg tonight. 911 for swelling of lips, tongue or throat, trouble breathing or swallowing, or feeling faint. She repeated: "Stop the pills, take the Zyrtec, 911 if my mouth swells or I can't breathe." Amoxicillin (hives) added to allergy list. For Dr. Voss, inbox 23:20: choose replacement antibiotic, call patient Thu by 10:00. Patient told to expect the call.

The threshold turns the danger the assessment named into signs Ms. Rinaldi can notice herself, as in the chapter on the discharge and the patient who comes back. The last lines are the handoff, built like the handback in the chapter on handoffs, cross-cover and the night call: what changed tonight, which decision the day team now owns, and who acts by when. If you take a call without chart access, write that too.

## Consent, location and identity

The rewrite, the portal entry and the night note all record who was on the other end, and the video rewrite adds where Ms. Pacheco was and that she agreed to be seen remotely. These lines look administrative, and the rules behind them can differ by state and by organization, so this book leaves them as questions. Ask before your first remote visit, not after a complaint.

Ask your organization whether a remote visit needs its own consent, whether a verbal yes is enough and what the note must say about it; what it accepts as proof of identity for a patient you have never met; and whether others in the room must be named. If the patient may be across a state line during a visit, ask your board, and that state's board, whether you may treat her while she is there, and on what terms. Whatever the answers, record where the patient is: if she collapses mid-sentence, the address on file may not be where help is needed.

## What the note must contain

- **The modality.** Video, phone or portal; start and end times; any switch partway, and when.
- **Who, where and consent.** How identity was confirmed, where the patient was, who else was present, and consent as your organization requires.
- **The remote exam, by source.** What you observed, what the patient's devices measured on camera, what was only reported, and what could not be assessed.
- **Why remote was enough.** The findings that made remote care reasonable, and the in-person option offered.
- **The threshold for in-person care.** The signs or readings that bring the patient in, and those that send them to the emergency department.
- **Phone and portal advice.** Who asked, in their words; what was advised and by whom; how the answer was delivered and whether it was received.
- **The after-hours call.** The time, your chart access, the advice, the escalation threshold with the patient's read-back, and who owns the next step by when.
