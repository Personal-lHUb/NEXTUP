# Facts, Quotes, and What Never Goes in the Chart

## A hard morning, written two ways

Raymond Teague, sixty-two, was admitted on Friday with cellulitis of the left lower leg. He has type 2 diabetes. Overnight he pulled out his IV, saying it burned. A new line went in at 05:15, and the cefazolin dose due at 02:00 was given at 05:30. The night nurse filed an incident report about the late dose. At 07:40 on Sunday he is first on the rounding list of Kendra Walsh, ten months into her first job as a hospital medicine NP. Neither of them is real; the case was built for this chapter.

When Kendra walks in, Mr. Teague is sitting up and speaking loudly. He swears several times while describing the night. He says his leg pain is 8 out of 10, that "the pills don't touch it," and asks for IV hydromorphone by name. His fasting glucose is 287; asked about it, he says he stopped metformin about four months ago. Before she leaves he says he is going to get a lawyer. At 08:30, still irritated, she writes:

> Pt belligerent and uncooperative on rounds, cursing at staff. Pulled out IV overnight; IV team didn't come for 3+ hrs (unit short-staffed again), so cefazolin given 3.5 hrs late. Hx noncompliance, off metformin for months. Drug-seeking, demanding IV Dilaudid. Apparently he knows more about his treatment than we do. Incident report filed re: late dose. Pt states he will "get a lawyer." Continue current plan.

Very little of that is false. Mr. Teague was loud, the dose was late, and he did ask for hydromorphone. But almost every line gives a verdict instead of evidence, and several lines are not about the patient at all. Here is the same encounter rewritten, with nothing added that Kendra did not see or hear:

> 07:40, seen at bedside. Sitting up, speaking loudly; swore several times while describing the night. States: "That IV was burning and nobody came, so I pulled it." Per RN Kwame Osei, PIV removed by patient ~01:40, new PIV R forearm 05:15; cefazolin due 02:00 given 05:30. Reports L leg pain 8/10, "the pills don't touch it"; asked for IV hydromorphone by name. Last oxycodone 5 mg at 04:10. States he stopped metformin ~4 months ago: "It tore up my stomach." Fasting glucose 287.
>
> Exam: T 99.1°F, HR 92. L lower leg erythema within the border marked on admission; tenderness limited to the red area; no crepitus, blisters or dusky skin. New IV site clean. Asked him to call his nurse if the IV burns again rather than remove it; he said, "Fine, as long as somebody comes."

The plan is left aside, because the pain request belongs to the chapter on prescribing. What changed is the language. The rewrite keeps the loud voice, the swearing, the removed line, the late dose and the request by name. It drops the verdicts on Mr. Teague's character, the account of who failed overnight, the report, the lawyer and the sarcasm. Where the weak note offered explanations, the rewrite gives times.

## Observations instead of adjectives

An adjective about a patient is a conclusion. "Belligerent" asks the reader to accept your judgment of a man they have never met. "Speaking loudly; swore several times while describing the night" gives them what you saw and heard. Later readers will judge anyway, so let them judge from the facts and not from your mood at 08:30. A simple test separates the two: could someone else in the room have confirmed it? A camera would have recorded the volume and the words. It would not have recorded belligerence.

The test does not ban clinical description. "Diaphoretic," "pale" and "alert" are findings with an accepted meaning, and another clinician could check them. The words to replace are judgments of character, motive or mood: rude, difficult, manipulative, uncooperative, hysterical. "Agitated" becomes "pacing, pulled at the IV line twice, did not sit when asked." "Intoxicated" becomes "odor of alcohol, slurred speech, unsteady gait," followed by a level if you drew one. "Anxious" becomes "tearful, hands shaking."

There is a clinical reason too. Confusion from low blood sugar, low oxygen or infection can look like hostility. "Uncooperative" records your interpretation and erases the finding underneath it. If the patient turns out to have been delirious, a reader who already knows the ending will see that word as the moment the sign was missed.

## Quoting the patient

The patient's own words matter most where they carry the decision. That means a symptom described in terms that change the workup, such as "the worst headache of my life." It means a refusal, a plan to leave, a thought of self-harm or a threat to hurt someone. It means what the patient says back when you check what they understood. And it means the reason a patient gives for something you might otherwise label. "It tore up my stomach" turns Mr. Teague's stopped medication into a side effect, and a side effect has remedies.

Quotation marks make a promise: these exact words were said. Put them only around words you are sure of, and write them down soon, before memory smooths them. If you remember only the gist, write the gist without the marks: *States he stopped metformin because of stomach upset.* Do not correct the grammar or tidy the wording. Name the speaker: a daughter's account is not the patient's, and a nurse's report is not your exam. When an interpreter is used, note it, since the English inside your quotation marks is the interpreter's rendering.

The same marks let you record a complaint about the care without making it yours. "Nobody came," in Mr. Teague's words and attributed to him, is a fact about the encounter. The same claim in your voice would be an accusation. When the words themselves are the clinical fact, as in a threat, quote them exactly, profanity included; when they are not, describe the behavior, as the rewrite does with "swore several times." Quote a phrase or a sentence, not a transcript. A note made mostly of quotations buries your own observations and your reasoning.

## Labels that read badly later

Some words are not descriptions at all but labels: short verdicts that travel. They get copied into later notes and onto the problem list, as the previous chapter warned with "likely stress," and each copy sits further from the facts behind it.

"Noncompliant" says the patient failed without saying at what, for how long or why. A reviewer reads it as the point where the clinician stopped asking. Swapping in "nonadherent" helps less than it seems, because the real problem is the missing facts, not the word. The factual version names the behavior, the patient's reason and what you did about it: "Has not taken metformin for ~4 months; states it caused stomach upset. Discussed extended-release metformin and other options; see plan."

"Drug-seeking" names a motive, and motives cannot be observed. The factual version records the exact request, the recent doses, any checks you ran and your assessment of the pain itself. The label has a clinical cost too. A patient with an infected leg whose pain keeps climbing needs another look at the leg, and a word in the last note that explains the pain away makes that look less likely. Kendra's exam line shows that she looked. "Poor historian" usually describes the conversation rather than the patient; write what you could not obtain and where the information came from instead. "Frequent flyer" belongs nowhere in a record.

Patients have a legal right to their records, and under the federal information-blocking rules in force since 2021, most US providers may not withhold signed notes without a recognized exception, so many notes reach the patient portal as soon as they are signed. A patient who reads "drug-seeking" about himself may stop reporting his pain, or may answer with a complaint to your board. As the chapter on where claims begin noted, a complaint needs no injury behind it.

## What never goes in the chart

The chart is the record of the patient's care. Some things that feel urgent while you write belong somewhere else, and each has a place to go.

Blame of colleagues comes first. "IV team didn't come for 3+ hrs" states a cause Kendra did not witness; the IV nurse may have been at a code. The facts that matter are in the rewrite, each with its time. A note that accuses invites the other clinician's note to answer, and a record in which clinicians argue helps no one defend the care. Writing about a colleague who never responds is worked in the chapter on the consultant who never comes.

Staffing complaints are next. "Short-staffed again" is an opinion about the cause, not a clinical fact. If staffing delayed care, the delay goes in the chart as a fact with its time and its source, the way the rewrite records the late dose. The cause goes to your manager or through your organization's reporting channel, where someone can act on it.

The incident report is an internal document, and organizations generally keep it inside their safety and quality process. How far the law protects it varies, and depends partly on how it is handled. A line in the chart that names the report points every reader of the record toward it. The chart carries the clinical event instead: what happened to the patient, what you found and what you did. The chapter on the adverse event returns to this boundary.

Litigation stays out too, whether as your guess or as the patient's threat. "Pt states he will 'get a lawyer'" changes nothing about the care Mr. Teague needs. Tell your risk manager instead, and ask how your organization wants such statements handled. "Documented for legal purposes" is worse: it tells every reader you were writing for a lawyer rather than for the next clinician.

Last comes sarcasm, along with jokes, exclamation points and your feelings about the encounter. "Apparently he knows more about his treatment than we do" may have been a private sigh at 08:30. Read aloud to a jury, it sounds like contempt, and no explanation given later will change how it sounded.

## Reading your note as a stranger would

Before you sign, give the note one more pass, for tone alone. It takes less than a minute. Read it as three strangers would: the patient opening it in the portal, the colleague named in it, and a juror who has never met any of you. For every adjective about the patient, ask whether you could point to what you saw or heard. Check that each pair of quotation marks holds words actually said, with a speaker. Look for any line that explains someone else's failure, the staffing, a report or a lawyer.

The reread matters most on the notes you write while angry. Kendra's two versions describe the same morning. Much of the distance between them can be covered in that one pass for tone, made before the patient, the colleague or the juror reads a word.
