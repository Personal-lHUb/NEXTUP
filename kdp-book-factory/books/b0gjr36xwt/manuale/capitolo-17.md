# Informed Refusal and the Partial Yes

## Antibiotics yes, needle no

The scenario that follows is constructed. Carmen Vasquez spent seven years as a neuroscience ICU nurse and has now worked ten months as an NP in the emergency department of a county hospital. At 13:25 on a Thursday she sees Derek Sutton, thirty-eight, who has had fever and a headache since the previous evening. Light hurts his eyes, and his neck is stiff. His temperature is 102.6°F and his heart rate 112. He is alert and oriented, with no rash and no focal deficit.

She thinks first of bacterial meningitis and goes straight to Dr. Joanne Whitaker, the ED attending. Blood cultures are drawn at 13:40, and by 13:50 ceftriaxone and vancomycin are running. Dr. Whitaker will do the lumbar puncture, or LP, and at 14:05 Carmen goes back to explain it. Mr. Sutton hears her out, then shakes his head. "I'll take the antibiotics, and I'll stay as long as you want. But nobody's putting a needle in my back."

She talks it through with him and his wife for fifteen minutes, and Dr. Whitaker joins them at 14:10. He does not change his mind. At 14:30 he signs the hospital's refusal form, and at 14:40 Carmen calls the admitting hospitalist.

## What the record will be asked

**Informed consent** is the process in which a clinician explains a proposed test or treatment, its purpose, its main risks and benefits and the reasonable alternatives, and a patient with capacity agrees. **Informed refusal** is the same process ending in no. It puts at the center an element that consent often treats in passing: what could happen if the patient says no. What must be disclosed, and by what legal standard, varies by state; ask your risk manager what applies where you work.

Suppose that on Saturday Mr. Sutton is worse, and the cause turns out to be an infection the antibiotics do not treat. A reviewer will bring one question to the 14:05 conversation, in two parts: did he understand what he was refusing, and did he understand what could happen as a result? Three more questions make the answer believable. Could he make this decision? What alternatives did he hear? And once he said no, what did his clinicians do with the plan that was left?

## A signature is not a conversation

Many organizations have a refusal form, and some policies require it; use it when yours does. It holds a name, a date, a signature and a printed paragraph of risks that fits every patient and every test. It cannot show what Mr. Sutton was told about this test, in what words, or what he said back. It cannot show whether he could make the decision, which alternatives he was offered or what happened next. A signature proves that a pen touched paper, much as an order, in the chapter on what a chart becomes after a bad outcome, proved only that someone touched a keyboard.

The form records a signature; the note records the conversation. A form signed in thirty seconds with nothing explained looks exactly like one signed after fifteen minutes of discussion, and only the note tells them apart. If a patient declines to sign, write that, and who was present. The refusal is no less real, because the record of it was never the form.

## The note as written

At 14:50, with two new patients waiting, Carmen writes:

> 14:50. Pt refuses LP. Risks explained, pt verbalized understanding. Refusal form signed. Abx given. Admit to medicine.

Every line is true, and the note answers neither half of the question. "Risks explained" does not say which risks: those of the needle, or those of going without it. "Pt verbalized understanding" is a conclusion with nothing inside it, the written version of the "Do you understand?" that the chapter on the capacity question warned against. There is no capacity finding and no alternative. "Refuses," standing alone, reads as obstinacy, and the reason he gave is gone. Nor does the plan say how the refusal changed it, or whether anyone means to ask him again.

## The rewrite

Here is the same note, rewritten from what Carmen did and heard:

> 14:50. Lumbar puncture declined; rest of plan accepted. Suspected bacterial meningitis (history and exam above). Blood cultures 13:40; ceftriaxone and vancomycin started 13:50. LP recommended to confirm the diagnosis, identify the organism and guide the choice and length of treatment; Dr. J. Whitaker, ED attending, to perform.
>
> Discussed 14:05–14:20 with patient and his wife, present at his request; Dr. Whitaker from 14:10. Explained in plain words: without the LP we may not learn whether this is bacterial, viral or something else, and could miss an infection that needs a different drug; he may stay on IV antibiotics longer than he needs; the longer antibiotics run, the less likely a later LP is to identify the germ. Risks of LP: headache afterward, sore back, rarely bleeding or infection; the needle goes in below where the spinal cord ends. Options offered: LP now, with medication for anxiety and extra numbing; LP later, knowing it will show less; no LP, with admission and a full course of IV antibiotics as if bacterial.
>
> Patient, 14:05: "I'll take the antibiotics, and I'll stay as long as you want. But nobody's putting a needle in my back." His reason: "My brother-in-law had one, and his back's never been right." Asked what could happen without it: "You can't be sure what bug it is, so I'm on the strong stuff longer, and you might miss something else." Capacity for this decision: alert, oriented; restated purpose and consequences in his own words, applies them to himself, reason consistent, choice unchanged throughout. Has capacity at this time to decline LP.
>
> Plan: admit to medicine; IV antibiotics as above, now planned as a full course. LP order cancelled, reason "declined by patient." Told he may change his mind at any time; RN Luis Navarro to page if he does. Refusal form signed 14:30. 14:40, Dr. Anita Rhodes, admitting hospitalist, told of the refusal and his reason; she will offer the LP again on admission.

## What the conversation has to show

Four elements carry the rewrite. First come the risks, in the words Carmen used. "Risks explained" could cover anything; "could miss an infection that needs a different drug" is the risk a reviewer will test if Saturday goes badly, and the note shows that Mr. Sutton heard it before anyone knew it mattered. Write the risks of refusing for this patient and this test, the most serious first, and the risks of the procedure itself, since a patient deciding about a needle is weighing both.

The alternatives show that the no answered a real offer. Medication for anxiety, extra numbing, an LP later: each was a way the test could still have happened, and each one he declined makes the refusal more clearly his. Going without belongs on the list too, with what replaces the test, because that is the plan he chose.

The patient's own words carry two things: his reason and his account of the consequences. The reason explains a decision that would otherwise look arbitrary. The account is what "verbalized understanding" leaves out, and it answers the second half of the reviewer's question in his voice rather than yours. Quote both by the rules in the chapter on facts and quotes.

The capacity line is short because the method lives elsewhere. The chapter on the capacity question shows how to assess the four abilities for one decision; the refusal note names the decision, states the finding and points to the patient's answers that support it. A bare "has capacity" would not carry a refusal this consequential. When capacity is in doubt, settle it first: a refusal is the patient's only if they could make it.

## The partial yes

Mr. Sutton's no was partial, and most refusals are. A patient accepts the admission and declines the procedure, or takes the pills and declines the injection. The note keeps three things apart. The part accepted comes first, so that the no is not read as a refusal of care. The part refused is named exactly and as a refusal: "LP declined," not "LP deferred," which makes a refusal look like a plan. And the plan that remains says what the refusal changed. Without the LP, the antibiotics are no longer a bridge to a result; they are the treatment for a diagnosis that may never be confirmed, and the admitting team needs to know it.

The orders have to agree with the note. An LP order left open after the refusal sits on the timeline as a procedure nobody performed, like the result nobody acted on in the chapter on closing the loop. Carmen cancels it with a reason and hands the refusal, with the plan to ask again, to the next clinician by name.

A refusal also ages. The second scenario is constructed as well. Isabel Torres, a hospital medicine NP, cares for Martin Zielinski, seventy-six, admitted with pneumonia. After his first injection of heparin to prevent blood clots, he declines the rest: "Those shots leave bruises all over me." On the morning of day three, Isabel finds four doses marked refused in the medication record and no provider note that mentions them. Read after a clot, those entries would describe refusals nobody heard.

Her note that morning answers them: what he declined and why; that she told him a clot could form in his leg and travel to his lungs while he spends most of the day in bed; what he accepted instead, inflatable compression sleeves and walks in the hall, which she told him protect less; and that she will offer the injections again each morning and record each answer with its time. A refusal written once and never revisited reads as a decision the clinicians stopped thinking about.

## What the note must contain

- **The recommendation.** What was proposed, why, who would do it and who was present.
- **The risks in plain words.** The risks of refusing for this patient, the most serious first, and those of the test or treatment itself.
- **The alternatives.** Each option offered, including going without and what would replace it.
- **The patient's own words.** The reason for the refusal and the patient's account of what could happen.
- **The capacity finding.** For this decision at this time, with the answers behind it.
- **The partial yes.** What was accepted, what was refused and what the refusal changed in the plan.
- **The next offer.** That the patient may change their mind, who will ask again and when, and orders updated to match.
- **The form.** Signed or declined, never in place of the conversation.
