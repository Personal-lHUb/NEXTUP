# What You Don't Know Yet, Written Plainly

## Six hours into a stomachache

The scenario that follows is constructed. Erin Kowalski spent six years as a recovery-room nurse before her NP program. For the last seven months she has worked at an urgent care clinic that closes at 21:00 and has no imaging on site. At 18:20 on a Wednesday she sees Ben Sorensen, nineteen, whose stomach began to ache around noon. The pain sits around his navel and comes in cramps. He vomited once at 15:00 and has eaten almost nothing since breakfast. His roommate was sick with vomiting last week.

His temperature is 99.5°F. His abdomen is soft and mildly tender around the navel, with no guarding and no rebound tenderness. Pressing on the lower right side hurts no more than pressing anywhere else. He climbs on and off the table easily, hops on one foot without wincing and keeps down the water he sips in the room.

Erin thinks a stomach virus is the likeliest explanation. She also knows that appendicitis can begin as vague pain around the navel before it settles in the lower right abdomen, and that six hours in, an exam may not yet tell the two apart. What she does next depends on how she holds that uncertainty. So does her note.

## Two ways to write it wrong

Here is one note she could write:

> 19M abd pain x 6 hrs, vomiting x1, roommate w/ similar illness. Abd soft, mild periumbilical tenderness. Dx: viral gastroenteritis. Ondansetron, clear fluids. RTC PRN.

And here is another:

> 19M abd pain x 6 hrs. Ddx includes gastroenteritis, appendicitis, bowel obstruction, pancreatitis, kidney stone. Cannot rule out surgical abdomen. Etiology unclear. Clinical correlation recommended. Advised ED eval if symptoms persist or worsen.

The first note is certain, and the certainty is the problem. Suppose Ben comes back two days later with a ruptured appendix. A reviewer will find a diagnosis stated as fact six hours into the illness, a plan that rests entirely on it, and nothing to show that Erin knew the picture could still change. She did know, but the note gives her no credit for it.

The second note looks careful, and a reviewer distrusts it just as quickly. It names appendicitis and then shows nothing done about it. As the chapter on the differential explained, that reads worse than leaving it out. "Cannot rule out" is true of almost any diagnosis at any visit, so it says nothing about how worried Erin was. "Clinical correlation recommended" belongs in a radiology report, where it asks the treating clinician to weigh a finding; in a provider note, the treating clinician is the author. Like "RTC PRN," "persist or worsen" gives Ben no threshold he could recognize at two in the morning. Taken together, the note shows a clinician who saw danger everywhere and planned for none of it.

The two notes fail in the same place. Neither says what Erin believed at 18:40, how firmly she believed it, or what would have changed her mind. Each of those fits in a sentence.

## A diagnosis with a date on it

A *working diagnosis* is your best explanation for now, held firmly enough to act on and loosely enough to revise. The *final diagnosis* is the one the facts eventually confirm, sometimes days later and from someone else. Reviewers know the two often differ early in an illness, so a working diagnosis that later changes is not an error in itself. What they distrust is a working diagnosis written as if it were already final.

The wording carries the difference. "Most consistent with" and "favor X over Y because" state a leaning and its basis. "Not excluded" leaves a possibility honestly open, provided the next words say what is being done about it. Avoid "r/o" as a diagnostic label: some readers take "r/o appendicitis" to mean still under consideration and others to mean already excluded, and months later nobody can tell which you meant. Match the strength of each word to the evidence. If one explanation is clearly ahead, "most likely" is accurate. If you cannot yet tell two apart, say so, and say what will.

Then add the sentence most notes leave out: what would change your mind. Here is Erin's assessment, rewritten:

> Assessment: abdominal pain x 6 hrs. Working diagnosis: viral gastroenteritis, favored given crampy, intermittent pain, one episode of vomiting, roommate ill with vomiting last week, tolerating fluids in clinic. Early appendicitis not excluded: periumbilical pain can precede localization, and at 6 hrs the exam may not yet show it. At 18:35: T 99.5°F, no RLQ tenderness, no guarding or rebound, hops without pain. Would reconsider appendicitis if pain localizes to the RLQ, becomes constant or steadily worse, or hurts with walking, or if fever develops or he cannot keep fluids down.

The rewrite drops the diagnoses Erin never seriously weighed and keeps the two she did. Its last sentence is the hinge of the note. It shows an uncertainty that was real, specific and watched, and it gives the plan its content.

## The plan the uncertainty writes

Every condition in "would reconsider if" has to reappear in the plan in two forms. It becomes something a clinician will check at a stated time, and something the patient will watch for until then. A reviewer will set the assessment beside the plan and look for the match. A risk that the assessment names and the plan never mentions looks like the moment it was forgotten. Erin's plan does both:

> Plan: ondansetron, small sips of clear fluids. Options discussed: (1) ED now for blood work and imaging, likely several hours, may show nothing; (2) home, recheck here 09:00 tomorrow, when the exam may show what it cannot now, accepting that appendicitis, if present, would be found later. Patient prefers 2, to avoid a night in the ED; roommate home overnight, has a car. Told to go to the ED tonight, not this clinic (closes 21:00), if pain moves to his lower right belly, hurts when he walks or rides over bumps, or keeps getting worse, or if he gets a temperature of 100.4°F or higher or cannot keep fluids down. Repeated the list back. Recheck booked 09:00; front desk to call him if not here by 09:30.

Three details do the work. The recheck has a time and an owner, so the reassessment is a commitment rather than a hope. The warning signs are the conditions the assessment named, translated from "RLQ" into words Ben will use about his own belly. And the instructions name a place that will be open when he needs it. In a hospital the person watching is usually a nurse, and the same conditions become limits set in advance, as in Nadia Brooks's rewritten entry in the chapter on what a chart becomes after a bad outcome. The chapter on discharge works these instructions in full. What matters here is their source: the uncertainty you wrote down, not a generic sheet.

## When the evidence leaves a real choice

Erin did not decide alone where Ben would spend the evening, and she did not have to. With a reassuring exam six hours in, going to the emergency department now and coming back for a recheck in the morning were both reasonable, and each had a cost that the plan names. When the evidence leaves a real choice like this, the patient's values belong in the decision. That is *shared decision-making*: the clinician sets out the reasonable options and what each one means, and the patient's priorities settle which one fits.

On the record, a shared decision has three parts. The options appear as they were offered. Each comes with what it trades away, in terms the patient could weigh. And the choice is recorded with the reason the patient gave and anything that makes it safe, here the roommate and the car. Priya Raman's plan, in the chapter on the provider note, offered that kind of choice in two sentences. "Risks and benefits discussed, patient agrees with plan" does none of this: it names no option, risk or preference, and it would read the same for any patient.

There is one limit. A choice is shared only when you would stand behind every option you offer. If Ben had shown rebound tenderness and a fever, going home would not have been a reasonable option. His wish to go anyway would have been a refusal, not a preference. Writing that up as a shared decision would misstate what happened, and that note belongs to the chapter on informed refusal.

## The symptom that has no name yet

Some visits end without a working diagnosis worth the name. The second scenario is also constructed. Rachel Dunmore, a former dialysis nurse, is in her second year as a primary care NP. At 10:40 on a Monday she sees Joan Petrakis, fifty-four, who has felt tired for three months. The clinic scale recorded 162 pounds four months ago and 151 today, and Mrs. Petrakis has not been trying to lose weight. Nothing in the history or exam points anywhere in particular, so Rachel orders a first set of blood tests.

Two weak entries are tempting. "Fatigue, likely stress" gives the symptom a convenient explanation with nothing underneath it. Once such a label reaches the problem list, later notes tend to repeat it until nobody looks further. "Weight loss, r/o malignancy" has the ambiguity described earlier, and it names the most frightening possibility without saying what will settle it. The honest version makes the symptom itself the problem and says where the search stands:

> Assessment: unintentional weight loss, 11 lb over 4 months (clinic weights), with fatigue x 3 months. Cause not yet identified. No fever, night sweats, cough, change in bowel habits or blood in stool; appetite reduced; depression screen negative today. Initial labs ordered. Plan: will call patient with results Thursday. Return visit in 2 weeks whatever they show, sooner for fever, night sweats, blood in stool or further weight loss. Patient told the cause may take more than one visit to find; if labs do not explain it, next steps chosen together at the 2-week visit.

A symptom is a legitimate entry in an assessment, and "cause not yet identified" is a sentence a reviewer can respect. The hedged version of Erin's note said something similar with "etiology unclear," but Erin had a leaning and hid it behind the phrase. Rachel has no leaning yet, says so, and puts a date on what happens next. An unexplained complaint becomes dangerous on the record when it lacks a next step, not when it lacks a diagnosis. Keep the symptom open on the problem list until something explains it. Let each later note say what has been excluded and what remains, so that a reader a year from now can follow the question to its answer, or to the last day anyone was still pursuing it.
