# Missed Visits and Referrals That Go Nowhere

## A positive test and two empty slots

The scenario that follows is constructed. Nina Sandoval spent eight years as a public health nurse and has now worked eleven months as an NP at Maplewood Family Practice. At her annual visit on March 4, Marlene Ostrowski, fifty-eight, takes home a fecal immunochemical test, or FIT: a stool test for blood too slight to see, used to screen for colorectal cancer. On Tuesday, March 18, the result posts: positive.

At 16:10 Nina reaches her at home. A positive test does not mean cancer, she explains, but the blood has a source, and a colonoscopy is how it is found. Ms. Ostrowski wants to talk it through in person before agreeing to anything, so a visit is booked for March 25 at 10:00. She does not come. The clinic rebooks her for April 8 at 09:20, and she misses that visit too.

## What the record will be asked

Suppose the story stops there, and the following January Ms. Ostrowski is found to have colon cancer. On the timeline from the chapter on what a chart becomes after a bad outcome, her record shows a positive screening test in March, two no-show flags and then months of silence. A reviewer will ask three things of it. Was the follow-up necessary, and does a line written before anyone knew the ending say so? Was she told why it mattered, in words she could act on? And when it did not happen, who noticed, when, and what was done?

The scheduling system answers none of these questions. A no-show flag records an empty slot, not anyone's decision about it. Whether the effort that followed was reasonable is the standard-of-care question again, and the stakes shape the answer: a missed blood pressure recheck and a missed visit after a positive cancer screening do not call for the same pursuit. An argument that the patient chose to stay away will rest on what the record shows she was told.

## The note as written

Between patients, Nina writes two entries:

> 3/18. FIT positive. Pt notified, f/u appt scheduled.
>
> 4/11. Pt no-show x2. Letter sent. Noncompliant with f/u.

"Pt notified" does not say what she heard: nothing shows the word colonoscopy, or why it mattered, so the visit reads like a routine recheck she could skip. Nobody appears to have noticed the first no-show: the March 25 flag stands alone, and the rebooking shows up only as a second empty slot. "Letter sent" has no date, no content and no copy. "Noncompliant" is the label the chapter on facts and quotes took apart, a verdict on a woman whose reason for staying away nobody recorded. And the entries stop at the letter, with no next step and no date, so a positive cancer screening settles into the chart as if it had been handled.

## The rewrite

Here are the same weeks, written from what Nina did and heard:

> Tue 3/18, 16:25. Telephone encounter. FIT from 3/4 visit, resulted 09:40 today: positive. Called home 16:10, reached patient, identity confirmed by date of birth. Told in plain words: the test found hidden blood in her stool; it does not mean cancer, but the source must be found, and colonoscopy is how; polyps and early cancers found this way are easier to treat. She asked to discuss in person before any referral: "I'm not agreeing to a scope over the phone." Visit booked Tue 3/25 10:00 for that discussion. She said: "So it may be nothing, but only the scope can tell." Added to problem list: positive FIT, colonoscopy not yet done.
>
> Tue 3/25, 14:45. No-show, 10:00 visit for positive FIT (see 3/18). 10:45 called cell: no answer; voicemail asked her to call clinic, result not stated. 14:30 Keisha Monroe, MA, reached patient; reason given: forgot. Rebooked Tue 4/8 09:20; told the visit is about her test result.
>
> Tue 4/8, 10:15. Second no-show, visit for positive FIT. 09:50 called cell and home: no answer; voicemails as on 3/25. Portal message 10:00: a test result needs follow-up, please call me. If no contact by Fri 4/11: letter per clinic policy. Will review 4/11.
>
> Fri 4/11, 15:40. No call; portal shows message unread. Letter sent by certified mail per clinic policy, copy in chart: a test result needs follow-up; waiting could let a treatable problem become harder to treat; my direct line and clinic hours. Chart alert: positive FIT, colonoscopy not done; route any contact to me.
>
> Wed 4/16, 12:50. Telephone encounter. Patient called 12:30. Why she missed both visits: "I kept putting it off. I was scared of what they'd find." Explained again why colonoscopy is needed and what it involves; questions answered. Agrees to referral: "Okay. Better to know." Referral sent 12:45 to Dr. Samuel Tran, Lakeside Gastroenterology, marked positive FIT. Will check scheduling by 5/14.

## Outreach the record can count

The first entry, written on March 18 before anyone knew they would be asked, answers the reviewer's first two questions. "Easier to treat" is the reason for the visit in words a patient can weigh against a missed shift; "we need to see you" gives her nothing to weigh. Her read-back shows she understood why it mattered. The problem-list line keeps the open question in front of anyone who opens her chart, including a colleague who sees her for a sore throat in June.

Each outreach entry is built like the call log in the chapter on the consultant who never comes: time, method, what was said or left, what came back. A voicemail or an unopened portal message is an attempt, as the chapter on closing the loop on the abnormal result established, and the note never calls it "notified." Within what your policy lets a message say, it says that something needs attention and how to reach you. The copy in the chart shows what the letter said, not just that it went out.

The number of attempts, the methods and whether the letter goes by certified mail come from your organization's policy, not from this book, as they did for the result in that chapter. Treat policy as the least you do; the stakes set how far beyond it you go. Nina tried the home number as well as the cell and gave the letter a date, because this result could not wait for Ms. Ostrowski to remember it. Each entry ends with the next step and its deadline. Her reason, once she gave it, goes in her own words: fear can be answered, and a label cannot.

Had she never called, the April 11 entry could stand as the last step policy required. It records what was done, that the result is still open and how the chart will catch her next contact. "Lost to follow-up," written after an entry like that, describes where an effort ended instead of replacing one.

## The referral that never comes back

The scenario continues, still constructed. On May 14, the date Nina set, the referral still reads "sent" in the clinic's system, with no appointment beside it. She calls Lakeside, then the patient, and writes:

> Wed 5/14, 11:40. Referral check, positive FIT (referral sent 4/16). No appointment in system. 11:05 called Lakeside Gastroenterology: referral received 4/17; voicemails left for patient 4/18 and 4/25, no reply. 11:20 called patient: "I thought they'd call me again." Lakeside's scheduling number given; she will call today.
>
> Addendum, 16:40. Lakeside confirms colonoscopy booked Tue 6/10. Will check for report by 6/24.
>
> Tue 6/24, 09:40. Colonoscopy report not in chart. 09:10 called Lakeside: done 6/10; report sent, received 09:30 and reviewed. Two polyps removed; pathology pending. Per Lakeside, Dr. Tran's office will give patient the pathology result and set her next colonoscopy; copy to be sent here. Problem list updated: colonoscopy 6/10, pathology pending at Lakeside.

A referral marked "sent" records an order, not an appointment, and an appointment is not a result. Nina's two checks sit where referrals stall: the scheduling that never happens and the report that never comes back. Each was dated at the step before it, so neither depended on her memory. Who tracks referrals is your organization's decision: a referral coordinator in some, the ordering clinician in others. Ask who it is where you work, and what happens when one stalls.

Ownership follows the rule from the chapter on closing the loop on the abnormal result: it passes only when the next clinician knows and accepts. Until the colonoscopy was done and its report read, the question raised by the test Nina ordered was hers. On June 24 Dr. Tran's office took on the next step, and the entry names it. Nothing in her entries faults Lakeside or the patient; the unanswered voicemails and the missing report appear as facts with times.

## The patient who says no

Suppose that on April 16 Ms. Ostrowski had said she would not have a colonoscopy. What had been silence would have become a refusal, and the note would be the one set out in the chapter on informed refusal: the risk of leaving the bleeding unexplained, in plain words; the alternatives offered, such as a visit with Dr. Tran only to talk; her reason and her account of what could happen; capacity for this decision; and the next offer, at each visit, with each answer timed. The problem-list line stays.

The opposite mistake is writing "declined colonoscopy" for a patient who never answered. It records a conversation that did not happen, the error the chapter on the patient leaving against medical advice ruled out for the departure nobody witnessed. Unanswered calls, an unopened portal message and an unanswered letter are outreach, not a refusal, and the note calls them that.

## What the note must contain

- **Why the follow-up matters.** At the call or visit that sets it up, the reason in plain words and what the patient said back.
- **The open item.** Where the chart will show it until it closes, such as the problem list or an alert.
- **Each missed visit, noticed.** Who reviewed it, when, and what was decided.
- **Each attempt.** Time, method, what was said or left, and the reply or its absence; a copy of any letter.
- **The next step and its date.** At the end of every entry, within the steps your policy sets.
- **The referral, tracked.** Whether it was received and scheduled, whether the report came back, who checked and when, and who owns the next step until the report has been read.
- **The patient's answer.** Their reason in their own words; a refusal written as an informed refusal, and silence never written as one.
- **Where the effort ended.** What was done, that the item is still open, and how the chart will catch the next contact.
