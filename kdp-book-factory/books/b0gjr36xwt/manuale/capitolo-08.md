# Templates, Copy-Forward, and the Late Addendum

## Yesterday's exam, signed today

The scenario that follows is constructed. Mateo Ruiz spent five years as a cardiac step-down nurse and has now worked six months as a hospital medicine NP. Evelyn Kraus, eighty-one, was admitted on Monday with worsening heart failure and has been on IV furosemide since. At 05:30 on Wednesday her nurse charts new confusion. Mateo sees her at 08:45: drowsy, oriented only to herself, moving all four limbs. Her sodium, 134 on Tuesday, is 129 this morning, and at 08:55 he holds the furosemide.

At 09:10 he opens Tuesday's progress note, copies it forward into a new one, updates the weight and the sodium, and signs at 09:24. Part of it reads:

> Hospital day 3. Feels better, breathing easier, slept well. Exam: alert and oriented x3, NAD. Lungs: fine crackles at both bases. 1+ edema to mid-shin. Wt 71.2 kg. Na 129. Assessment: acute on chronic systolic heart failure, improving. Plan: continue IV furosemide 40 mg BID. Start lisinopril tomorrow if creatinine stable.

Every copied line was true on Tuesday, when Mateo wrote it. Signed on Wednesday, at least three are false: she did not say she felt better, she was not oriented, and the drug he says to continue was stopped twenty-nine minutes before he signed. "Tomorrow" now means Thursday, because copied text keeps the tense of the day it was written. And the one new number, a falling sodium, sits beside an assessment that still says "improving."

On the timeline, this note sits between the 05:30 nursing entry and the 08:55 order and fits neither. A reviewer can draw only two conclusions: Mateo did not examine her, or he examined her and missed her confusion. Neither is true, but his own note now contradicts the order that shows what he did.

Your signature adopts every line above it, as the chapter on the provider note explained, so yesterday's exam becomes your statement that you examined the patient today and found exactly that. *Copy-forward*, carrying an earlier note's text into a new one, saves real time, and many organizations permit it within limits set by policy. What it costs is the comparison a progress note exists to make. These are the lines that should have changed:

> Hospital day 3. Seen 08:45. RN charted new confusion at 05:30. Drowsy, rouses to voice, oriented to self only; oriented x3 on my exam yesterday. Follows commands, moves all limbs, face symmetric. Lungs: fine crackles at both bases, less than yesterday. Edema 1+ to ankles. Wt 71.2 kg (72.6 yesterday). Na 129 (134 yesterday). Assessment: new confusion, sodium falling on IV diuresis; other causes under evaluation. Plan: IV furosemide held 08:55. Lisinopril deferred until sodium and mental status improve.

Treat a copied note as a draft that is wrong until you have checked it. Stable sections, such as past history, allergies and home medications, can carry forward once you have read them against today's chart. The sections that describe a day (the patient's report, the exam, the assessment, the plan) get written fresh or checked line by line against this morning. Watch for words with a date hidden inside them: today, tomorrow, overnight, "will start." Then write the comparison yourself. "Less than yesterday" is the kind of phrase copy-forward erases, and it shows a patient being followed.

## Defaults that argue with the history

Templates cause the same problem from the other side. Instead of carrying yesterday's facts into today, they supply facts nobody observed. Many exam templates open with *default normals*, every system already marked normal until you change it, and many review-of-systems templates end in "all other systems negative." Some fields arrive prefilled, such as smoking status or "no known drug allergies." Some phrases insert themselves at a keystroke: "risks, benefits and alternatives discussed," "return precautions given," "patient seen and examined." Each is a sentence you sign without having written it.

A templated exam can be accurate and still argue against nothing, as the chapter on the differential showed. Worse is the template that says something false, and reviewers know where to look. One place is a contradiction inside the note: three days of vomiting in the history, and "GI: denies nausea, vomiting, diarrhea" below it. Another is a contradiction with the timeline, such as "gait steady" for a patient whose nurse, that same hour, charted a two-person assist to the commode. A third is the finding nobody could have made: equal pedal pulses in a patient with a left below-knee amputation, or a full review of systems, all negative, for a patient intubated since admission. The last is repetition: the same exam, down to a misspelled word, across several days or several patients.

One such line costs more than itself. The reader cannot sort the findings you checked from the ones the template supplied, so they discount all of them, including the pertinent negatives that carried your exclusions. An exam recorded but never performed raises a different question, too: not whether your judgment was sound, but whether your record is true. That question reaches every other note you have signed.

The check takes about a minute. Before you sign, read each templated section against the history you took and the exam you did. Delete the systems you did not ask about instead of leaving them "negative," and write "not examined" for what you did not examine. Delete an inserted phrase unless the thing it names happened, and when it did, say what was actually discussed or given.

## Correcting a signed note

At 10:05 that Wednesday, Mrs. Kraus's nurse asks Mateo why his note says to continue a drug he stopped. The 09:24 note cannot become the rewrite now. What he can do is leave it standing and add a correction:

> Correction, 10:15, to progress note signed 09:24 today. Subjective, exam, assessment and plan were carried forward in error from yesterday's note. Correct findings at 08:45: drowsy, oriented to self only, follows commands, no focal deficit. New confusion; sodium 129, down from 134. IV furosemide held 08:55, not continued. Lisinopril deferred. Weight and labs as signed are correct.

The correction follows the old rule of the paper chart, where an error got a single line through it, the right entry beside it, and the writer's initials and the date. The old text stays readable, and the new text says who changed it, when and why. Most record systems offer a way to correct or amend a signed note, and your organization's policy says which one to use. What you never do is type over a signed note, delete it and sign a clean copy, or remove an entry because it looks bad. A note in the wrong patient's chart goes through your organization's procedure too, usually via health information management, not the delete key.

Deleting is the worst move because it does not work. A silent edit does not remove the error; it adds a record that the entry was changed, by whom and when. A mistake corrected openly is one of the most ordinary entries in any chart. The same mistake rewritten in silence and found later turns a question about care into a question about honesty, and a reader who doubts one entry doubts the rest. Altering a record once a claim is pending or reasonably expected can also be treated as **spoliation**, the destruction or alteration of evidence. What follows depends on the state and the court, and the consequences can weigh more than the original question about the care.

## Late entries and addenda

Some care will be written after it happened, and the record has an honest way to show that. A **late entry** documents care that was given earlier but not written at the time. An **addendum** adds information to a note already signed: a result that came back, a call returned, a detail left out. Organizations name and define the two differently, so follow your policy on the label; the requirements underneath are the same.

At 11:30 Mateo goes back to Mrs. Kraus. Two admissions arrive at noon, and he writes nothing until 15:50:

> Late entry, written 15:50, for bedside reassessment at 11:30. Delayed by two admissions. At 11:30: awake, oriented to person and place, not date. Daughter at bedside said she seemed more like herself. Repeat Na 131 (resulted 11:10). Plan unchanged.

The entry is labeled for what it is. It gives two times and keeps them apart: the system stamps the time of writing on its own, but the time of the care has to be in the text. It states the reason for the delay in a few factual words, with no defense attached. It records only what Mateo remembers with confidence or can take from another source, such as the lab result; the daughter's remark has no quotation marks, because four hours is enough to lose her exact words. A late entry that says it is late is ordinary; one disguised as contemporaneous is not.

A delay costs weight. Written four hours later, before anything has gone wrong, this entry reads as a busy afternoon. Written after a fall or a complaint, the same words read as a record being built for an argument, however accurate they are. Once a records request, a complaint or a claim has arrived, add nothing to the chart without speaking to risk management first; the chapter on the adverse event covers that moment.

## What the audit trail sees

Behind the chart you read, the system keeps a second record you usually cannot see. The **audit trail**, or audit log, records activity in the chart: which account opened which patient's record, when, and often from which device; what was viewed, created, edited, signed, printed or deleted; and in many systems each earlier version of a changed note. It does not depend on what anyone chose to write. What your system captures, and for how long, is a question for your health information management or privacy office.

After a bad outcome, the audit trail can be requested along with the chart, and a reviewer lays it beside the notes, minute by minute. For Mrs. Kraus it would show a note opened at 09:10 and signed at 09:24, a correction at 10:15 and a late entry at 15:50, each saying what it is. The two records agree, and that agreement is what a reviewer looks for. A signed note changed in silence, or an entry describing 08:45 that was created at 16:00 and never says so, shows up just as plainly. So does every look, including a look at the chart of a patient who is not yours.

Before you sign, a change is just an edit. After, it is an entry of its own, with your name and the minute attached. The only kind that helps you is the one that says what it is.
