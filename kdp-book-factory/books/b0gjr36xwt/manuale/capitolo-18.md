# The Patient Leaving Against Medical Advice

## A bag packed at 14:10

At 14:10 on a Wednesday, Dennis Quigley, sixty-six, is dressed, his bag is packed and he wants his IV out. He was admitted on Monday at 22:00 with right lower lobe pneumonia, a temperature of 102.8°F and an oxygen saturation of 89%, and started on IV ceftriaxone and azithromycin. He came off oxygen on Tuesday evening, but at 06:00 this morning his temperature was 100.9°F, and the team plans at least one more day of IV treatment. Nobody in this case is a real person.

His nurse, Silas Nakamura, pages Fiona Strand, a hospital medicine NP in her first year. Mr. Quigley's wife had a stroke last year and cannot be left alone, and the neighbor staying with her goes home tonight. "I'm not lying in this bed," he tells Silas, "while she's on the floor somewhere."

Fiona sits with him from 14:20 to 14:45, and Dr. Vikram Mehta, the attending, joins them at 14:35. Mr. Quigley signs the hospital's form at 15:00 and walks out at 15:15.

## What the record will be asked

A patient leaves **against medical advice**, usually shortened to AMA, when they end care before the treating clinicians judge it safe, after being told so. Leaving refuses everything that would have followed. So the record will first be asked what the chapter on informed refusal asked of any no: whether he understood what he was refusing and what could follow, whether he heard the alternatives, and whether he could make the decision.

A departure adds a question of its own: once he said no, was he given every reasonable chance to do well anyway, and to come back? Suppose Mr. Quigley returns on Saturday in septic shock. A reviewer who knows that ending will look at the hour before he left to see what he took with him: a treatment, a follow-up and a way back, or a signature and nothing else.

## The note as written

At 15:30 Fiona writes:

> 15:30. Pt leaving AMA. Risks explained incl. death. Pt has capacity. AMA form signed. Dr. Mehta aware. IV d/c'd.

The chapter on informed refusal showed what a form cannot record, and the note beside this form says little more. "Risks explained incl. death" names the worst outcome without the path to it: what pneumonia does when treatment stops early, and which signs would mean it was happening. "Pt has capacity" is a conclusion with none of the answers that the chapter on the capacity question puts behind one. His reason is gone, so the departure reads as impatience rather than a husband's choice. And the note ends at the door. Nothing says what treatment he left with, who will see him next or whether anyone told him he could come back. Read on Saturday, it describes care that ended with a signature.

## The rewrite

Fiona's rewrite keeps to what she did and heard:

> 15:30. Left hospital against medical advice at 15:15. His reason: "My wife can't be alone tonight. The neighbor goes home at six." Day 3 of IV ceftriaxone and azithromycin for RLL pneumonia. T 100.9°F at 06:00, 99.8°F at 14:00; HR 96; SpO2 93% RA at rest, 91% walking at 13:30 per RN S. Nakamura. Recommended: stay at least 24 more hours for IV antibiotics and monitoring until fever resolves.
>
> Discussed 14:20–14:45; Dr. V. Mehta, attending, from 14:35. Explained in plain words: the infection is better but not controlled. If he leaves now, it is more likely to get worse, spread to his blood or lower his oxygen, and any of these can be life-threatening and can happen at home overnight. Offered: social worker to arrange overnight help for his wife so he could stay (declined: "She won't let a stranger in the house"). His account of the risk: "It's not finished, and it could get bad fast. If I can't breathe or I get mixed up, I come right back." Capacity for this decision: restated the risks in his own words, applies them to himself, reason consistent with his values, choice unchanged over 25 minutes. Has capacity at this time to decline further admission.
>
> Leaving with: oral amoxicillin-clavulanate and azithromycin, chosen with Dr. Mehta to continue similar coverage by mouth, sent to his pharmacy 14:55. Told that pills are a step down from IV at this stage but far better than none. IV removed by RN 15:05, catheter intact. Return to the ED or call 911 for breathlessness at rest, confusion, chest pain, blue lips, temperature 102°F or higher, or vomiting that keeps him from taking the pills; written copy given, and he repeated them back. Blood cultures from Monday: no growth to date, final pending. I will follow them and call his cell, number confirmed, if positive. 14:50 called the office of Dr. Grace Ochoa, PCP: visit booked Friday 09:30; discharge summary to her today. Told: "You can come back here any time, tonight included, and we'll pick up where we left off. Nobody will hold it against you." He said: "Good. I might need that." Form signed 15:00, copy given.

## Leaving the door open

Nothing on the AMA form, and nothing in the note as written, matches the rewrite's last paragraph. A patient who refuses the hospital has not refused all care. What he still accepts is the partial yes from the chapter on informed refusal, and here that is the outpatient plan. Withholding a prescription or a visit to press him to stay would send a man with pneumonia home untreated, and on the record that reads as punishment. The oral antibiotics need a reason, as the chapter on prescribing asks of any drug, and a line on what he was told about stepping down from IV.

Write the invitation to return the way you said it, because a reviewer cannot tell "may return PRN" from a door politely closed. The wording matters at the bedside too: a patient who thinks he has burned a bridge has a reason to stay home while his breathing worsens. Leave out warnings you cannot stand behind, such as a claim that insurance will refuse to pay. You rarely know it, the patient may believe it, and a note that records it reads as pressure, not information.

Each part of the plan he leaves with also needs an owner, as the chapter on closing the loop required of every result. Cultures that post after a patient has left belong to someone, and the note names who. The visit has a day and an hour, and the next clinician hears about the stay from the team, not from the patient. Write the departure itself by the rules in the chapter on facts and quotes: "insisted on leaving despite repeated warnings" records a contest, not a conversation.

## The patient who is simply gone

If Mr. Quigley had refused to sign, the rewrite would change in one line: "Declined to sign form; witnessed by RN S. Nakamura." Some patients leave before any conversation can happen, as in a second case, imaginary like the first. Tariq Rahimi, a former interventional radiology nurse, is in his second year as an NP in a community emergency department. At 01:00 on a Sunday he sees Troy Underwood, forty-seven, whose chest pressure began at 23:00 and has faded. The ECG and the first high-sensitivity troponin are normal, and a second troponin is due at 03:10. At 01:40 Tariq tells him why: "A heart attack doesn't always show on the first test." At 02:40 his nurse, Nora Vance, finds the room empty.

Nobody discussed leaving with him, so there is no refusal to record, and the note must not invent one. "Left AMA" implies that the risks were explained and a choice was made. Your organization may use another label for a departure nobody witnessed, such as *eloped* or *left before treatment complete*. At 02:55 Tariq writes:

> 02:55. Not in room at 02:40, when RN N. Vance came to draw repeat troponin; last seen by her 02:05, vitals normal, no pain. Gown on bed; saline lock on bedside table, catheter intact. ED, restrooms and waiting room checked; security checked main exit, did not see him. Workup incomplete: ECG 01:05 and troponin 01:10 normal; repeat troponin due 03:10 not drawn. At 01:40, alert and oriented, told the plan and why the second test is needed. No discussion of leaving took place. 02:50 called cell on file: voicemail asked him to call or come back, no results stated. Dr. Alan Wexler, ED attending, informed 02:55; per department policy, no police welfare check requested: no concern about capacity. Will call again 03:30.
>
> Addendum, 03:40. Reached by phone 03:30, identity confirmed. Speech clear, answers coherent. Left because "the wait was killing me, and I start work at seven." No pain since midnight. Explained that the second test could still show a heart attack the first one missed; invited him back now or at any hour. Declined tonight; will call his PCP for a visit Monday. Told to call 911, not drive, if the pressure returns or comes with sweating or shortness of breath. He said: "So it could still be my heart. If it comes back, I'm calling 911."

The line about the discussion that never took place keeps the first entry honest. The addendum is the informed refusal, held by phone after the fact and labeled the way the chapter on templates and the late addendum describes. Your organization's policy sets who must be told after a departure nobody saw, and whether security or the police are asked to look for the patient. Write which steps it called for, who took them and when. Had Mr. Underwood been confused, or held under a legal order, his leaving would be a safety event, not a choice. What staff may do about it then depends on state law and policy, so ask your attending and risk manager.

## What the note must contain

- **The departure.** The time, the patient's reason in their own words, and the care still recommended.
- **The informed refusal.** The risks of leaving now, the alternatives offered (including help with the reason for leaving), and the capacity finding, written as the chapters on informed refusal and the capacity question describe.
- **The door left open.** The invitation to return as it was said, and the patient's reply.
- **What the patient left with.** Prescriptions accepted or declined, lines removed, instructions for this illness, pending results and their owner.
- **The follow-up.** Who sees the patient next, when, and who arranged it.
- **Who was told.** The attending and anyone else policy names, with times.
- **The form.** Signed or declined, filed beside the note and never in place of it.
- **The unwitnessed departure.** When the patient was last seen and found gone, the search, what they had been told, each attempt to reach them, and who was notified. Never a conversation that did not happen.
