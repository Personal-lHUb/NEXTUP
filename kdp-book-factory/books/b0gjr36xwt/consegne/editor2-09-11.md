# The Test You Order and the Test You Don't

## Ice in the parking lot

The coworker who drove Curtis Faber to urgent care saw him fall. At 07:50 on a Tuesday in January, Mr. Faber, thirty-eight, slipped on ice in the parking lot at work, pitched forward onto his outstretched right hand and struck his forehead on the pavement; he was "out for maybe five seconds," the coworker says. The clinic has X-ray on site but no CT scanner; the nearest one is in an emergency department twenty minutes away. The NP on shift, Celeste Hartley, sees him at 10:20.

Mr. Faber, Celeste and the clinic are fiction, written for teaching. He remembers slipping and everything after he came to. He has a mild headache, has not vomited, takes no medications and has had no alcohol. His vital signs and neurological exam are normal, and apart from a swelling on his forehead he shows no sign of a skull fracture. His right wrist is tender in the hollow at the base of the thumb, the anatomic snuffbox.

Celeste orders an X-ray of the wrist with scaphoid views, and it shows no fracture. She does not order a CT of his head. The first decision leaves an order, a timestamp and a result in the chart. The second leaves nothing unless she writes it down.

## What the record will be asked

After a bad outcome, a reviewer checks the tests from both sides. For every test that was done, the question is why: what was it looking for, and what did the result settle? For a test that could have been done and was not, there are two questions: whether the clinician considered it, and whether the decision was reasonable with what was known at the time.

Whether the test was considered is hard to answer from the record. On the timeline described in the chapter on what a chart becomes after a bad outcome, a CT that was weighed and set aside looks exactly like a CT nobody thought of: in both cases, there is simply no order. Only a sentence in the note tells them apart. Without it, a reader who already knows that the patient came back two days later with a bleed will tend to read the empty space as an omission.

Whether the decision was reasonable is the standard-of-care question, and it is answered with findings that have times attached. The reviewer will look for the facts that made a scan unnecessary at 10:20 and check each against the triage entry. The setting belongs in the answer too. In a clinic without a scanner, a CT means sending the patient to an emergency department, and a note that says so shows the decision where it was actually made.

## The note as written

The note Celeste signs at 11:15 reads, in full:

> 38M slipped on ice 07:50, struck forehead, brief LOC. GCS 15, neuro intact. No indication for CT head. R wrist pain, XR neg. Thumb spica splint. Dx: concussion, R wrist injury. Head injury instructions given. F/u 10 days.

"No indication for CT head" is a verdict with nothing underneath it, the same problem the chapter on the differential found in "PE unlikely." A reviewer cannot tell whether Celeste applied a rule, which one, or whether Mr. Faber fell inside it. "Brief LOC" is the very finding that made the scan worth considering, and the note gives it no witness and no duration. Nothing says whether he takes a blood thinner, whether he vomited or whether he remembers the fall.

The wrist line fails differently. "XR neg" seems to close a question the X-ray could not close, because a scaphoid fracture can be invisible on the first film. Celeste knew that, which is why she splinted the thumb and booked the recheck. But "F/u 10 days" does not say what the follow-up is for, who will order the second X-ray or what Mr. Faber was told. Read six weeks later, after a fracture that failed to heal, the note shows a normal X-ray and a plan that could belong to a sprain.

## The rewrite

With both decisions on the page, the note reads:

> 38M, slipped on ice in work parking lot 07:50, fell forward onto outstretched R hand, struck forehead on pavement. Coworker who drove him here saw the fall: "out for maybe five seconds." Recalls the slip and everything after coming to. Headache 3/10. No vomiting, no seizure. No anticoagulant or antiplatelet, no bleeding disorder, no alcohol today. Seen 10:20: BP 128/78, HR 74, GCS 15. 4 cm frontal hematoma, no step-off. No hemotympanum, periorbital or mastoid bruising, or fluid from nose or ears. Pupils equal and reactive, no focal deficit, gait normal. No midline neck tenderness, full painless rotation. R wrist tender in anatomic snuffbox; XR with scaphoid views: no fracture seen.
>
> Assessment: concussion with brief witnessed LOC; frontal scalp hematoma. Intracranial injury considered. Canadian CT Head Rule applies (minor head injury with witnessed LOC, age 16 or over, no anticoagulant); none of its 7 criteria present: GCS 15 at 10:20, 2.5 h after injury; no sign of open, depressed or basal skull fracture; no vomiting; age 38; no amnesia before the fall; mechanism a ground-level slip. CT not required by the rule. R wrist: possible scaphoid fracture, which can be occult on the first X-ray.
>
> Plan: rule explained to patient. Options: CT at nearest ED (none on site; several hours, small radiation dose, not needed per rule) or home with someone watching him tonight. He said: "If the rule says I don't need it, I'd rather go home and have my wife keep an eye on me." Wife home tonight. Told to go to the ED for worsening headache, vomiting more than once, confusion, trouble waking, weakness or seizure; repeated these back. Thumb spica splint, worn at all times. Recheck here booked Fri 09:00 (day 10); repeat scaphoid views ordered today for that visit. Told the first X-ray can miss this fracture, so the splint stays on until then; will call to rebook if he cannot come.

## The rule and the patient's view

For the CT, the rewrite supplies what the chapter on the differential asks of any decision tool: its name, its inputs and whether the patient fell within its reach. Three details matter most for a test not ordered.

The population comes first. The Canadian CT Head Rule applies to patients aged sixteen or older with a minor head injury, meaning a witnessed loss of consciousness, definite amnesia or witnessed disorientation, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 13 to 15. The coworker's account placed Mr. Faber inside it, so the note gives that account a speaker and quotation marks. The rule was not built for patients taking an oral anticoagulant or with a bleeding disorder; Dolores Fitch, on apixaban in the chapter on the provider note, fell outside it. When a rule does not apply, say so, and give the clinical reasons for your decision instead.

The times come next. One criterion is the Glasgow Coma Scale score two hours after the injury, so the note needs both the time of the fall and the time of the exam. "GCS 15" with no time beside it does not let a reader apply the rule.

Then the output, stated no larger than it is. "CT not required by the rule" is not "no brain injury," and the working diagnosis stays concussion. A rule that says a scan is unnecessary has not said that nothing is wrong. That is the honest uncertainty of the chapter on what you don't know yet, applied to a decision tool, and it is why the plan still tells Mr. Faber what would bring him back.

The plan records a choice that was shared. Mr. Faber was neither asking for a scan nor refusing one; he was weighing hours in an emergency department against an evening at home with someone watching, and his own words show which mattered to him. The three parts of a shared decision from that same chapter are all there, his choice recorded with his reason and the person who makes it safe. If Celeste had not offered the scan and he had asked for one, the encounter would belong to the chapter on the antibiotic or scan you decline.

## The test that waits

The wrist shows a third kind of decision: a test that belongs to a later visit. A deferred test is a plan, and the record will ask of it what it asks of any plan: who will order it, when, and how the patient knows. Celeste's rewrite answers each in a line. She placed the order herself before he left, so the second X-ray does not depend on whoever sees him in ten days working out why he came. The recheck has a day and an hour. And Mr. Faber was told why, in words that also explain the splint.

When the deferred test belongs to someone else, the note has to carry the handoff. "Repeat imaging per PCP" gives the primary care clinician a task they may never hear about. Write who was told, by what means, and what the patient was asked to do. What to record when the follow-up never happens is worked in the chapter on missed visits and referrals that go nowhere.

A test you recommend and the patient declines is neither a deferral nor a shared choice. If Mr. Faber had refused the wrist X-ray, or a CT the rule called for, the note would need everything the chapter on informed refusal sets out. Writing "patient deferred imaging" would make a refusal look like a plan.

## What the note must contain

- **The indication, or the reason not to test.** For a test ordered, the question it was meant to answer and the finding that raised it. For a test weighed and not ordered, the test by name and why it was not needed.
- **The decision rule and its inputs.** The rule by name, the findings that place the patient inside it, each criterion with its value, the times the rule depends on, and the output stated no larger than it is.
- **The patient discussion.** When the evidence left a real choice, the options, what each would cost, and the patient's preference in their own words.
- **The deferred plan.** For a test left for later, who orders it, when it happens, what the patient was told and what they will do if the date slips.

# Closing the Loop on the Abnormal Result

## A potassium at five past five

At 17:05, in a primary care practice that closes at 17:30, the laboratory phones with a *critical value*, a result on its list of values too dangerous to wait in an inbox: potassium 6.6. Gina Calloway, the medical assistant at the front desk, reads it back and brings a slip of paper to Hannah Albright, the NP who ordered the test. No real practice, assistant or patient sits behind this call.

The result belongs to Walter Delgado, seventy-one, who has heart failure and takes lisinopril. A week ago Hannah added spironolactone, and at 09:40 on Tuesday the clinic drew a basic metabolic panel to check his potassium and kidney function. The same panel shows creatinine at 1.9, up from 1.3 in March, and the lab notes no hemolysis that would make the number doubtful.

Hannah calls his cell phone at 17:14 and gets voicemail. At 17:20 she reaches him at home. He feels "a little tired, otherwise fine," and asks whether he can come in tomorrow. She tells him to go to the emergency department now and to take neither spironolactone nor lisinopril until he hears from the clinic. Then she phones the department, and at 19:00 she calls back to check that he has arrived.

## What the record will be asked

A reviewer follows a result along the timeline. It posts at a time the lab system stamps. Someone opens it, and many record systems log who did and when, in the audit trail described in the chapter on templates and addenda. Then something happens, or nothing does. The questions follow the same path: who saw the result and when, what was done about it, and who was told, when and by what means.

The systems often answer the first question, and for a phoned critical value the lab usually logs the call. Only your note answers the other two. Opening a result, or clicking the button that marks it reviewed, records a look, not a decision, and a result opened and filed without a word shows that someone knew. Your organization's policy decides who owns a result; in many, it is the clinician whose name is on the order. The owner writes the step after the look.

One more question matters most when the chain breaks: what was the plan if the patient could not be reached? A voicemail is an attempt, not a notification, because nothing in it shows that the message was heard.

## The note as written

At 17:45 Hannah opens a telephone encounter and writes:

> Lab called critical K 6.6. Pt notified, advised ED. Hold spironolactone.

The note is accurate and leaves out most of the chain. Nothing says who took the lab's call, when it reached Hannah or whether it was read back, so the minutes after 17:05 are blank on her side while the lab's log is not. "Pt notified" covers a voicemail and a second call without saying which one reached him. "Advised ED" could mean go now or go if you feel worse, and it hides both his wish to wait and his agreement not to. The department that was expecting him does not appear.

Suppose Mr. Delgado never reaches the emergency department. His wife may say he was told to go only if he felt worse, and the note cannot answer her. It never says why the result could not wait until morning, and it holds one drug where Hannah held two, so a reader will assume lisinopril continued.

## The rewrite

Written with the whole chain in it, the same call reads:

> Telephone encounter. Lab called critical K 6.6 at 17:05 to Gina Calloway, MA, who read it back; given to me 17:10. Drawn 09:40 today. Same panel: creatinine 1.9 (1.3 in March). Lab reports no hemolysis. On lisinopril 20 mg; spironolactone 25 mg added 7 days ago.
>
> 17:14 called cell: no answer; voicemail asked him to call clinic, result not stated. 17:20 called home, reached patient, identity confirmed with date of birth. Feels "a little tired, otherwise fine." No palpitations, chest pain, shortness of breath or new weakness.
>
> Assessment: hyperkalemia with rising creatinine, likely from spironolactone added to lisinopril. Needs ECG, repeat level and possible treatment tonight, beyond what this clinic can provide.
>
> Plan: told him his potassium is high enough to disturb his heart rhythm and he needs the ED now. He asked, "Can't I come in tomorrow?" After the explanation: "All right. My wife will drive me now." Told to take no spironolactone or lisinopril until he hears from us. 17:35 called County Hospital ED, gave result and history to Dr. Imran Haddad, ED attending. Will call ED at 19:00 to confirm arrival; if he is not there, will call home and cell again, then his wife's cell listed in chart.
>
> Addendum, 19:05: ED reports arrival 18:02, under evaluation.

Each link now has a name and a minute. The failed call belongs in the note: it shows the attempt and accounts for the minutes before Hannah reached him. The voicemail leaves out the result, because someone else may hear it; what a message may contain is set by your organization's policy. Mr. Delgado's first answer sits beside his second, so the record shows an instruction understood and accepted. And the call to Dr. Haddad hands the result to the clinician who acts on it next, before the patient walks in.

The plan's last line does the most work. Telling a patient where to go does not close the loop; it closes when the patient is in front of someone who can act. Hannah's plan says how she would check, and what she would do if he had not arrived, and the 19:05 addendum records the answer.

## The finding nobody asked about

A second case, made up like the first, moves to the hospital. Simone Bergstrom is an NP on the hospital medicine service. On a Monday she admits Frank Iverson, fifty-two, whose CT of the abdomen and pelvis shows sigmoid diverticulitis without abscess. The report's last line answers a question nobody asked: a 7 mm solid nodule in the right lower lobe, for which the radiologist recommends a chest CT in 6 to 12 months.

By Wednesday he is ready for home. Simone has told him about the nodule and messaged his primary care physician, but her draft discharge summary says only "Incidental RLL nodule, f/u PCP," a line that would read the same if neither had happened. As the chapter on the test you order and the test you don't showed, "per PCP" hands a task to someone who may never hear of it, and a finding nobody was waiting for is easier still to lose.

Whoever owns a scan owns its whole report, not only the answer to its question; here, that is Simone's team. Ownership passes to another clinician only when that clinician knows about the finding and accepts it, and the summary has to show that moment. Her rewrite moves the finding to the top:

> Incidental finding requiring follow-up: 7 mm solid nodule, right lower lobe, on CT abdomen/pelvis 6/9. Radiology recommends chest CT in 6–12 months. 6/11 09:30, EHR message to Dr. Ruth Lindqvist, PCP, naming the nodule and the recommended scan; her reply 11:05: "Got it, will order at his follow-up visit." Discussed with patient at bedside 11:40: told it is probably harmless but needs a repeat scan to be sure, and that Dr. Lindqvist will order it. He said: "So another scan in six months to a year, and she sets it up." Report copy given; finding listed in his discharge instructions.

The rewrite shows Mr. Iverson hearing about the nodule in plain words, with a reason not to panic and a reason not to forget, and his instructions carry it home. Dr. Lindqvist's reply is the moment ownership changes hands. Without one, the note would say whom Simone called next. For a patient with no primary care clinician, it would name who will order the scan, and that may be the team that found it.

## When the patient has already left

The third case is fictional too. At 11:00 on a Saturday, Joy Kessler, an urgent care NP, sees Ana Gutierrez, thirty-four, with burning on urination. She sends a urine culture, prescribes trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and writes the pending test into the plan: "Urine culture pending; clinic will call if treatment needs to change. Cell number confirmed." That is the deferred-test plan from the previous chapter, applied to a result.

On Monday at 14:20 the culture posts: *E. coli*, resistant to the drug she went home with. Joy is on shift, and she records each step as an addendum to the Saturday visit when she takes it:

> Addendum, Mon 14:30. Urine culture from Saturday: E. coli, resistant to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (prescribed), sensitive to nitrofurantoin. Antibiotic needs changing. 14:35 called cell: no answer; voicemail asked her to call clinic, result not stated.
>
> Addendum, Mon 16:50. Second call, no answer. Portal message asking her to call. If not reached by Tue 12:00: call emergency contact on file, then letter per clinic policy.
>
> Addendum, Tue 09:10. Reached patient by phone, identity confirmed. Still burning; no fever, back pain or vomiting. Told culture shows the first antibiotic will not treat this infection. Nitrofurantoin sent to her pharmacy; told to stop trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. ED for fever, back or side pain, or vomiting. She said: "I'll pick it up at lunch."

The entries were written one at a time, and the system stamped each at the moment it describes. Together they show a result seen within ten minutes and pursued until it reached the patient the next morning. The middle entry holds the plan for a patient who cannot be reached, written before anyone knew it would be needed. How many attempts, by what means, and whether a letter goes by certified mail are set by your organization's policy; ask your risk manager. Had the culture posted on Joy's day off, the note would have to show who reviewed it instead, which belongs to the chapter on handoffs and cross-cover.

## What the note must contain

- **The result and its reviewer.** The value, when it posted or was phoned in and to whom, and when you saw it.
- **The action.** What you decided about the result, including a decision to change nothing, with its reason.
- **Who was notified.** The person you reached, how you confirmed who they were, what they were told and what they said back; when another clinician takes over, that clinician by name and their reply.
- **The method and the time.** Phone, portal, bedside or letter, each attempt with its time, failed ones included.
- **The plan if the patient cannot be reached.** The next attempt, its method and its deadline, written before you know you will need it.

# The Delayed Scan and the Bed That Never Opens

## A scanner out of service

One CT scanner serves the whole community hospital. At 09:25 on a Friday Bianca Ellison, an NP in its emergency department, sees Victor Langley, sixty-seven, who has had cramping abdominal pain since 06:00. He has vomited four times and has passed no stool or gas since the night before. In his thirties he had open surgery for a ruptured appendix. His abdomen is distended and tender all over, without guarding or rebound.

The hospital, Bianca and Mr. Langley are drawn for this book, from no real record. She suspects a small bowel obstruction. At 09:40 she orders blood work and a CT of the abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast, and starts IV fluids, an antiemetic and morphine. At 09:55 the CT technologist calls: the scanner is out of service, and the repair should be done by noon. It is back at 12:40.

The scan, read at 14:05, shows an obstruction without signs that the bowel is losing its blood supply, and the general surgeon recommends managing it without an operation. The hospitalist accepts Mr. Langley at 15:20, but there is no medical bed. In this hospital, by policy, the emergency clinician keeps writing orders for an admitted patient until a bed is assigned. His is assigned at 20:35.

## What the record will be asked

The order and the scan each carry a timestamp, so the gap between them is on the timeline before anyone writes a word. The questions start there. What did you know about the delay, from whom and when, and what estimate were you given? What did you do in the meantime, for the condition you suspected rather than the one the scan later confirmed? And how often did you look again, and what did you find each time?

Behind them sits the standard-of-care question from the chapter on what a chart becomes after a bad outcome, asked now of the wait itself. Waiting is a decision whenever another path exists, such as a scanner elsewhere or a surgeon at the bedside before the images. A reviewer will ask whether a reasonable NP, knowing what you knew at each hour, would have kept waiting. *Boarding*, holding an admitted patient in the emergency department because no inpatient bed is free, adds one more question: who was responsible for him while he waited? Your organization's policy decides; the note says what it decided.

## The note as written

At 20:40, with the bed assigned, Bianca writes:

> 67M abd pain, vomiting, prior abd surgery. CT delayed, scanner down. CT: SBO, no ischemia. Surgery saw, recs nonop mgmt, NGT. Admitted to hospitalist, boarding in ED awaiting bed. Pt resting comfortably, VSS. Continued to monitor.

More than eleven hours fit into one clause and two reassurances. "CT delayed, scanner down" does not say when Bianca learned it, what she was told or whether she looked for another way to get the scan. "Resting comfortably" and "VSS," for vital signs stable, carry no time, and the nursing flowsheet shows a heart rate of 112 at 12:20; a reviewer will look for her response to that number. "Continued to monitor" gives no interval, no findings and no limit that would have ended the wait. Had his bowel perforated at 18:00, nothing here would show that anyone examined him between the scan and the bed.

## The rewrite

In the rewrite, every step of the wait has its time, from the first exam to the bed:

> 67M, cramping abdominal pain since 06:00, vomiting x4, no stool or flatus since last night. Open surgery for ruptured appendix, age 34. Seen 09:25: T 98.9°F, HR 98, BP 138/84. Abdomen distended, diffusely tender, no guarding or rebound. Assessment: suspected small bowel obstruction, likely adhesive; compromised bowel not excluded. Plan: NPO, IV fluids, ondansetron, morphine; labs and CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast ordered 09:40.
>
> 09:55. CT technologist reports scanner out of service, repair expected by 12:00. Nearest other CT 40 min by ambulance; transfer for imaging would not be faster than the repair estimate. 10:10 Dr. Marisol Gaines, general surgery, told by phone of suspected SBO and CT delay; will see after CT, sooner if called. RN Theo Chandler to call me for HR above 110, systolic BP below 100, T 100.4°F or higher, worsening pain or more vomiting. Will call Dr. Gaines for guarding or rebound, constant pain or fever.
>
> 11:00. Pain 4/10 after morphine 4 mg IV at 10:05; no further vomiting. HR 92, BP 132/80. Abdomen unchanged. WBC 11.8, lactate 1.4. Continue to wait for CT.
>
> 12:25. Called by RN for HR 112, pain 8/10. Pain still cramping and intermittent. Abdomen unchanged, no guarding or rebound; T 99.1°F. Morphine 4 mg IV 12:30; at 12:50 HR 101, pain 4/10. Scanner back in service 12:40, one trauma scan ahead of him. Continue to wait.
>
> 14:05. CT: small bowel obstruction, transition point in right lower quadrant; no sign of ischemia, no free air. 14:40 seen by Dr. Gaines: nonoperative management, NG tube, surgery to follow. NG placed 14:55, 400 mL bilious output.
>
> 15:20. Accepted by Dr. Naomi Harrow, hospitalist. No medical bed; house supervisor: third in line, no estimate. Per hospital policy, ED orders and reassessment continue until a bed is assigned; RN call limits as at 09:55. Patient and wife told: admitted, no bed or estimate yet.
>
> 17:15. HR 88, pain 2/10. Abdomen softer, less distended. NG 300 mL since placement. Plan unchanged.
>
> 19:10. HR 86, pain 2/10. Abdomen soft, mildly tender. NG 150 mL since 17:15. Plan unchanged.
>
> 20:35. Bed assigned. 20:40 report by phone to Dr. Harrow: course, NG output, exam at 19:10.

## Times, findings and decisions

Each reassessment has the same three parts: a time, the findings that bear on the danger named at 09:25, and a decision. The findings are chosen, not complete: at 17:15 Bianca records the pulse, the pain, the abdomen and the tube output, which are what would show the bowel getting worse. The decision is written even when it is to keep waiting, because "continue to wait" can be judged against the findings beside it, and "continued to monitor" cannot.

The 09:55 entry does the most work. It records the delay as a fact, with its source, its time and its estimate. It shows the alternative Bianca weighed and the surgeon who now knew. And it turns the sentence about what would change your mind, from the chapter on what you don't know yet, into limits set in advance, with a nurse to watch for them. At 12:25 the limits work as intended: the nurse calls when the pulse crosses one, Bianca comes, and the entry shows why she does not yet call the surgeon.

How often to look again is your clinical judgment, set by how fast the danger you named could move; a reviewer will set your entries beside the flowsheet and look at what happened between them. An entry written after the fact is labeled as the chapter on templates and the late addendum describes. "Scanner out of service, repair expected by 12:00" belongs to the timeline; a remark about radiology is commentary, which the chapter on facts and quotes keeps out of the chart.

## The transfer that stalls

Another constructed case moves the wait to a forty-bed rural hospital, where Corinne Abbott, a former flight nurse, works as a hospital medicine NP. Elaine Burke, fifty-eight, was admitted on Sunday with right upper abdominal pain and jaundice. Her ultrasound shows a dilated bile duct, her bilirubin is rising, and the plan is an ERCP, an endoscopic procedure to clear the duct, which this hospital does not offer. By Monday evening the chart could say "Accepted at Regional, awaiting bed. Pt and family aware." Some of Corinne's entries show what that line would hide:

> 10:15. Transfer requested for ERCP, not available here. 10:50 accepted by Dr. Isaac Farrow, gastroenterology, Regional Medical Center, pending bed; no estimate. 13:40 Regional: no bed yet. University Hospital called as an alternative: no bed.
>
> 14:00. T 98.8°F, HR 84, BP 126/78, alert. RUQ tenderness unchanged. Bilirubin 4.1 (3.6 at 05:00). Waiting remains reasonable; reassess every 2 h. For fever, rigors, systolic BP below 100 or confusion: blood cultures, antibiotics, call Dr. Farrow about priority and air transport. RN to call me for any of these. If no bed by 20:00, will call both centers again.
>
> 16:30. Patient and daughter Megan Burke told: accepted, no bed yet, checks every 2 h; tell her nurse at once for fever, shaking chills, feeling faint or confusion. Megan asked, "What if she gets worse while she waits?" Told: antibiotics here and a call to Regional about moving her sooner.
>
> 18:20. Bed assigned. Ground ambulance earliest 22:00 per dispatch, crew on another transfer. 18:35 Dr. Farrow by phone: air transport not needed while stable. Patient told 18:45; she said, "Fine, as long as I get there tonight."

Acceptance is not a bed. Who accepted, when, and what the acceptance is pending are separate facts, and the bed gets its own time when it appears. Transport is a second delay, with its own source and estimate, and how she travels is a decision; Dr. Farrow's view is recorded as his, with the time he gave it.

The alternatives, a second center and air transport, each have an answer, so a reviewer asking whether there was a faster route finds that Corinne looked for one. If Mrs. Burke worsens on the road, Megan will be asked what the hospital told her, and the 16:30 entry is the record's side of that answer. The 14:00 entry also holds the plan for the bed that never opens: an hour at which Corinne stops waiting and calls again, written before anyone knows it will be needed, like the plan for the unreachable patient in the chapter on closing the loop on the abnormal result.

Sometimes the bed does not open before your shift ends. The last entry is then the handoff: its time, the clinician taking over, by name, and what you told them about the danger you named, the limits, the latest findings and when to look again. If Regional still had no bed at 20:00, Corinne's note would end with both calls and their answers, then her report to the night clinician, whom the nurse would now call. From that minute the record would show who was responsible for Mrs. Burke.

## What the note must contain

- **The delay as a fact.** What is delayed, who told you and when, the estimate and each change to it, with no comment on the department or facility.
- **The interim plan and its owner.** What you did for the condition you suspected, and who was responsible for the patient while they waited.
- **The limits that end the wait.** The findings that would make you stop waiting, who watches for them and what you will do.
- **Each reassessment.** Its time, the findings that bear on the danger and the decision, including the decision to keep waiting.
- **For a transfer.** The accepting facility and clinician, when they accepted and what it is pending, transport and its estimate, the alternatives considered, what the patient and family were told, and when you will act if the bed does not open; and, if your shift ends first, the handoff: time, the clinician by name, what you told them.
