# When You Say No to an Antibiotic or a Scan

## An antibiotic request before a long drive

Last winter Scott Garrity had two colds like the one he has now, and another clinic gave him azithromycin for each. At 15:10 on a Thursday in February, with his nose blocked since Saturday, the forty-two-year-old sees Holly Bianchi, NP, at a family practice; both of them are fictional. His sore throat and cough have faded; the yellow discharge and pressure over both cheeks have not changed since Tuesday. He has had no fever.

His temperature is 99.0°F. His cheeks are mildly tender and his nasal lining swollen, with purulent discharge; his eyelids look normal, his eyes move without pain, and his neck is supple. At 15:25 he asks: "Every time I get this, it turns into a sinus infection. Can you just call in a Z-Pak? I'm driving to my mother's in Ohio on Saturday, and I'll be there a week."

Holly does not call in the antibiotic. He leaves at 15:45 with a different plan, not entirely happy with it.

## What the record will be asked

A declined request is a test or drug left out with a witness: the patient knows exactly what he asked for, and the chart shows it only if you write it down. Suppose that next Wednesday Mr. Garrity is in an Ohio emergency department with a swollen, red eyelid. A reviewer will ask three things of Thursday's note. Was the request heard: what did he ask for, and why? Was the reason explained: what made an antibiotic unnecessary that afternoon, and what was he told? And what was offered instead: what did he leave with, and what would change the answer?

The same questions can come with no complication at all. A patient who leaves feeling dismissed can write to your board, and the complaint, as the chapter on where claims begin explained, needs no injury behind it.

## The note as written

At 15:50 Holly writes:

> 42M sinus congestion x 6 days. Requesting azithromycin. Viral URI, abx not indicated. Educated on antibiotic stewardship. Supportive care. RTC PRN.

The request survives, but not the two things inside it that the rest of the visit answered: a belief that his colds always turn bacterial, and a week away from the clinic. "Abx not indicated" is a verdict with nothing underneath, like "No indication for CT head" in the chapter on the test you order and the test you don't. "Educated on antibiotic stewardship" says the conversation was about resistance in the community, not about him; read beside an Ohio emergency record, it says nothing about the risks of his own illness. "Supportive care" names no treatment, and "RTC PRN" gives no threshold. Nothing records his answer, so a reader cannot tell a patient who agreed from one who left unconvinced.

## The rewrite

Holly could have written this instead:

> 42M, nasal congestion since Sat (day 6). Sore throat, cough days 1–2, resolved. Since Tue, thick yellow discharge and pressure over both cheeks, unchanged, not worse. No fever at home. Two similar illnesses last winter, azithromycin elsewhere both times. No drug allergies. Seen 15:10: T 99.0°F. Mild bilateral maxillary tenderness; nasal mucosa swollen, purulent discharge. No eyelid swelling or redness; eye movements full and painless. No severe headache; neck supple.
>
> 15:25, asked: "Every time I get this, it turns into a sinus infection. Can you just call in a Z-Pak? I'm driving to my mother's in Ohio on Saturday, and I'll be there a week."
>
> Assessment: acute rhinosinusitis, day 6, most likely viral. Bacterial sinusitis considered, IDSA criteria absent: under 10 days, no worsening after initial improvement, no high fever with severe symptoms at onset. Discolored discharge does not distinguish viral from bacterial. Spread to orbit or brain considered: no eye findings, no severe headache, neck supple.
>
> Plan: no antibiotic started today. Explained: most likely a virus; an antibiotic would not shorten it and can cause diarrhea or a rash; usually improves within 10 days. Saline rinse twice daily, fluticasone nasal spray daily, ibuprofen or acetaminophen per label. Away 8 days from Sat, so delayed prescription: amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg twice daily for 5 days, #10, no refills, sent to his chosen pharmacy near his mother's home. Not azithromycin: not recommended for sinusitis because of resistance. Pick up and start only if no better at all by Mon (day 10), or worse after starting to improve: new fever, more face pain or discharge. If started, portal message to me. Same day to an ED for eyelid swelling or redness, pain moving an eye, double or blurred vision, severe headache, stiff neck, confusion, or T 102°F or higher. He said: "I'd still rather have it now. But if I'm not better by Monday, or it gets worse again, I pick up the pills and message you. Anything with my eye or a bad headache, I go in."

## The request, the reason and the answer

His words come first, quoted by the rules in the chapter on facts and quotes, because they hold what the plan answers. The line on discolored discharge answers his belief, and the delayed prescription answers his trip. Shrink the quotation to "requesting azithromycin" and both lines lose their reason.

The decision is written as Holly's, with a date on it: "no antibiotic started today." "Antibiotics deferred" would hide that anything was asked for, and "patient declined antibiotics" would put the choice in the wrong hands. "Today" keeps it a judgment about day 6 that a later day may revise.

The reason sits in the assessment, built as the chapter on the differential describes. It names the diagnosis the request aimed at and gives a finding for each criterion Holly applied, from the Infectious Diseases Society of America's guideline on acute bacterial rhinosinusitis; the dangerous diagnosis gets reasons of its own. The explanation is recorded as what Mr. Garrity heard about his own illness: what the drug would not do for him and what it could do to him. Resistance can be part of that conversation, but a reviewer who knows about the eyelid looks for this patient's risks.

## What he leaves with instead

A no that ends the visit reads as a dismissal; a no followed by a plan reads as care. Symptomatic treatment, named and with its frequency, shows a man who left with something for what bothered him.

A *delayed prescription*, written today to be used only if something changes, is still a prescription, and the chapter on prescribing applies to it in full, down to why his drug differs from the one he named. It adds one element: what that something is. Holly's conditions are the bacterial criteria from her assessment, turned into a date and symptoms he can recognize: the sentence about what would change your mind, from the chapter on what you don't know yet, handed to the patient. A delayed prescription suits a patient who cannot easily come back; for one who can, a recheck on a set day may serve better. The portal message lets the record show whether and when he started.

The second list means be seen today, not start the pills: in this scenario, signs of an infection spreading toward the eye or brain call for an exam that day, not tablets at home. The note keeps the two lists apart, as the conversation did.

"I'd still rather have it now" stays in the note. A note showing a patient who disagreed, understood and left with a plan holds up better than one saying he agreed, because a complaint letter would confirm the first and contradict the second. If after the discussion you do prescribe, write the clinical reason: "per patient request" is a drug without an indication.

## The scan he wanted now

Wendy Cho's visit, invented like Holly's, is about a scan. She is a primary care NP, and at 09:30 on a Monday she sees Dale Henning, thirty-nine, whose lower back has hurt since he lifted a box at work on Thursday; the pain reaches his right buttock, not his leg. He wants an MRI and, if it shows a disc, a spine surgeon. Part of her note reads:

> 09:40, asked: "My brother waited on his back and ended up needing surgery. I want to know now." No fever, weight loss, cancer history, injection drug or steroid use; no numbness between the legs, bladder or bowel change, or leg weakness. Strength, reflexes and sensation normal both legs; straight-leg raise negative. Assessment: acute low back strain; nerve root compression, cauda equina syndrome, infection and cancer considered, not supported by findings above. MRI and surgical referral not ordered today: neither would change the plan; disc changes are common on MRI in people without back pain. Accepted: physical therapy referral, light-duty note for 2 weeks, heat, keep walking. Recheck booked in 4 weeks; MRI reconsidered then if not improving, sooner for pain or numbness spreading down a leg or leg weakness. ED at once for numbness between the legs, trouble passing urine, or loss of bladder or bowel control. He said: "If my leg goes weak or I can't pee right, I go in. Otherwise we talk MRI in a month."

A request for a scan often carries a fear, and Mr. Henning's has a name: his brother's surgery. The reason not to scan is what it could add for this patient now: nothing the plan would act on. The fear gets its own answer: the signs that would bring the scan forward, which he can watch for himself, and a date when it comes up again. That date makes the no a deferral with an owner, as the chapter on the test you order and the test you don't asks of any test that waits. The referral is declined on the same grounds, and what he accepted is written as accepted: a patient kept in care, not sent away.

## What the note must contain

- **The request.** The patient's words and the concern behind them.
- **The decision.** What you did not prescribe or order, written as your clinical choice, with its date.
- **The clinical reason.** The diagnosis the request aimed at, with the findings that set it aside, and the dangerous alternative with its own.
- **The explanation and the reply.** What the patient heard about their own illness, and their answer, disagreement included.
- **What was offered instead.** Symptomatic treatment, a delayed prescription with its conditions, a referral or recheck, and what was accepted.
- **The signs that change the answer.** Those that mean start the delayed plan, and those that mean be seen today.
- **The way back.** Who reassesses and when, and how the record will learn the delayed plan was used.
# Hostile Encounters in Neutral Words

## A son who wants a hundred percent

"He can't breathe. I want him on a hundred percent." Craig Lowell flew in on Saturday afternoon to see his father, Eugene, seventy-four, admitted on Friday night with an exacerbation of COPD, and he wants the oxygen turned up. His father's blood gas showed carbon dioxide building up, so the orders set an oxygen saturation target of 88 to 92 percent. At 18:30 the nurse, Hector Avila, calls the hospital medicine NP, Rebecca Dorsey, to the room.

The scene, like everyone in it, is imagined. Rebecca examines Mr. Lowell, who says he feels about the same as that morning, and explains the target. Craig swears, asks for "a real doctor, not a nurse," and at 18:50 slaps the overbed table and says he will be waiting for her in the parking lot. Hector calls security, and Craig leaves the unit with an officer at 19:00.

## What the record will be asked

If Mr. Lowell is moved to intensive care at 03:00, and his son says he warned the staff and nobody listened, a reviewer will ask three things of the evening. What happened: what did the son report, and what did the clinician find when she looked? What was said, and by whom? And were the decisions driven by clinical facts? Pressure can bend care either way, toward oxygen turned up to end an argument or toward a real change dismissed because of who reported it. The note has to show that neither happened.

## The note as written

At 19:20 Rebecca writes:

> Called to bedside re: son demanding O2 be increased. Son extremely hostile and aggressive, cursing at staff, refusing to listen to medical explanation. Explained multiple times that pt is a CO2 retainer. Son demanded "a real doctor." Became threatening; security called, son escorted off unit. Pt stable on 2 L. Incident report filed. Continue current O2.

The note is about the son. The patient appears twice, as "a CO2 retainer" and as "stable on 2 L." The second is the only clinical answer to the complaint, and it has no saturation, no breathing rate and no exam, nothing to show whether Rebecca looked at Mr. Lowell or at the monitor. "Hostile," "aggressive" and "refusing to listen" are verdicts that the chapter on facts and quotes replaces with what was seen and heard. "Became threatening" leaves out the words, so nobody can judge whether calling security was a measured response. The request for a physician is quoted, and nothing shows it was answered. And the incident report is named in the chart, which that chapter ruled out.

## The rewrite

In the rewrite, Mr. Lowell comes first and his son second:

> 18:35. Seen at request of RN Hector Avila. Sitting up, full sentences. SpO2 90% on 2 L NC, RR 22, HR 94. Scattered expiratory wheeze, no accessory muscle use; unchanged from my 08:15 exam. Alert, oriented, answers promptly. Patient: "I'm breathing about like this morning." Admission ABG Fri 21:40: pH 7.33, PaCO2 58; SpO2 target 88–92% ordered.
>
> Son, Craig Lowell, at bedside, speaking loudly; swore several times. Asked: "He can't breathe. I want him on a hundred percent." With patient's permission, explained to both: oxygen kept in this range because more can raise his carbon dioxide and make him drowsy or confused; level in range, breathing unchanged since morning. Son asked for "a real doctor, not a nurse." 18:42 called Dr. Philip Greer, attending: gave exam and request. Agrees with current target; will see patient and speak with son at 19:30.
>
> 18:50. Son struck overbed table with his palm and said to me: "If he dies tonight, I'll be waiting for you in the parking lot." Security called by RN; son left unit with officer 19:00. Patient: "He's scared. But I don't want yelling in here." Asks that Dr. Greer update son by phone tonight.
>
> 19:15. SpO2 89%, RR 20, HR 90; resting, alert. Assessment: COPD exacerbation, day 2, unchanged; oxygen within target. Plan unchanged: 2 L NC, target 88–92%. RN to call me for SpO2 below 88%, RR above 28, drowsiness or new confusion.

The rewrite treats the son's alarm as a report about a patient and answers it with an exam. That is the line a reviewer will look for first, and the confrontation made it the easiest one to skip. The comparison with the morning exam says what "stable" only claimed. The reason for the target sits beside the blood gas that set it, so the unchanged oxygen reads as a clinical decision, built as the chapter on the antibiotic or scan you decline describes.

The request for a physician is answered like any request: who was called, when, and what came of it. The note quotes the remark and says nothing about it. And Mr. Lowell, alert and answering, speaks for himself.

## Threats, security and the behavior agreement

The 18:50 entry is the easiest to get wrong. Without it, the chart shows a worried son removed from his sick father's room for no stated reason. Inflated, it fails as the weak note did. The threat goes in once, in its exact words, because here the words are the fact. Beside it goes what it changed for the patient: who left, when, and what Mr. Lowell asked for afterward.

Everything else goes through your organization's channels. The security report, the incident report and a workplace violence report, where one exists, hold the full account, from the officers' names to any injury. An escort to your car, and how shaken you were, are for your manager and security. A complaint from the son, if one comes, is answered through patient relations or risk management, never in the chart.

On Sunday the nurse manager and security meet with Craig and set up a **behavior agreement**. This is a written statement, made with a patient or visitor whose conduct has disrupted care, of what is expected from now on and what follows if it is not met. Who writes it and where it is filed are set by your organization's policy. Your note records what it changes about care, such as who visits and who receives updates, and that Mr. Lowell agreed: "Per patient's wishes, Dr. Greer updates son by phone daily at 11:00."

Then let the episode end. If "son hostile, security involved" is copied into each progress note, it becomes a label that travels, of the kind the chapter on facts and quotes described, ready to explain whatever happens next. If your organization uses a safety flag in the chart, the warning lives there. And the care does not change: the reassessments after 18:50 should come as often as those before.

## The request that keeps coming back

Gemma Lockhart, an urgent care NP, sees Neil Carver, fifty-one, on day three of a cold. Mr. Carver's visit is invented too. He has type 2 diabetes and wants a steroid shot. She declines, with reasons written as the chapter on the antibiotic or scan you decline describes. He asks again ten minutes later, then a third time, louder, standing by the door. Her first draft argues back:

> Pt repeatedly demanding steroid injection despite multiple explanations. Became argumentative. I spent 20 minutes patiently explaining why it is not indicated. Pt would not accept plan.

"Repeatedly" and "despite" turn a conversation into a contest. "Patiently" grades Gemma's own manner, and a reader told that a clinician was patient wonders why it needed saying. "Would not accept" is a verdict, and the answer she gave, the part a reviewer needs, is missing. Her rewrite keeps the sequence and drops the commentary:

> 10:20 asked for steroid injection: "I always get one for this." Not given today: viral URI, day 3. Explained a steroid would not shorten a cold and would raise his blood sugar for several days. Offered saline rinse, acetaminophen; recheck if not improving by day 10. 10:30 asked again; same answer. 10:40, standing, voice raised: "I'm not leaving without that shot." Answer unchanged; offered review by the physician on site, declined. Left 10:45 with written instructions.

The timestamps count the requests, and one observed behavior and one quotation carry the escalation. The clinical reason appears once, where it was first given. Repeating it at each request would make the note longer without making it stronger. The offered second opinion, with his answer, shows a decision open to review rather than a personal refusal.

## When the disagreement is with a colleague

The last case, just as hypothetical, puts the disagreement inside the team. In an emergency department, NP Natalie Corbin sees Lorraine Abernathy, sixty-three, who fainted at her kitchen sink. Natalie would admit her for cardiac monitoring; Dr. Gordon Hale, the attending supervising her shift, thinks the faint was orthostatic. The tempting line, "Dr. Hale overruled my recommendation to admit," charts the conflict and leaves out every clinical reason on both sides. Part of her note, written as clinical content, reads:

> 15:40. Syncope, discussed with Dr. Gordon Hale, ED attending, 15:20. Admission for telemetry considered: no warning before the faint; ECG first-degree AV block, not present on 2022 ECG. Dr. Hale examined patient 15:30. His assessment: orthostatic syncope; BP 134/80 lying, 108/70 standing, with lightheadedness; hydrochlorothiazide started 12 days ago. His plan: discharge, stop hydrochlorothiazide, ambulatory heart monitor, cardiology clinic within 1 week.

The option Natalie favored stays in the note as an option considered, with her findings behind it. The decision appears with its owner and his reasons, as the chapter on the provider note asks of any physician's role. Nothing records tone or "against my advice." A disagreement with a nurse or a consultant is written the same way: each of you records what you saw and did, and neither note answers the other.

If, having heard the reasons, you still believe a plan leaves the patient at risk, the route is the chain of command, with its steps recorded as in the chapter on the consultant who never comes. A concern about a colleague's conduct, rather than about this patient's care, goes to your manager or to peer review. And an addendum written days later to put your objection on the record reads the way the chapter on templates and the late addendum warned: like a record built for an argument.

## What the note must contain

- **The report and your look.** What was raised, by whom, and your exam or review in response, with times.
- **Each request and your answer.** The request in the person's words, and the clinical reason, given once.
- **The behavior, observed.** What was said and done, without adjectives, and a threat in its exact words; the rest of any incident goes in your organization's reports.
- **What changed for the patient.** Security, visiting and updates, and the patient's own wishes.
- **The physician asked for.** Who was called, when, and what they did.
- **For a disagreement.** The option considered, the findings behind it, the decision and its owner, and any escalation; never the conflict.
# The Capacity Question, Decision by Decision

## A no at 16:40

Albert Yates, eighty-four, was admitted on Tuesday at 10:30 after three days of black stools. On admission he was oriented and conversant, and gastroenterology recommended an upper endoscopy, or EGD, for Wednesday at 09:00. His NP is Tessa Novak, a former geriatric nurse. He, his family and everyone caring for him exist only on these pages.

At 16:40 a GI fellow brings the consent form. Mr. Yates pushes it away: "Nobody's doing anything to me." Since 15:30 his nurse, Imani Shaw, has charted that he is pulling at his IV line and calling her by his late wife's name. The fellow pages Tessa with one question: can he refuse?

At 17:05 Tessa finds him drowsy and certain he is waiting at a bus station. She explains the procedure in plain words; two minutes later he asks, "What test?" His vital signs are steady, and nothing has to be decided tonight. At 07:35 Wednesday she goes back. He is clear-headed, and he still says no.

## What the record will be asked

If Mr. Yates bleeds again on Thursday, the chart will be asked four things. Capacity for which decision: this endoscopy, not his ability to run his life. Assessed how: what he was told, what he was asked and what he answered. Assessed by whom: which clinician, and who else confirmed or disputed the finding. And when: the time of each assessment, set against a mental state that changed by the hour. The last is the sharpest: readers who already know the ending, as the chapter on what a chart becomes after a bad outcome explained, will lay a confused evening beside a refusal accepted the next morning and ask what changed overnight.

## Capacity, not competence

**Decision-making capacity** is a clinical judgment that a patient can make one particular decision at one particular time. The treating clinician makes it at the bedside, and it can change by the afternoon. **Competence** is a legal status decided by a court, which may appoint a **guardian** (called a conservator in some states) to make some or all decisions for a person it finds incompetent. The words are often used loosely, but on the record the difference matters: "lacks capacity" with no decision attached reads as a verdict on the whole person.

Adults are presumed to have capacity, so the finding of incapacity is the one that needs support. Neither a diagnosis nor an orientation check supplies it: a man with delirium can know the date and still not grasp that the bleeding is his. A choice you think unwise is not evidence either, though a serious refusal is a reason to look closely.

The assessment rests on four abilities, each judged against the decision at hand. *Understanding* is taking in what is proposed, why, its main risks and the alternatives, and giving it back in one's own words. *Appreciation* is accepting that this applies to oneself: this illness, these risks. *Reasoning* is weighing the options against each other and against what matters to the patient, and reaching a choice that follows. *Expressing a choice* is stating one that holds steady long enough to act on.

## The note as written

Tessa's two entries, written between pages, read like this:

> 17:20. Pt refusing EGD. Confused, A&O x1. Lacks capacity. Son notified. Will readdress in AM.
>
> 08:05. Pt A&O x3 today, has capacity. Continues to refuse EGD. Risks explained. GI aware.

Orientation carries both findings, and orientation is not one of the four abilities. Neither entry says what Mr. Yates was told or records a word he said. Read together, they show a clinician who found him incapable on Tuesday and capable on Wednesday, with nothing but the clock to explain the change. If he bleeds on Thursday, a plaintiff's attorney will supply the explanation: a confused man said no twice, and the second time nobody looked closely.

## The rewrite

Rewritten, the two entries hold nothing beyond what Tessa asked, heard or did:

> 17:05. Capacity assessment for one decision: EGD recommended by GI for 09:00 tomorrow, declined when consent sought at 16:40. Oriented and conversant on admission at 10:30; per RN I. Shaw, since 15:30 pulling at IV and calling her by his late wife's name. Now drowsy, says he is "at the bus station." Explained purpose and main risks of EGD in plain words. Understanding: could not say why he is in the hospital; 2 min later asked, "What test?" Appreciation: "I'm not sick. I need to get home." Reasoning: gave no reason related to the procedure. Choice: says no each time asked. Assessment: lacks capacity at this time for this decision, due to fluctuating delirium. Decision can wait: HR 84, BP 128/72, no melena since 12:00, Hb 8.4 at 16:00. Reassess 07:30, before procedure. Son Paul Yates, his health care agent per document in chart, updated by phone 17:30; agrees to wait for morning reassessment.
>
> 07:35. Capacity reassessment, same decision. Oriented to place, date and reason for admission; recalls "the young doctor with the papers." Explained again. Understanding: "You put a camera down my throat to find the bleeding and maybe stop it. They put me under, and it can tear something." Appreciation: "I've had black stools since Sunday and my blood's low. It could stop by itself, or it could get worse." Reasoning: "My wife choked on one of those tubes and was never right after. If I bleed more, you can give me blood, and I can always change my mind." Choice: declines EGD; unchanged at end of discussion. Assessment: has capacity at this time to decline EGD; deficits found at 17:05 not present. Son, by phone 07:50 with patient's permission: father has refused "any tube down his throat" since his wife's death. Will reassess if mental status changes.

Each entry opens with the decision and gives each ability its own line, in his words wherever he had any, quoted by the rules in the chapter on facts and quotes. The evening deficits are specific enough that their absence in the morning means something: the reader sees a patient who changed, not a clinician who changed her mind. Tessa may not share the morning reasoning, and she does not need to: the choice follows from facts he understood and a value he has long held. The son's call supports that history without standing in for his answers.

## Asking, and asking again

Those answers exist because of how the questions were put. "Do you understand?" invites a yes that proves nothing. Open questions get answers you can quote: *Tell me in your own words what we are suggesting. What do you think is happening to you? What could happen if you don't have it? How did you decide?* Use hearing aids, glasses and a professional interpreter as needed, so the deficit you record is the patient's, not the room's. When an answer is wrong, write the wrong answer: "What test?" tells a later reader more than "poor understanding."

Fluctuation changes the timing, not the method. When a decision can wait, assess at the patient's best hour and write why waiting was safe. Each reassessment is a new entry with its own time, naming the decision again and saying what changed. A finding of capacity at 07:35 does not stretch to a different answer given at 21:00, after the confusion returns; a finding of incapacity at 17:05 does not bind the morning. If you write one up hours later, label it as the chapter on templates and the late addendum describes.

## The surrogate and their choice

Suppose that at 07:35 he had still asked "What test?" while his hemoglobin kept falling. The decision could not wait for a better hour, and it would pass to a **surrogate decision-maker**: the person who decides for a patient who lacks capacity. The note has to show three things about that person.

The first is who they are and where their authority comes from. A **health care power of attorney**, called a health care proxy in some states, is a document in which a patient names a **health care agent** to decide if they cannot; Mr. Yates named his son in one. A court-appointed guardian, or conservator in some states, is another source. If no one was named, state law, where it addresses the question, and your organization's policy set who may decide. Some documents and some laws also say who must certify incapacity before an agent's authority begins, so ask your risk manager whether your finding is enough or a physician must make it.

The second is what they were told: the same information the patient would have received. The third is what they chose and on what basis. A surrogate is generally expected to decide as the patient would have, from the patient's known wishes and values, a standard called **substituted judgment**; when those wishes cannot be known, the surrogate decides by the patient's **best interests**. The exact rule depends on state law. Either way, record the basis in the surrogate's words, including how they know what the patient wanted: "He told me after Mom died he'd never let them put a tube down his throat."

## Psychiatry and the ethics committee

Most capacity assessments are made by the treating clinician. Many clinicians ask psychiatry when the question is hard: when a psychiatric illness such as severe depression or a psychotic disorder may be distorting appreciation, when your findings are borderline and the stakes high, or when someone disputes your conclusion. Put the decision in the consult request, "Does Mr. Yates have capacity to refuse EGD?" rather than "capacity eval." Record the question, the consultant's findings and what you did with them; the consult informs your decision without taking it off your hands.

The ethics committee answers a different question: not whether the patient can decide, but what to do when the people deciding disagree, or when nobody can. Common reasons are a surrogate whose choice seems to contradict the patient's known wishes, relatives who disagree, and a patient without capacity and without a surrogate. In most hospitals the committee advises rather than decides. Record when you asked, who took part, what was recommended and what followed.

## What the note must contain

- **The question.** The decision assessed, named specifically, and the time of the assessment.
- **The information.** What the patient was told, and any interpreter or sensory aid used.
- **The assessment.** Each of the four abilities, with the patient's own answers, right or wrong.
- **The conclusion.** A finding limited to this decision at this time, with its likely cause.
- **The timing.** Whether the decision could wait; each reassessment, timed, with what changed.
- **The clinicians.** Who assessed, and any psychiatry or ethics consultation: question, answer, action.
- **The surrogate.** For a surrogate: name, relationship, source of authority, what they were told, what they chose and why.
