```html
<p><b>After a bad outcome, your note is read as the record of a decision, and the people reading it already know how the story ended. Nursing school and years at the bedside taught you to chart what you observed. Nobody taught you to write down a judgment: the alternatives you weighed, what you did not know yet, and why you chose what you chose.</b></p>
<p>This casebook is for nurse practitioners in their first years of practice in the United States, and for final-year NP students. It works through the provider-level moments where a note is most exposed: the test you order and the one you don't, the abnormal result, the consultant who never comes, the capacity question, the patient leaving against medical advice, the discharge, the handoff, the telehealth visit, the prescription and the request you decline.</p>
<p>Each scenario follows the same shape, so you can find your way through it in the middle of a shift: the questions the record will be asked later, a weak note set beside a defensible rewrite of the same encounter, and the short list of elements that note has to contain. A phrasebook, a scenario finder indexed by setting, and a plain-English glossary of legal terms close the book.</p>
<p>The goal is a note that shows what you saw, what you considered and why you decided, written in your own words and in the time a shift allows. In this book, "defensible" describes a note that makes your reasoning visible, not a legal result. No note makes anyone immune to a lawsuit or a board complaint, and this book does not pretend otherwise. It is not legal or clinical advice, laws and standards differ by state and by employer, and every scenario is constructed for teaching.</p>
<p><b>What you will find in this book:</b></p>
<ul><li>How a provider note is read after something goes wrong, and why that reading differs from the way your nursing notes were read</li><li>How to write the differential you actually ran and a working diagnosis that says what would change your mind</li><li>How to document a decision not to test or treat, so the record shows what you weighed and why you chose as you did</li><li>How to record refusals, departures against medical advice and capacity findings in the patient's own words</li><li>What belongs in the chart after an adverse event, what belongs in your organization's incident report instead, and what to ask your risk manager when a records request or a complaint arrives</li></ul>
<p><b>Keep it where you chart, and let your next note show the thinking you already do.</b></p>
```
