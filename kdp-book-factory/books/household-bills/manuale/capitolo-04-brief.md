## Temi da trattare
- Why bills slip through, and why it is rarely laziness: due dates scattered across the month, notices arriving by paper, email and app, an autopay nobody remembers, paydays that don't line up with due dates.
- The master list: finding every recurring bill (statements, bank and card history, the quarterly and annual ones) and what to note for each: payee, amount (fixed or variable), due date, how it is paid, from which account, and whom to contact.
- Where the system lives: a paper notebook, a spreadsheet or the bank's built-in tools. The strengths and weak points of each type without naming products, plus keeping statements and account access in one safe place.
- The bill calendar: lining due dates up with paydays, asking a provider to move a due date, and handling biweekly paychecks and irregular income.
- Autopay done carefully: what is worth automating and what to watch by hand, paying the minimum versus the full balance, the account that funds it, overdraft risk, and how to change or stop an automatic payment.
- Reading a bill and a statement: statement date versus due date, grace period, minimum payment, past-due balance, estimated versus actual meter readings, and fees, explained in general terms.
- The irregular and forgotten bills: annual renewals, insurance premiums, property tax, registrations, subscriptions and free trials, and setting a little aside each month so they don't land all at once.
- The weekly check and the monthly review: a short routine to confirm what has cleared, catch errors and double charges, see what is left after the fixed bills, and a yearly review to carry the system into the next year.
- When a payment slips by mistake: a missed due date, a late fee, a returned payment, an overdraft. What to do in the first days, how to ask for a fee to be waived, and how late payments can reach credit reports. The details vary, so the book says where to check.
- When the money isn't there: what is at stake with each kind of bill (housing, utilities, insurance, secured loans, credit cards, medical), calling providers before the due date, hardship and payment plans, utility assistance programs, shutoff protections that vary by state, nonprofit credit counseling, and how high-cost quick loans work and what they cost. Information only, without judgment.
- Fake bills and payment scams: shutoff-threat calls demanding immediate payment, fake invoices, look-alike emails and texts, and how to check with the real provider before paying.
- Sharing the job at home: couples splitting the bills, teaching a teenager, taking over for an aging parent or a partner who can no longer do it, and leaving clear notes on where everything is if the usual bill-payer is suddenly unavailable.

## Quello che il libro dichiaratamente NON fa
- No pages to fill in: no grids, blank logs or checklists to complete. The book explains how to set up your own register on paper, in a spreadsheet or in the bank's tools, and does not print one. Readers who want a notebook to write in should buy a notebook.
- No daily spending diary: the book does not serve the reader who wants a page left open on the counter.
- No personal financial advice. It explains how bills, fees and options work, and refers the reader to a nonprofit credit counselor, an attorney or the provider when the situation calls for one.
- No promise of savings or results: no 'never be late again', no dollar amounts saved.
- No state-by-state figures on late fees, grace periods, shutoff protections or credit reporting. Where these vary, it says so and tells the reader where to check.
- No banks, apps or services named as recommendations: it describes types of tools, not products.
- No investing, retirement planning, wealth building or tax preparation.
- No debt-payoff method and no credit repair program.
- No small-business bookkeeping: household only.
STRUTTURA COMPLETA DEL LIBRO
Tesi portante: Bills are usually paid late because the setup is scattered, not because the person paying them is careless: due dates, notices and paydays each run on their own schedule, and nothing brings them together. One list, one home for it, a calendar tied to paydays and a short routine bring them together, and knowing what each bill puts at stake and whom to call gives the reader a calm next step when a payment slips or the money isn't there.
1. Introduction — Perché questo libro, per chi è, cosa ottiene il lettore.
2. Why Careful People Still Pay Bills Late — Opens the book with the everyday reasons a bill gets paid late: due dates spread across the month, notices arriving by paper, email and app, an autopay nobody remembers, and paydays that don't line up with due dates. It comes first because a reader who stops blaming themselves can start fixing the setup instead.
3. Paid, Due or Missing Without Relying on Memory — Sets out the goal the whole book works toward: being able to see for every bill whether it is paid, due or missing, without relying on memory. It lays out the parts of the system the reader will build and what order can and cannot do, so the method that follows has a clear finish line.
4. Tracking Down Every Bill Your Household Pays — Shows how to find every recurring bill, including the ones that never arrive in the mailbox, by working through bank and card history, statements, email and the household's own routines. It comes before anything is set up because a system built on a partial list fails on the bill that was left off.
5. Reading a Household Bill Without Guessing — Walks through a typical utility or service bill line by line, so the reader knows which date and which amount really matter. It comes before the master list is filled in because the list can only be as accurate as the reader's reading of the bills behind it.
6. What a Credit Card Statement Is Telling You — Explains the parts of a credit card statement that decide whether a payment counts as on time and what carrying a balance involves. It follows the household bill because cards run on different rules, and the reader needs those rules before recording cards or choosing how they get paid.
7. Recording the Details That Matter for Each Bill — Turns the rough list into a master list by setting out what to record for each bill: payee, amount, due date, how it's paid, from which account and whom to contact. It follows the reading chapters because each detail now has a clear meaning, and it comes before the home is chosen because what the list holds decides what the home must do.
8. Picking a Notebook, Spreadsheet or Your Bank's Tools — Compares the three types of place a bill system can live (a paper notebook, a spreadsheet and the tools built into a bank's website or app) without naming any product. It then explains in words how to set up whichever the reader picks so that paid, due and missing show at a glance.
9. A Safe Spot for Notices, Statements and Logins — Gives every incoming notice and stored document one destination, and makes it a safe one: a spot for paper mail, one folder for billing emails, useful alerts, and secure storage for statements and account access. It closes the building part because the list stays current only if everything that feeds it arrives in one place.
10. Lining Up Due Dates With Your Paydays — Builds the bill calendar by placing every due date against the days money actually arrives, so the reader sees which paycheck covers which bill and where the tight weeks fall. It opens the part on timing because autopay and every later routine rest on this calendar.
11. Biweekly Paychecks and Income That Won't Sit Still — Adapts the calendar for households whose money doesn't arrive on the same dates each month: biweekly pay, gig and commission work, seasonal jobs and benefits paid on their own schedule. It follows the basic calendar because these are the cases where a simple payday map stops working.
12. Choosing What Goes on Autopay and What Doesn't — Sets out how to decide, bill by bill, what to automate and what to keep paying or checking by hand, including how the amount of a card's autopay is set. It comes after the calendar because a sensible autopay depends on knowing when the money will be there.
13. Staying in Control of the Account Behind Autopay — Covers the account that funds automatic payments: which account it is, what happens when it runs short, and how overdraft coverage works in general terms. It ends with how to change or stop an automatic payment cleanly, which every household needs once more than one is running.
14. The Bills You Forget Until They Arrive — Offers light guidance on what to remember: insurance premiums, property tax, registrations, annual renewals, subscriptions and free trials that don't follow the monthly rhythm. It comes once the monthly machinery is in place because these are the bills that slip past a system built only for monthly ones.
15. Setting a Little Aside for the Irregular Bills — Explains the simple idea of setting a little aside each month so the irregular bills don't land all at once, and the types of place that money can be kept. It follows the forgotten bills because once they are on the calendar, the reader can see what they add up to over a year.
16. A Short Weekly Check of What Cleared and What's Due — Describes a brief weekly routine: confirming what has cleared, looking at what is due before the next check, and dealing with whatever has arrived since the last one. It opens the routine because a small habit repeated often keeps the list in step with the bank account.
17. A Monthly Review of Statements and What's Left Over — Sets out a longer monthly look that matches statements to the list, catches errors, double charges and price increases, and shows what is left after the fixed bills and the set-aside. It is where the bill system becomes a simple picture of the household's month, the home-budget use many readers want, without a daily spending diary.
18. The First Days After a Missed Payment — Gives a calm sequence for the days right after a payment is missed by mistake, returned by the bank or turned into an overdraft. It opens the part on trouble because the most common slip is usually also the simplest to set right when it's handled early.
19. Fee Waivers and What Reaches Your Credit Report — Explains how to ask a provider or bank to waive a late or overdraft fee, and how a late payment can reach the credit reports. It follows the first days because once the bill is paid, these are the two questions the reader is usually left with.
20. What Each Bill Puts at Stake When Money Runs Short — Explains without judgment what is at stake with each kind of bill when there isn't enough to pay them all: housing, utilities, insurance, secured loans, credit cards and medical bills. It comes before any call is made so the reader understands what each bill puts at stake, as information and not as instructions.
21. Asking for More Time Before the Due Date — Walks through the call to a provider before a payment is missed: how to prepare, what to ask for, and what hardship programs and payment plans generally involve. It follows the chapter on stakes because the reader now knows what each bill puts at stake and can decide which calls to make.
22. Finding Utility Assistance, Counselors and Legal Aid — Maps the help available outside the company that sent the bill: utility assistance programs, shutoff protections that vary by state, nonprofit credit counseling, housing counselors and legal aid. It follows the provider call because these programs often work alongside a payment plan, and the provider is often the one who points to them.
23. Comparing What Payday and Other Quick Loans Cost — Explains, as information and without judgment, how payday loans, auto title loans, pawn loans, rent-to-own, paycheck advance apps and buy-now-pay-later plans work, and how to compare what they cost. It closes the part on money running short because these offers tend to appear just when other options seem used up.
24. Spotting a Fake Bill Before You Pay It — Describes the payment scams aimed at households: shutoff-threat calls demanding immediate payment, fake invoices, and look-alike emails and texts. It opens the last part because a well-kept list is also a quick way to recognize a bill that doesn't belong.
25. Sharing the Bills With a Partner or a Teenager — Covers the household where more than one person pays, or soon will: partners and housemates splitting bills, and a teenager learning how bills work. It comes late in the book because sharing a system is easier once there is a system to share.
26. Taking Over the Bills for an Aging Parent — Explains how to take over the bills for an aging parent or a partner who can no longer manage them, and how to leave clear notes so someone could step in for you. It follows the chapter on sharing because a handover is sharing under pressure, and it goes more smoothly when it's prepared in advance.
27. A Yearly Review to Get Ready for Next Year's Bills — Closes the book with a yearly review that carries the system into the next year: renewals and annual bills rolled forward, the list and calendar refreshed, old records archived. It comes last because this review is what lets the routine carry on from one year to the next.
28. Conclusion — Chiusura del ragionamento e primi 30 giorni di applicazione.
