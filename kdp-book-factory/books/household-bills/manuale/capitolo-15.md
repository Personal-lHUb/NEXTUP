# Setting a Little Aside for the Irregular Bills

Once the yearly and seasonal bills are on your calendar, something new becomes visible. They are no longer surprises, but they are still lumpy. A car insurance premium of several hundred dollars can share a month with a property tax installment and a renewal you had half forgotten, while the months on either side carry nothing unusual at all. Knowing the dates tells you when these bills will land. It doesn't change how hard they land.

The idea here is an old and simple one. You take the bills that arrive a few times a year or once a year, add up what they cost over twelve months, and move a small, steady share of that total aside each month. When a big bill comes due, the money for it has been gathering quietly in the background. It is arithmetic, plus a place to keep the result.

## Adding up a year of irregular bills

Start from the calendar you have already built, and pick out every bill on it that doesn't come monthly. For each one, note what it cost the last time and how often it comes around. A premium paid twice a year counts twice. A water bill that arrives every quarter counts four times. Add the lot together and you have what your irregular bills cost in a year. Divide by twelve and you have the monthly share.

Omar rents an apartment and drives an older car. His irregular bills are car insurance at $480 every six months, a registration fee of $96 once a year, renters insurance at $180 a year and one annual subscription at $108. Over twelve months that comes to $960, $96, $180 and $108, or $1,344 in all. Divided by twelve, it is $112 a month. That is the number the exercise exists to produce: a sum small enough to move every month, covering bills that would otherwise arrive as a string of jolts.

Not every irregular bill costs the same each time, though. Insurance premiums change at renewal. Property tax changes when the assessment or the local rate changes. A quarterly utility bill rises and falls with the weather. For bills like these, last year's amount is only a starting point. One common approach is to use the highest amount from the last year or two, or to round the monthly share up to a figure with some room in it. Omar, for instance, might set aside $120 instead of $112, which leaves $96 of slack over a year for a premium that comes back higher.

When a renewal notice arrives with the new amount, that is the moment to redo the sum for that one bill. A bill you have never paid before, such as the first property tax bill on a new home, has no history at all. The company or office that sends it can usually tell you what to expect, and county and city tax offices often publish due dates and past amounts. A figure written down with the word "estimate" beside it is still more useful than a blank.

## Where the set-aside money can sit

The money needs a home that keeps it apart from what you spend day to day. Left in the same checking account as everything else, it tends to look available, and an ordinary month can quietly use it up. There are three broad types of place for it.

A **separate savings account** keeps the money most clearly apart. It has its own balance, so a glance at checking shows only what is free to spend, and depending on the account it may earn a little interest. The drawbacks are practical. Moving money back to checking before a bill is due can take a day or more, especially between two different banks. Some banks also limit how many withdrawals or transfers you can make from savings in a month, or charge a fee past a certain number. The account terms say what applies to yours.

A **labeled sub-account** is a feature some banks build into their website or app: one balance split into named portions, such as "car insurance" or "property tax." It makes it easy to see how much has built up for each bill. The weak point is that the separation is sometimes only on the screen. Depending on the bank, the labeled money may still count as part of your checking balance, which means an automatic payment or a card purchase could draw on it without asking. The bank can tell you whether a labeled portion is a separate account or just a name on the same money.

A **cash envelope** is the most tangible of the three. The monthly share goes into an envelope marked with the bill it's for, and you can see and count what has built up. Some people find this the easiest method to keep going. It also carries the most risk. Cash can be lost or stolen, it earns nothing and no deposit insurance protects it. Many irregular bills, insurance premiums and property tax among them, can be paid in cash only in person, if at all, so the money usually has to reach a bank account before the due date. Cash kept at home needs as safe a spot as your statements and logins.

Whichever type you choose, it helps to keep a note in your bill system of how much of the set-aside belongs to which bill. When all of it sits in one account or one envelope, that note is what tells you that $300 is for the car insurance and $90 is for the renewal.

## Starting when there's little to spare

Twelve monthly shares assume a year's head start and room in the month for the full amount. Many households have neither. The next big bill may be six weeks away, and the combined share may be more than the month can hold.

There is a smaller way in: set aside for the nearest big bill first and leave the others for later. Rita's calendar shows her car registration due in two months at $150, her renters insurance due in six months at $210 and an annual membership due in ten months at $60. Saving for all three at once would take about $116 a month from the start. Instead, she divides the registration by the two months left and puts $75 aside each month. Once it's paid, she turns to the renters insurance, now four months off, and sets aside about $53 a month. The membership comes after that. The most she needs in any month is $75.

This works because each target is small and its finish date is close enough to see. Every bill that gets covered frees up its share for the next one in line. Over time, as the month allows, the later bills can start gathering earlier, until each one has its full cycle to build up in.

Sometimes even the nearest bill needs more each month than there is room for. That is better learned early than on the due date. Many insurers allow a premium to be paid monthly or quarterly instead of all at once, sometimes with an installment fee, and some counties and cities split property tax into installments. The rules depend on the insurer and the local tax office, so their website or a phone call will tell you what is possible for your bill.

## When a big bill arrives before the money has caught up

It happens. The set-aside started in September and the car insurance is due in November. A renewal comes back higher than planned. The envelope was needed for something else in the spring. The bill is here, and the money gathered for it falls short.

Take Omar again. Say he starts setting aside $120 a month in September, and his car insurance renewal arrives for November at $560 instead of the $480 he planned on. By then he has $240 put by. The other $320 has to come from somewhere, and the choices are few: the current paycheck, if it can carry it; an installment plan, if the insurer offers one; or a request to move the due date. Needing one of them is not a failure. The set-aside did its job: it made the gap $320 instead of $560.

Drawing the set-aside down is exactly what it's for. What matters is how it starts again afterward. The simplest way is to redo the sum from today: take each irregular bill still ahead, count the months before it's due, and work out what it needs each month to be covered on time. Right after a big withdrawal, that total often runs higher than the usual share for a while, because the next bills have fewer months to gather in. If it's more than the month can hold, the nearest-bill-first approach works here too. Omar's registration, due next, gets its share first, and the others follow in date order.

It helps to update the note that says which bill owns which part of the money, so it shows plainly which bills the withdrawal left short. An empty pot says nothing about the person who emptied it. If the money went to something that was never on the list, a car repair or a medical copay, that was yours to weigh, and the restart works the same way.

## This week's step

Open your calendar and find the next irregular bill on it, the soonest one that doesn't come every month. Look up what it cost last time, or what the renewal notice says it will cost now, and count the months between today and its due date. Divide the amount by the months, and that is its monthly share. Then choose where that money will sit: a separate savings account, a labeled portion of an account you already have, or an envelope with the bill's name on it. If a sub-account is your choice, find out this week whether it is truly apart from your checking balance. Finally, add the bill, its share and the place to your bill system, so the first amount you set aside has somewhere to be counted.
