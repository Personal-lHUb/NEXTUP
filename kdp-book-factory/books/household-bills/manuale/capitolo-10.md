# Lining Up Due Dates With Your Paydays

Will the money be in the account on the day a bill is due? Your master list can't say: it shows what each bill costs and when it is due, not what the balance will be that day. A due date is a fixed point set by the company that sends the bill. A payday is a fixed point set by whoever pays you. The two schedules were never designed to fit together, and nobody but you is in a position to line them up.

The bill calendar is where they finally meet. It doesn't need to be clever. It needs to show, on one page or one screen, the days money arrives and the days money has to leave. Seen together, those two sets of dates tell you which paycheck is carrying which bill and where the thin stretches of the month fall.

## One month, two kinds of dates

Start with a single month view. It can be a blank page in your notebook with the days numbered down the side, a new sheet in your spreadsheet, or the calendar inside your bank's bill-pay tool. Put the paydays in first, and make them stand out with a box, a different color of ink or bold type. Everything else on the page will be read against them.

Use the day your pay actually becomes usable, which isn't always the official payday. Some employers deposit early when payday falls on a weekend or a holiday, and others deposit on the next business day. Your payroll office, or a few months of bank history, will show which pattern yours follows. What matters for the calendar is the day the money is there to spend.

Then copy every due date from your master list onto the month, with the name of the payee and the usual amount, or a rough range for bills that vary. Draw a line across the page just above each payday. The stretch between one line and the next is a pay period, and the deposit at its top is the first place to look for the money for everything due inside it.

Add up the bills inside each stretch, and look for two patterns. One is a pay period where the bills eat most of the deposit, leaving little for groceries, gas and everything else. The other is a cluster of due dates in the last few days before the next payday, when the account is at its lowest. Either one marks a tight week. It is the kind of week in which a bill gets paid late even though nobody forgot it: the due date simply came after the money had gone elsewhere.

## Which paycheck pays which bill

Take, as an example, Luis. He is paid on the 1st and the 15th, about $2,000 each time after taxes. His rent of $1,250 is due on the 1st, the car payment of $320 on the 3rd, car insurance of $140 on the 8th, his phone at $75 on the 12th, and electricity, which runs between $90 and $160, on the 13th. His credit card payment is due on the 19th and the internet on the 24th.

On his month view the problem is plain. The first pay period carries rent, the car, insurance, the phone and electricity, which together take nearly the whole deposit at its top. The second carries two bills. For months Luis had been paying the electric bill two days late, on the 15th, and blaming himself for it. The calendar shows that the bill simply sat in the tightest week of his month.

The fix starts with assigning each bill to a paycheck on purpose instead of by habit. Rent due on the 1st shows why. Paying it from a deposit that arrives the same morning leaves no margin, so Luis assigns rent to the paycheck of the 15th before it and leaves that amount untouched in the account until the 1st. The paycheck of the 1st now covers the car, the insurance, the phone and electricity. The paycheck of the 15th covers the card, the internet and next month's rent. The split isn't even, since rent alone is most of a paycheck, but the money for every bill is now in place before its due date.

The same reasoning applies to any bill due just before a payday. Electricity on the 13th, two days before the next deposit, belongs to the paycheck of the 1st. The safer moment to pay it is soon after that paycheck lands, not on the 12th, when the balance is at its lowest. Luis's electric bill usually arrives near the end of the previous month, so he now pays it on the 2nd. Paying early works only once the bill for that period has been issued, so the payment is applied to the right amount; with a variable bill, that means waiting until the actual bill is in hand. Leave room, too, for the time a payment takes to reach the provider: each company explains on its bill or website how and when payments are credited, and the method you use, whether online, by phone or by mail, changes how many days that takes.

Weekends matter here too. When a due date falls on a Saturday, a Sunday or a holiday, some providers count a payment on the next business day as on time and others expect it before the weekend. The bill or the provider's billing page usually says which. For credit cards, a federal rule settles part of this: if the card company doesn't accept mail on the due date, a mailed payment arriving the next business day can't be treated as late. For other methods, the cardholder agreement explains how your card handles it. Until you know, the cautious choice is to treat the earlier day as the due date.

## Asking to move a due date

Sometimes no amount of reassigning makes a tight week comfortable. Then it's worth asking whether the due date itself can move. Many providers allow it, and asking takes a few minutes.

Look first in your online account, under billing or payment settings, since some companies let you pick a new date there. Otherwise, call the customer service number in your master list. Be specific: say which account, which date you'd like instead and, if it helps, the day your pay arrives.

Then ask what you need to know. Is the change possible, and which dates are available? When does it take effect, and what will the next bill look like? Is there a limit on how often the date can change, and does anything you've already scheduled for the old date need adjusting? Before you hang up, note the date of the call, the name or ID of the person you spoke with and what they said.

Policies differ because billing systems differ. Credit card companies often let you choose a date within a range. Utilities usually bill on a cycle tied to when your meter is read, so some can move the date only by moving you to another cycle, some run a program that lets you pick a date, and some can't change it at all. Loan and mortgage payment dates are normally set in the loan agreement, though some lenders will adjust them on request. Rent is set by the lease, so any change is something to agree with your landlord and get in writing. These are tendencies, not rules, and the only answer that counts is the one your own provider gives you.

Expect the first bill after a change to look unusual. To reach the new date, the billing cycle has to stretch or shrink once. Move a due date later and the next bill may cover more days than usual, so it comes in larger; move it earlier and it may cover fewer days and come in smaller. A flat-rate service may add or subtract a partial month to even things out. The change can also take a cycle or two to go through, so until a bill actually shows the new date, keep paying by the date printed on the latest one.

Beth is paid once a month, on the 1st. Her credit card was due on the 27th, when that paycheck was nearly a month old and her account was running low. She found the option in her card account and moved the due date to the 6th. The statement that followed covered about five weeks of purchases instead of four, so the balance on it was larger than usual, but it came due after payday, which was the point. Her phone company told her the date on her account couldn't be moved, so she leaves that bill due on the 25th and pays it in the first week of the month, as soon as it's issued.

## One home for the list and the calendar

A calendar kept apart from the master list will sooner or later disagree with it. Suppose Luis moves a due date, updates his list and forgets the calendar on the refrigerator, and a month later the calendar is the one he checks. The fix is to keep both in the home you already chose for the system, so that a single change updates everything you look at.

In a notebook, put the month view on the page right after the list and update both in the same sitting. In a spreadsheet, you can sort the list itself by day of the month and add a row for each payday; the list then becomes the calendar, and there is no second copy to fall out of step. If the system lives in your bank's bill-pay tools, its calendar shows the payments scheduled there but not the bills you pay elsewhere, such as rent paid by check or a card paid on the card company's own site. Those need adding by hand, or at least a note in the same place, so the picture is complete.

Whenever a provider changes a date, whether you asked for it or not, update the list and the calendar on the same day and note when the change takes effect. If the two ever disagree, trust the most recent bill, then correct both. The bill is the provider's own statement of when it expects payment, so it outranks anything you wrote down earlier.

## This week's step

Take this month and map it. Mark the days your pay actually lands, copy every due date from your master list onto the same view, draw a line above each payday and add up what each pay period has to carry. Find the tightest week, the one where the bills are heaviest or bunched just before the next deposit. Then choose one provider whose due date falls in that week. Look for a due-date option in your online account or call the number on your list, and ask whether the date can be moved and what the next bill would look like if it were. Whatever the answer, record it in the same place as your list and calendar.
