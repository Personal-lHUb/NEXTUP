# A Safe Spot for Notices, Statements and Logins

Where did your last bill first reach you, and how long did it sit there before you saw it? Your list stays accurate only if what feeds it keeps arriving where you can find it. A bill buried in a pile of catalogs, a renewal notice sent to an email address nobody opens, a text about a new due date lost under a dozen others: each is a gap between what is happening and what your list shows. Closing those gaps takes fewer places to look, not more attention.

Carla keeps her master list in a spreadsheet and updates it faithfully. Her water bill comes by mail, her phone bill by email, and her car insurance through an online account that texts her when a document is ready. Each arrives somewhere different, and the spreadsheet knows only what Carla carries to it. The list is sound. What fails is the path in.

## One spot for paper

Paper mail needs one landing place, the same one every day. A tray on a shelf, a basket near the door or a wall pocket in the kitchen will all do. What matters is that anything that looks like a bill, a statement or a provider's notice goes there the day it arrives, before it can mix with flyers and school forms. If others in the house bring in the mail, tell them where the spot is; two small piles in two rooms are how a notice goes missing.

The spot is a waiting room, not storage. Once you have opened a notice and moved what matters onto your list, the paper leaves: into the file for that bill, into the shredder, or into the recycling if it carries nothing personal. If the tray is empty, your list is current. If it isn't, you can see exactly how much catching up there is.

## One folder for billing email

Email has the opposite problem. Nothing piles up where you can see it, so a bill can sit unread for weeks. The answer is the same: one destination. Most email programs let you create a folder and a rule, sometimes called a filter, that moves messages from a chosen sender into it automatically. Make a folder with a plain name such as "Bills" and add a rule for each provider on your master list. A rule catches only the senders it knows, so when a bill turns up in the main inbox or in spam, add that sender to the rule instead of moving just that one message.

Some households open a separate email address used only for bills. It keeps billing messages apart from everything else, but it is one more inbox to remember, and an address that quietly stops being checked is the very problem you are trying to solve. A folder inside the account you already read every day is the lighter option; a separate address can make sense when two people share the job and both need to see the same messages.

## Making sure every notice can reach you

The best routing does nothing if the provider is sending to the wrong place. People move, change phone numbers, stop using an old email account or lose a work address when they change jobs. The provider may not find out unless someone tells it. A postal change of address catches some paper mail for a while, but not email or phone numbers, and from the provider's side the notice went out as usual.

Devon took out his renters insurance years ago using an email address he has since abandoned; everything else moved to his new address, but the policy never did. The renewal notice, with a higher price for the coming year, went to the old address. He found out when a larger payment than usual left his checking account. Nothing was hidden from him. The message simply went where he no longer looked.

For each provider on your master list, confirm three details: the mailing address, email address and phone number on file. Most online accounts show them on a profile or settings page; otherwise, call the customer service number on a recent bill. Check again after any move or change of number, since that is when these details go stale.

## Paper or electronic, bill by bill

Many providers encourage electronic statements, and some switch you to them automatically when you set up an online account. Neither format is better in general. What matters is which one you will actually see, and the answer can differ from one bill to the next.

Electronic statements arrive quickly and take no space, and the provider usually keeps them in your online account for a while, though how long varies. They depend on an email address that works and someone who opens it. Paper statements are harder to overlook and easy to file, which suits a reader who rarely checks email or thinks better with a page in hand. They take longer to arrive, and some providers charge for mailing them; the account terms or the provider's website will tell you whether yours does.

You don't have to pick one format for the whole household. Take Carla again. She reads email on her phone every day but rarely opens paper mail before the weekend, so her utilities and phone now send statements electronically. Her mortgage statement and yearly insurance documents she keeps on paper: they are records she wants in a file, not reminders she needs to see that day. A retiree who checks email once a week might choose the opposite, and that is just as sound. For each bill, ask which version you will have seen before the due date, and note the answer on your master list so you know where to look when a statement seems late.

## Alerts worth having

Many banks, card issuers and utilities offer alerts by email, text or app notification. Used well, they bring the right fact at the right moment. Used carelessly, they become noise, and you learn to swipe away the one that mattered along with the rest.

Three kinds tend to earn their place. A **due-date reminder**, sent a few days before a payment is due, helps most with bills you pay by hand. A **payment confirmation** tells you a payment was received without your having to log in and check. A **low-balance warning** from your bank, triggered when the account drops below an amount you pick, gives you time to act before a payment reaches an account that can't cover it. What each provider offers, and how far ahead a reminder can be set, varies; check each account's alert settings.

To keep alerts from burying you, turn on only those that ask you to do something or confirm something you would otherwise have to check, and turn off the promotional messages that often come bundled with them. Let the urgent ones, such as a low-balance warning, arrive by text or notification, and send the rest to your billing folder. If you catch yourself dismissing an alert without reading it, it isn't doing its job; change it or switch it off.

## Keeping statements and account access safe

Gathering everything in one place also means protecting it in one place. Statements carry account numbers, your address and sometimes other identifying details, the kind of information someone could use to pose as you with a provider. Logins are more sensitive still, because they open the accounts themselves.

### A locked place for paper

Paper you decide to keep belongs in one file, sorted by provider, by year or both, in a drawer, cabinet or box that locks. It should not be the open tray by the door, which holds only what just arrived. Electronic statements worth keeping can go into one folder on your own computer or in a storage service you trust, organized like the paper file. Download them rather than leaving them on the provider's website, because accounts close, providers change systems and older statements can drop out of view.

### A secure way to store passwords

Passwords on sticky notes can be read by anyone who walks by, and the same password everywhere means that a leak at a single site puts every account sharing it at risk. A password manager, a type of program that stores your logins in scrambled form that only one strong master password unlocks, lets you use a different password for each account without memorizing them. Some people prefer a small notebook kept in the locked file; it can't be reached over the internet, but it can be lost and must be updated by hand. Whichever you use, keep passwords off the master list itself, which is meant to be seen and, one day, shared.

Where a provider offers two-step sign-in, which asks for a code sent to your phone as well as the password, it is worth considering. A stolen password alone isn't enough to get in. That is one more reason to keep your phone number current with every provider, since the code usually goes to the number on file.

### What to shred, and what to check first

Before paper leaves the house, look for an account number, a Social Security number, a date of birth, a signature or anything to do with logging in. If you find any, shred it; a cross-cut shredder, which cuts pages into small pieces rather than strips, makes them much harder to piece back together. Envelopes and inserts carrying only advertising can go in the recycling.

Some records deserve a second look before they go. Statements that back up something on a tax return, papers tied to a home you own, bills caught up in a dispute or an insurance claim, and medical bills still being settled with an insurer may be needed later. How long to keep each depends on the record and the situation, and no single rule fits every household. If you are unsure, keep it in the locked file and ask someone who would know: the provider, a tax professional or, where there is a dispute, an attorney. A page kept a little longer costs drawer space; a shredded page can't come back.

## This week's step

This week, pick the spot for paper mail, put a tray or basket there, and tell everyone who brings in the mail where it goes. That is the whole step. The rest is optional and can wait for other evenings: a billing folder in the email account you read most often, with a rule for each provider on your master list, and a check of the mailing address, email and phone number each provider has on file, correcting any that point to an old home, an old number or an inbox you no longer open. If you get to that check, start with the bills you pay by hand, which rely most on a notice reaching you.
