# Spotting a Fake Bill Before You Pay It

People who send fake bills count on most households not knowing offhand who they pay or what they owe. Your master list tells you which companies you pay, roughly how much, on which day, and how each bill reaches you. That makes it a reference, and a reference is exactly what a fake bill can't survive. With your list in hand, checking takes a minute. The scams aimed at households come in a few familiar shapes, and each one tries to get you to pay before you look.

## The call that says your power goes off in an hour

Here is an example. Lynn is making dinner on a Thursday evening when her phone rings. The caller ID shows the name of her electric company. A brisk voice says her account is two months past due and a crew is on its way to disconnect her service within the hour. The only way to stop it, the caller says, is to pay $486 right now. Not by card on the company's website, but by buying gift cards at the store down the street and reading the numbers over the phone, or by sending money through a payment app to an "authorized agent."

Everything in that call is built around speed. The one-hour deadline is there so Lynn doesn't have time to open her list, see that her electric bill cleared eleven days ago, and call the company herself. The fear of sitting in the dark with children at home is real, and the caller counts on it. Urgency is the giveaway because a genuine provider has no reason to rush you past checking. A real past-due account leaves a trail: bills showing a past-due balance, written notices, and a process that runs over days or weeks. The exact steps before a disconnection vary by state, but they don't fit inside a single phone call.

The payment method is the second giveaway. Gift cards, wire transfers and payment apps that send money from person to person share one feature a scammer likes: once the money moves, it is very hard to pull back. A gift card number read aloud can be spent within minutes. A utility, insurer or lender you actually owe has ordinary ways to take payment and no reason to demand gift cards or an on-the-spot transfer to a stranger's account. When a caller insists, the insistence tells you who is on the line.

Caller ID, on the other hand, settles nothing. The name and number on your screen can be faked, a trick usually called spoofing, so a call can look as if it comes from your provider when it doesn't. The same goes for a caller who knows your name, your address or the last few digits of your account. Scraps of personal information travel widely, and knowing them proves nothing.

What Lynn can do is simple. She can say she will call the company back, hang up, and dial the customer service number from her list or a paper bill. If something is really wrong with her account, the real company will tell her. If not, she has lost a few minutes of dinner.

## Invoices for things you never ordered

Not every fake bill arrives with a threat. Some come quietly, looking dull and official, and rely on being paid along with everything else in the pile. Arturo, who pays the bills for his household, opens a letter headed "Renewal Notice." It shows an account number, a due date and $189 for another year of a home warranty plan, but when he checks his list there is no home warranty on it, and there never has been. The letter is a sales offer dressed up as a bill, sometimes with a line in small print admitting as much.

A close cousin is the notice for something you already pay somewhere else. A letter or email says a service is about to lapse, whether it's a magazine subscription, antivirus software or a roadside assistance plan, and invites you to renew through this sender. You may really have the service, but the sender is not the company you pay. Paying the notice means paying twice: once to your actual provider and once to someone who may deliver nothing at all.

A third version arrives by email: a receipt for a large charge you don't recognize, perhaps a few hundred dollars for a software renewal or an electronics order, with a phone number to call if you did not authorize the purchase. Here the goal isn't for you to pay the invoice. It's for you to call. The person who answers may offer to process a refund, ask for your card or bank details, or ask to connect to your computer to help. The invoice is only the bait.

Your list handles all three the same way. Before paying or calling anyone, ask three questions. Is this payee on the list? Is the amount close to what you normally pay? Does it reach you the way this bill usually does? A bill that fails one of these isn't necessarily fake, since prices change and new bills do start, but it has earned a closer look before a single dollar leaves your account.

## Messages that look exactly right

The most polished fakes come by email and text. They copy a real provider's logo, colors and wording, sometimes flawlessly. A text says your payment failed and service will be interrupted unless you update your details at the link. An email says your account is locked, that you're owed a refund, or that a charge needs confirming.

Don't judge these by how they look, because copying a logo takes seconds. Look instead at what the message wants you to do. Almost always, it wants you to tap a link or call a number inside the message itself, and that is the part to leave alone.

A link in a fake message usually leads to a page built to look like your provider's sign-in page. Its web address may be close to the real one, with a letter swapped, an extra word or a different ending, close enough to pass at a glance on a small screen. Whatever you type there, from a password to a card number, goes straight to whoever built the page. A phone number in the message works the same way. It rings someone ready to sound like customer service and to ask for exactly the details they need.

The safe habit is to treat every link and number inside an unexpected message as untrusted, even when the message later turns out to be genuine. If a text says there's a problem with your account, close it and reach the provider on your own. Type the provider's address yourself, use a bookmark you saved earlier, or call the number on your list. A real problem will still be there when you look. A fake one simply isn't.

## Checking with the real provider

All of this comes down to one move: you make the contact, using a number you already trust. That number should come from somewhere the sender can't reach. A paper bill that arrived before the message did, a statement, the back of your debit or credit card, or the provider's official website reached by typing the address yourself all qualify. So does your master list, as long as its numbers came from those same places.

When you call, you don't need a long explanation. Say you received a call, letter or message about your account and want to know whether it's genuine. Give your account number from your own records, not from the message. The representative can tell you whether any balance is due, whether any notice is on file, and whether anything needs doing. Many providers also want to hear about fakes that use their name, and may tell you where to forward the message.

Sometimes you'll learn the message was real. That's fine. You can pay it through the channel you always use, and you've spent five minutes making sure.

## If you already paid or shared details

These scams are run by people who do this all day, and they catch careful, organized people too. If you paid or gave out information, you did nothing shameful; you were targeted. What matters now is speed.

Start with your own bank or the company that issued your card. Call the number on the back of the card or on your statement, explain what happened, and ask what can be done: stopping a payment that hasn't gone through yet, disputing a card charge, or replacing the card. Whether money can be recovered depends on how it was sent and how quickly it's reported, and sometimes it can't be. For gift cards, contact the company that issued the card using the number printed on it, and keep the card and the receipt. If you shared a password, change it right away through the provider's real site, and anywhere else you used the same one. If you gave out your Social Security number or other details that could be used to open credit in your name, the Federal Trade Commission's identity theft site, IdentityTheft.gov, lays out the next steps, including fraud alerts and credit freezes.

Then report it. The Federal Trade Commission collects reports of fraud and scams, and your state attorney general's office, or in some states a separate consumer protection agency, handles complaints. Your local police can take a report too, which a bank may ask for. Let the real provider know as well, so they can flag your account. While it's fresh, write down the dates, the amounts, the number that called and what was said; each call goes faster with notes in hand.

## This week's step

Open your master list and look at the contact number beside each bill. For each one, ask where it came from. If you copied it from a paper bill, a statement, the back of a card or the provider's official website that you reached yourself, it can stay, and you might note its source next to it. If you aren't sure, or if it came from a text, a voicemail, an unexpected email or a search result clicked in a hurry, replace it this week with one taken from a real bill or the official site. The next time a call or message makes your heart race, the number you need will already be in front of you.
