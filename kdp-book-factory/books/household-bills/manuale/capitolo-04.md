# Tracking Down Every Bill Your Household Pays

A bill system can only keep track of the bills it knows about. If your list holds eleven of the twelve bills your household pays, everything can run smoothly for months. Then it fails on the twelfth bill, on the day that bill comes due and nobody is looking for it. So before anything gets set up, the job is to find every bill, and to do it without relying on memory to produce the list. Memory is good at the rent and the electric bill. It is poor at the charge that comes once a year, the one paid from a different card, and the one someone else in the house set up.

## Follow the money first

Your bank and card history is the most complete record your household has. It doesn't forget, it doesn't depend on a notice arriving, and it shows bills that never come in the mail at all, such as a subscription that renews quietly or an insurance premium drawn on its own. Open the history for the account most of your bills leave from, either online or on paper statements, and read down it slowly. You are looking for any payee that shows up more than once. Mark each one, even the small ones, because a $4.99 charge that repeats is still a bill.

Go back further than feels necessary. One month of history shows the monthly bills and very little else. Some water and sewer bills come every two or three months. Many auto policies run for six months, and a household that pays the premium in full sees that charge only twice a year. In many places property tax is due once or twice a year, and memberships, software and roadside assistance plans often renew once a year. Look only at last month and every one of these is invisible.

Twelve months is the useful minimum. It is the shortest stretch that contains every bill that comes around once a year. It also shows each quarterly bill repeating, which makes those bills easier to spot. How far back you can see online depends on the bank. If the screen stops short, older monthly statements go further back, and the bank can tell you how to get copies.

Two things make the history harder to read than it should be. The first is the name. The payee on a statement is often a shortened code, a parent company or a payment processor rather than a name you'd recognize. When that happens, compare the amount and the date with your email receipts until you find the match. The second is checks. A check written for rent or a quarterly water bill may show up as nothing more than a number and an amount. If your bank lets you view images of cleared checks, the image shows whom the check was written to. If it doesn't, your checkbook register will.

Take, as an example, Tomás, who pays most of the household bills from one checking account. Looking at a single month, he counts nine recurring payments and thinks he has them all. Scrolling back through the year, he finds three more: a water bill from the town every quarter, a car insurance premium in January and July, and a yearly charge for a roadside assistance plan he had forgotten signing up for. None of the three fell in the month he first looked at. All three would have been missing from a system built on that month.

## Every account, every card, every person

The history of one account shows only the bills paid from that account, and most households have more than one. There may be a second checking account, two or three credit cards, a store card, and an old card kept in a drawer that still carries a charge nobody remembers setting up. Each one needs the same slow read over the same twelve months. A recurring charge on a card is a bill twice over: once when it lands on the card, and again when you pay the card.

Then there are the other people in the house. If you share a home with a spouse or partner, some bills almost certainly leave from their accounts, and they may not think of those as household bills at all. The same goes for a grown child who covers the family phone plan, or a parent who still pays the car insurance. Nobody has to hand over statements or passwords for this. It is enough to sit down together while each person goes through their own history and names what repeats.

Tomás and his wife, Jean, share a joint checking account, and he had assumed it carried everything. When Jean went through her own card, she found three more payments. There was the renters insurance she had put on autopay when they moved in, a streaming service billed to her card since before they married, and a monthly payment to the dentist for a crown. None of the three showed up in the account Tomás had been treating as the whole picture.

## What your inbox and your files remember

Some bills leave a trace in writing before they leave one on a statement. Signing up for almost anything produces a welcome message, and most renewals produce a notice or a receipt. These messages point to bills that are still running, including recent sign-ups that haven't charged yet.

Search your email for words such as *renewal*, *receipt*, *subscription*, *invoice*, *auto-renew*, *payment confirmation* and *welcome*. Look in the promotions, spam and archive folders too, because automatic sorting often files billing messages there. If anyone in the house has an older email address they rarely open, search that one as well. Accounts set up years ago often still send their receipts to it. A welcome message for a free trial deserves a second look, because a trial that was never cancelled turns into a bill without anyone deciding to pay it.

Paper does the same work for the older and larger bills. Go through the drawer, folder or box where household papers tend to end up. An insurance policy usually includes a summary page, often called the declarations page, which shows the coverage period and the premium, and so tells you when the next payment falls due. Last year's property tax bill shows how often the tax comes due, a vehicle registration card shows when it expires, and loan paperwork shows the payment schedule. You don't need to organize any of it yet. Read each paper for one thing only: does it point to a payment that will come around again?

## A walk through the household, one category at a time

By now the list is long, but it comes only from where money has already gone and what has already been written down. The last pass works the other way, starting from how the household lives and asking what that life costs. Take each category in turn and ask two questions: is everything in it on the list, and if something is missing, who pays it and how?

**Housing and utilities.** Start with the rent or mortgage and everything attached to it: association dues, renters or homeowners insurance, property tax, a storage unit. Your mortgage may have an escrow account, meaning the lender or loan servicer collects part of the tax and insurance with each payment and pays those bills for you. If it does, put those bills on the list anyway and mark them as paid through the mortgage, because the payment is adjusted to them at a review, usually yearly. Then come the utilities: electricity, gas, water, sewer, trash. These are often split among several providers, and some are billed by the town or county rather than a company. If you rent, the lease usually says which of them you pay.

**Phone, internet and insurance.** The cell plan, home internet and any TV or landline service belong here. So does a monthly installment on a phone bought over time, which may appear on the phone bill or arrive as a separate bill. Insurance often includes more policies than come to mind: auto, home or renters, life, and any dental, vision or pet coverage. Health insurance taken out of a paycheck won't appear in the bank history. Note it anyway, marked as paid through payroll, so the list shows all the household pays.

**Loans, cards and vehicles.** Every credit card and store card goes on the list, along with car loans, student loans and personal loans. A student loan that is on pause also belongs here. The pause may be called deferment or forbearance, a stop or reduction in payments agreed with the lender or servicer; the chapter on asking for more time explains the difference. Interest may keep building meanwhile, and billing restarts on a date worth knowing. For each vehicle, think of the registration, which renews on a schedule your state sets, an inspection if your state requires one, and a toll account that refills itself from a card. Your state's motor vehicle agency can tell you the schedule and the rules.

**Health and subscriptions.** Look for prescriptions refilled on a schedule, payment plans with a hospital or doctor's office, and any health premium you pay directly rather than through work. Subscriptions hide easily, because they are small and renew on their own: streaming, music, news, software, cloud storage, gym and club memberships, delivery memberships. Anything bought through a phone's app store is often billed under the store's name, so a single line on a card can cover several subscriptions at once. The subscriptions page in the phone's account settings usually lists them one by one.

## The lines you can't place

A few entries will resist every attempt to identify them. Leave them on the list with a question mark rather than guessing or cancelling. The bank or card issuer can usually tell you more about the business behind a charge, sometimes its phone number, and a short call often settles it. If nobody in the house recognizes a charge, call now, before the list is finished, and ask how to dispute it in writing: federal protections come with deadlines counted from the statement, as the chapter on the monthly review of statements explains.

## This week's step

Choose one account, the one most of your bills leave from, and open as many months of its history as you can see, ideally a full year. On a sheet of paper or in a blank document, write down every payee that appears more than once. Next to each one, add a rough note of how often it seems to come: monthly, every few months, once a year. Check the list against your note from memory, "Not sure" items first. Don't sort anything, fix anything or cancel anything yet. Anything you can't place gets a question mark; if nobody recognizes it, call now. The other accounts, the conversation at home, the email search and the walk through the categories each get an evening before the chapter on recording each bill's details.
