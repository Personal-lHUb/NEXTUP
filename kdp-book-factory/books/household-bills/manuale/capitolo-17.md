# A Monthly Review of Statements and What's Left Over

The weekly check asks a narrow question: did it go through? Once a month, a longer look asks two broader ones. Was each charge right, and what did the month actually look like once the bills were paid? The first helps you catch charges you never agreed to. The second turns your bill system into a plain picture of the household's money, without a diary of every coffee and every tank of gas.

The review works best on a set day, a few days after most of your statements have closed, so the previous month is complete on paper or on screen. Depending on when your statements close, that might be the first week of the month or the second. Give it more time than the weekly check takes. The first review is the slowest; later ones go faster because you learn what a normal month looks like.

## Matching each statement to the list

Lay out what the month produced: the statement for each bank account, each credit card statement, the bills that arrived by mail or email, and your master list. Then work through the list one bill at a time. For each bill, find the payment on a statement and compare it with what the list says. The payee should be right, the amount should match the bill, the money should come from the expected account, and it should appear once.

Three kinds of problem tend to surface here that a weekly glance misses. The first is a plain error: an amount that doesn't match the bill, or a payment drawn from the wrong account. The second is a double charge, from the company or from the household itself: a merchant may bill twice for the same period, or an autopay may run in the same week someone paid the same bill by hand. The third is the quiet price increase: a subscription up by a couple of dollars, a premium that changed at renewal, an introductory rate that ended. Such changes are often announced, but the notice may sit in small print or in an email nobody opened.

Then turn the direction around. Read down each statement looking for anything recurring that isn't on your list. This is where a forgotten subscription, or a free trial that turned into a paid plan, finally shows itself: an unfamiliar name on the same date, month after month.

Take, as an example, Rosa, who does her review on the second Saturday of each month. Matching her statements against her list, she sees that her internet bill is seven dollars higher than the amount she recorded; the previous bill, which she hadn't read closely, mentioned that a promotional rate was ending. Her phone bill appears twice on her credit card statement, once from autopay and once from the evening she paid it herself because a reminder email made her think it was overdue. Scanning the card statement, she also finds a charge from a streaming service she was sure she had canceled in the spring. None of the three would have stood out in a weekly look at what cleared, because each payment did clear.

## Questioning a charge

When something looks wrong, the usual first step is to call the company that made the charge. Often the matter ends in one conversation: a duplicate is refunded, a cancellation that never went through is processed, a wrong price is corrected. Before you call, have the statement with the date and amount of the charge, your account number and any record you kept, such as a cancellation confirmation. Ask what the charge is for, what the company will do about it, and how and when the correction will appear. Write down the date, the name of the person you spoke with and what was agreed, and keep that note with your list.

Sometimes the call doesn't settle it, or the refund promised on the phone never shows up. Then the card issuer or the bank can step in. Federal law gives credit card holders a formal way to dispute billing errors, such as charges they didn't authorize, charges for things they never received, and mistakes in the amount. Bank accounts have a separate error process for electronic transfers, which generally includes debit card purchases and automatic payments taken from checking. Both come with time limits counted from the statement on which the problem first appeared, and some protections depend on raising the dispute in writing, through the address or channel the issuer names. The deadlines and steps differ between cards and bank accounts and are set out in your cardholder or account agreement, so check them with your card issuer or bank as soon as you know a charge is in question.

The two routes also feel different from the household's side. A disputed card charge is part of a balance you owe the issuer, not money already gone from checking; the issuer's terms explain what you still need to pay while the question is open. A disputed debit charge has already left your account, and whether and how quickly any of it comes back during the bank's review depends on that bank's process. In both cases, a problem caught soon after the statement arrives leaves the most room for whichever process applies.

## What's left after the fixed bills and the set-aside

With the statements matched, the same papers can answer a larger question. Take three figures from the month just ended. The first is the income that actually arrived: the deposits on your bank statement from paychecks, benefits or other regular sources, after taxes and deductions, because that is the money the household had to work with. The second is the total of the fixed bills on your list, meaning every monthly bill, including utilities whose amount moves, using the amounts actually paid rather than the ones you expected. The third is what went into the set-aside for the irregular bills. Subtract the second and third from the first, and what remains is what the household had for everything else: groceries, gas, school costs, haircuts, a birthday present, the co-pay nobody planned for.

That one figure does much of the work a spending diary would, without the diary. The review doesn't ask what a sandwich cost on Tuesday. It shows instead how much room there was for the ordinary spending of a month, and whether the month as lived fit inside it. The statements answer the second part roughly: if the checking balance slid down over the month, or a card balance grew, and no irregular bill explains it, everyday spending ran past the room that was there.

Here is an example. Glen is retired, and last month his pension and a benefit payment together brought $3,100 into his checking account. His monthly bills, from rent and utilities to his phone, a supplemental health plan and two subscriptions, came to $2,240. He moved $180 into the account where he keeps money for his car registration and the yearly auto insurance premium. That leaves $680 for everything else: groceries, gas, the prescriptions his plan doesn't fully cover, a bus ticket to see his grandchildren. Seeing the figure written down, Glen understands why the last ten days of each month have felt tight; the number doesn't tell him what to do, only what his month was.

Two details keep the figure honest. If a credit card carries some of your fixed bills, such as a phone plan or a subscription charged to it, count those bills once, under their own names, and not a second time inside the card payment. And if your income changes from month to month, use what really arrived rather than what usually arrives, because the point is to see the month that happened.

The figure isn't a grade. A thin month, or one where the number comes out below zero, is information about the month, not about the person who paid the bills. What you do with it is your household's decision. Some people simply want to know it; others keep each month's figure next to the last one to see which way it is moving.

## Carrying changes back into the list and calendar

The review isn't finished until what you found goes back into the system. Rosa writes her internet bill's new amount on the list. The duplicate phone payment will come back either as a credit on the next bill or as a refund, depending on the company; she notes which one she was told to expect. Once the streaming service is canceled, it comes off the list, and the date of the cancellation and any confirmation number go wherever she keeps her records. If a company moved a due date, a card arrived with a new number, or an autopay now draws from a different account, the list and the calendar both change, so the next weekly check works from the right information.

Some changes reach further. A price increase on a yearly bill, such as an insurance premium at renewal, changes the amount the set-aside has to cover. A charge still in dispute stays visible: give it a date on the calendar to look again, so the question gets followed up instead of fading. When the list and the statements agree, and every open question has a date next to it, the month is closed.

## This week's step

Choose an evening this week and sit down with last month's statements for every bank account and credit card, the bills that came in, and your list. Go through the list bill by bill and find each payment, then read each statement from top to bottom for anything recurring that the list doesn't have. Make a note of anything that looks wrong, higher than before or doubled, and of which items deserve a call. Then take the income that arrived, subtract the fixed bills and what went into the set-aside, and write down the one figure that remains; if you haven't started a set-aside yet, that part is zero for now. Keep the figure where your list lives. You don't have to do anything with it yet, and next month's review will give it something to stand beside.
