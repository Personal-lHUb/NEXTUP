# Introduction

Take Dana, a mother of two, on a Thursday evening in October. She is an example rather than a real person, but her evening may sound familiar. With the kids in bed, she sits down at the kitchen table with the week's mail. On top is the credit card statement, and inside it, a late fee. She is sure she paid that card. Then it comes back to her: she paid the *other* card, the one for groceries and gas, that same afternoon, and somewhere in her head the two payments became one.

She goes looking for the water bill to see whether it went out, and finds it under a school flyer, still sealed. A sticky note on the fridge reads "car insurance, Nov?" in her own handwriting, with no year. On her phone, the bank's app shows three alerts she hasn't opened. None of it is hard to understand. There is simply a lot of it, spread across too many places for one tired person to hold in her head.

Perhaps you have had an evening like Dana's. Perhaps yours was harder: a notice warning that the power could be shut off, a call you let go to voicemail because you knew what it was about, a month when the paycheck ran out before the bills did. This book was written for all of those evenings, and for the ordinary ones in between.

## Who this book is for

This book is for the adult who handles the bills in an American home. You might be a parent keeping track of the electric and gas, the rent or mortgage, a couple of credit cards, insurance and a few subscriptions. You might be retired, with some bills paid automatically from your account and others by check, and you'd like fewer surprises. You might have just been hit with a late fee, or an overdraft fee after your balance dipped below zero, and want a routine that holds. Or you might already be behind and need to know the next step, from someone who won't lecture you about how you got there.

You don't need to enjoy money matters or know the vocabulary. When a term such as "grace period" comes up, it gets a plain explanation the first time it appears. If someone handed you this book—a grown daughter, a friend, a counselor who helps people with their budgets—you are exactly the reader they had in mind. And if you're the one passing it along, nothing in it assumes the reader already has a system.

## What you will build

Over these chapters you'll build a way of handling bills that fits your own home. It starts with one list of every bill that comes back again and again, including the ones that come around only once a year. That list gets one home. It can be a paper notebook, a spreadsheet or the tools built into your bank's website or app; the book explains the strengths and weak spots of each type without steering you toward any product.

From there, you'll set each due date beside the days your money actually arrives, so you can see which paycheck covers which bill. You'll decide, one bill at a time, what runs on autopay and what you would rather check by hand. Then you'll settle into a short routine: a few minutes each week, a longer look once a month, and a review once a year that carries everything forward. The goal is order you can see at a glance, and nothing grander than that.

Another part of the book is for when things go wrong. A payment can slip by mistake, or the money may not stretch far enough. For those times you'll find a calm, step-by-step way to respond: what each kind of bill puts at stake, whom to call, what to ask for, and where to look for help beyond the company that sent the bill.

There are things this book won't do. It prints no forms or blank pages; it explains in words how to set up your own list wherever you choose to keep it. It won't tell you what to do with your money. It explains how bills, fees and options work, and when your situation needs someone who can look at the details, it points you to a nonprofit credit counselor, an attorney or the provider itself. Late fees, grace periods, shutoff protections and credit reporting rules vary by state and by company. Where they do, the book says so and tells you where to check instead of giving a number that may not apply to you.

Nor does it promise a perfect record or a dollar figure saved. Order can't make a tight month loose. What a calendar tied to your paydays can do is show you a tight week before you are in it, which leaves time for a phone call rather than a surprise.

## How to use this book

Read one chapter at a time. The chapters are short enough for one sitting, and each ends with one step for the week, sometimes spread over several evenings. The step is written out in plain sentences, with nothing to fill in on the page.

The chapters build on one another, so the order matters. You find your bills and learn to read them, then record them and give the list a home. Next comes timing: the calendar first, autopay after it, because an automatic payment is only as safe as the money in the account on the day it goes out. After that come the bills that don't arrive every month, then the weekly and monthly habits, then the chapters on trouble, ending with *Catching Up When Several Bills Are Behind*. The last stretch covers fake bills, sharing the work at home, an aging parent's bills and a yearly review.

If you are already behind, go straight to the part called *Falling Behind and Catching Up*; its last chapter shows the way back, step by step. You don't need a tidy system to call a utility company before a due date or to ask about a payment plan. Deal with what's pressing, then come back to the beginning; the system is easier to build once the urgent calls are made.

## This week's step

The step is small: choose your evening. Pick one night a week when you can have half an hour undisturbed at the table where the mail tends to land. That same evening will later hold your weekly check. Then keep it the way you would keep any other appointment.
