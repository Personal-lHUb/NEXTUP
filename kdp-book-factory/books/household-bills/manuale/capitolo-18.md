# The First Days After a Missed Payment

A missed payment tends to announce itself in an unpleasant way. An email says your account is past due, the bank's app shows a negative balance, or a letter arrives with the word "returned" in bold. The first reaction is often a jolt of worry, followed by the urge to fix everything at once. Neither helps much. What helps is a short sequence, done in order over the next few days. The most common slips are also the easiest to set right, as long as they are caught early and handled one step at a time.

These pages explain how things usually work and are not legal, financial or credit advice; rules vary by state and change over time. If you face a lawsuit, an eviction, a garnishment or a large debt, turn to a lawyer or free legal aid, or a nonprofit credit counselor.

## Work out what actually happened

Before you pay anything or call anyone, find out which kind of slip you are dealing with. They look alike from a distance, but each one leads to a different next move. The first kind is a plain missed due date. No payment went out, the date passed, and the bill is simply unpaid. The second is a **returned payment**: you did pay, or autopay tried to, but your bank refused it. The third is an overdraft: the payment went through anyway and pushed the account below zero. The chapter on the account behind autopay explains both.

The fourth is a bill that never reached you, perhaps because a paper statement went to an old address or an email landed in a folder you don't check. To the provider, it is late all the same, even though you never saw it.

Two screens usually settle the question: the bank's transaction history and the provider's own account page or statement. The bank tells you what left your account and whether it came back. The provider tells you what it received and what it now says you owe.

Take, as an example, Andre, whose electric bill is paid by autopay from his checking account. A text from the utility says his payment "could not be processed." His bank's history shows the payment on the due date, marked as returned, with a fee beside it. That is a returned payment, not a forgotten one. The electric bill is still unpaid, and the bank has charged him for the attempt.

The four cases also point you to where the work is. A missed date or a bill that never arrived is settled with the provider. After an overdraft, the provider has been paid, and what needs attention is the bank account. A returned payment, as Andre's shows, involves both.

## Pay what's owed and confirm it posted

Once you know what happened, the next question is the exact amount owed right now. It may not match the bill you remember. A late fee may have been added, a returned-payment fee may sit on top of it, and a new billing period may have started. The provider's account page or a call to customer service gives you the current figure. If the bill never arrived, ask for a copy and check the mailing address and email the provider has on file, so the next one doesn't go astray too.

If the money is there, paying that amount soon is usually what keeps a slip from growing. If it isn't there yet, a call still helps: tell the provider when you expect to pay and ask what happens in between. How you pay also affects timing. A payment made online or by phone directly with the provider often reaches the account faster than one sent through your bank's bill-pay service, which may mail a paper check on your behalf. A check you mail yourself is slower still. When time is short, ask the provider which method credits the account fastest.

Paying is only half of it. The other half is confirming that the payment **posted**, meaning the provider has recorded it on your account. Keep the confirmation number, or a screenshot of the confirmation screen, and check the provider's account a few business days later. A payment that still shows as "pending" or "processing" has not posted yet. How long posting takes depends on the method and the provider; customer service can tell you what to expect.

Sometimes the two sides disagree. Kim paid her water bill online a few days after missing the due date. Her bank shows the money gone, but a week later the utility's website still shows the full balance due. Her first impulse is to pay again, which could mean paying twice. She calls the utility with the date, the amount and the confirmation number, and asks them to find the payment.

Payments sometimes land on the wrong account number, or sit in a queue until someone matches them to the right customer. If the provider still can't find it, your bank can usually give you a record showing where the money went and when. With both records in hand, the payment can usually be traced, and you avoid sending the same amount twice while the two sides sort it out.

## Break the chain

One slip can set off others, and the first days are when you can still stop them. Three links in the chain are worth checking.

The first is the **automatic retry**. Some providers resubmit a returned payment on their own a few days later, and some banks charge a fee each time a payment is presented to an account that can't cover it. Whether this happens, and how often, depends on the provider and the bank. If you have already paid another way, ask the provider whether a retry is scheduled and ask them to cancel it, so the old payment doesn't come through on top of the new one. If you haven't paid yet, ask when the retry will happen, so you know the day the money needs to be there.

The second link is further overdraft charges. When an account stays below zero, some banks add a fee for each new item that overdraws it, and some charge again if the balance stays negative for several days. The rules are in your account agreement, and the bank's customer service can tell you which ones apply to you. Meanwhile, look at everything scheduled to leave that account in the coming days: other autopays, checks you've written that haven't cleared yet, a subscription about to renew. While the balance is short, each one is a chance for another charge.

The third link is the next bill due before the account has recovered. This is common after a slip, because the money used to catch up was often meant for something else. Open your bill calendar and look at the two weeks ahead. If a payment is set to leave that account the day before your next paycheck arrives, that is where the next slip could come from. You can call that provider now and ask about moving the date, or adjust the autopay as described earlier. Either way, you are deciding before the date instead of finding out after it.

## Check what changed on the account

A missed payment can leave marks beyond the unpaid amount, and some of them don't show until you look. On the provider's side, check whether a late fee was added and how much it was. Check whether the account's status has changed: it may now read "past due" or "delinquent," words that mean the provider considers the payment overdue. On a credit card, federal rules generally don't let the issuer take away a promotional rate or raise the rate on the balance you already carry unless a payment is more than 60 days late, and a higher rate on new purchases requires written notice first, which spells out the change. A utility may have started the notice process that comes before a shutoff. Those rules vary by state and by company, and the notice itself or your state's utility commission can tell you the timeline.

After a returned payment, find out whether the provider still accepts electronic payments from you and whether autopay is still on. Ask directly, because none of this is guaranteed to appear on the next bill. On the bank's side, look for returned-item fees, overdraft fees and any notice about the account itself. Whatever you find, write it down.

That last part matters more than it seems. Every call you make in these days is worth recording: the date and time, the name the representative gives you, what they said, the amounts they quoted, and any reference number for the call. If someone promises to cancel a retry or look into a fee, write down exactly what was promised and when it should happen. Days later, when a statement shows something different, you won't be relying on memory to say what you were told. Keep these notes wherever your bill system lives, next to that bill's entry in your master list, so the whole story sits in one place.

Back to Andre, from the example above. When he calls the utility, he notes the day and time, the first name of the person he spoke with, the balance including the late fee, and the confirmation number for the payment he makes by phone. He also asks whether autopay is still active, and writes down the answer: it has been off since the return. When his next bill arrives, he can check it against that note in a couple of minutes, and he already knows that month's payment won't go out on its own.

## This week's step

If a payment has slipped, call the provider this week. Before you dial, open the bank's history and the provider's account page so you know which of the four kinds of slip you are dealing with. On the call, confirm the amount owed today, ask what has been charged since the due date and whether the account's status has changed, and, if a payment was returned, ask whether a retry is scheduled. Write down the date, the name of the person you spoke with and what they told you, and keep that note beside the bill in your list. If nothing has slipped, decide now where those notes would go, so the place exists on the day you need it.
