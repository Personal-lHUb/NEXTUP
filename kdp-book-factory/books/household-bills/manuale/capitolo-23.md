# Comparing What Payday and Other Quick Loans Cost

These offers tend to show up at a particular moment. A storefront sign on the way home, a message on your phone the day rent is due, a checkout button that splits a price into four. They appear when the calls have been made, the assistance application is pending and the gap is still there. Whether one of them fits your situation is your decision. What helps is being able to read the price, so the decision rests on the real numbers rather than the size of the first payment.

## How each kind works

### Payday loans

A payday loan is a small loan meant to be repaid out of your next paycheck. Lenders generally ask for identification, proof of income and a checking account. You usually give the lender a check dated for your payday, or permission to withdraw the money electronically from your bank account. The loan, plus a fee, is due in one piece on that date, typically two to four weeks away. If the account is short when the lender collects, the withdrawal can bounce or overdraw the account, and your bank's fees land on top of the lender's. An unpaid loan can be sent to a debt collector, and a collection account can reach your credit reports.

### Auto title loans

With a title loan, you borrow against your car. The lender holds the car's title, the paper that proves ownership, while you keep driving. The amount is usually a fraction of what the car is worth, and repayment is often due in about a month. If you don't repay, the lender can repossess the car and sell it. When the car is how someone in the house gets to work, what is pledged is the commute as well as the vehicle.

### Pawn loans

At a pawnshop you hand over an item, such as a ring or a tool set, and receive a loan worth part of what the shop expects to get by reselling it. You get a ticket with the amount due and the deadline. Repay the loan and the fee by then and the item comes back to you. If you don't, the shop generally keeps and sells it, and that usually settles the matter: no collector, no mark on your credit reports. In most cases, what you stand to lose is the thing you left on the counter.

### Rent-to-own

Rent-to-own stores lease furniture, appliances and electronics by the week or month, and the item becomes yours after a set number of payments. It is usually treated as a rental rather than a loan. Here is an example with round numbers chosen for the arithmetic: Kevin needs a washer with a cash price of $600, and the agreement asks $20 a week for 60 weeks. He would pay $1,200 in all, twice the cash price. He can generally return the washer and stop paying, but the payments already made stay with the store, and a missed payment can mean the store comes to take it back.

### Paycheck advance apps

These apps let you get part of your next paycheck early. Those offered through an employer advance pay you have already earned; those that link to your bank account advance an amount based on your expected deposit. Either way, they take the money back automatically when your paycheck lands. Many say they charge no interest, but there may be a fee for instant delivery, a monthly membership, or a request for an optional tip. Each of those is part of what the advance costs you. If the repayment is pulled from an account that turns out to be short, the same overdraft problem as with a payday loan can follow.

### Buy now, pay later

A buy-now-pay-later plan splits a purchase into installments. A common version takes four equal payments, the first at checkout and the rest every two weeks, from a card or bank account you link. Short plans often carry no interest if every payment goes through; longer ones may charge it. A missed payment can bring a late fee, and a payment drawn from a thin account can trigger an overdraft. Because each plan is small, several can be running at once, and none of them appears on your master list unless you put them there. Whether these plans show up on your credit reports depends on the lender and has been changing, so ask before you agree.

## A flat fee versus a yearly rate

Many quick loans quote their price as a flat fee, such as so many dollars for every $100 borrowed. A fee is easy to picture, and for a two-week loan it can look modest. Credit cards and car loans quote an annual percentage rate, or APR: the cost of borrowing expressed as a yearly percentage. Putting everything on the same yearly scale is what lets you compare a two-week loan with a five-year one.

Doris borrows $200 the week her rent comes due. The fee is $20, and the full $220 is due in 14 days. The numbers are picked to make the arithmetic easy, not to describe what lenders charge where you live. Twenty dollars on $200 is 10 percent, and it buys two weeks. A year holds about 26 two-week stretches, so 10 percent every two weeks works out to roughly 260 percent a year. Nothing about the fee changed, only the scale, and the scale is what lets you set the fee beside the rate on a credit card or a personal loan.

APR tells you how the price compares, not what leaves your account. For that you need two plain figures: the total you will repay, in dollars, and the date it is due. For Doris, those are $220 and a date two weeks away. Federal law generally requires lenders to show the finance charge, meaning the cost of the loan in dollars, and the APR in writing before you sign. Rent-to-own agreements, some advance apps and some short installment plans may not be covered the same way and so may not show either one, which is when your own arithmetic matters most: add up every payment, fee, membership and tip, and set the total against what you receive.

## When one short loan becomes several

A single-payment loan asks for everything at once: the amount borrowed and the fee, out of one paycheck that also has to cover the usual bills. When that is not possible, many lenders offer a rollover, where you pay only the fee and the loan is extended for another period. Back to Doris. If she can't pay $220 on day 14, she might pay the $20 fee and roll the loan over, and two weeks later she owes $220 again. Roll it over three times and the loan comes due a fourth time at the eight-week mark, with $80 in fees charged and the $200 still owed in full. A title loan can be renewed the same way, a month at a time, with the car still pledged.

The same pattern can take other shapes where rollovers aren't allowed. A loan is repaid on payday and a new one is taken out a day or two later, because repaying the first left the paycheck short. With advance apps, the amount taken back from one paycheck can leave a gap that the next advance fills. Several buy-now-pay-later plans can fall due in the same week. None of this requires a careless borrower; it follows from the arithmetic of repaying everything from a single paycheck that was already spoken for. When you are weighing an offer, the useful question is whether you can repay it and still cover the bills that fall in the two weeks after.

## Rules that change at the state line

Much of what applies to these products depends on where you live. Some states don't allow payday loans at all, or cap rates so low that storefront lenders don't operate there. Others allow them with limits on the loan amount, the fee, the number of rollovers or the number of loans at once, and some require the lender to offer a longer repayment plan when you can't pay. Title loans are legal in some states and not in others. Pawn fees and holding periods are largely a matter of state law, and the rules for paycheck advance apps are still being settled, with states treating them differently.

To check what applies to you, start with your state's financial regulator, the agency that licenses lenders. Its name varies: department of financial institutions, banking department, division of consumer finance or something similar. Many of these agencies offer a license lookup on their website, where you can type in a lender's name and see whether it is licensed in your state; if yours doesn't, a phone call gets the same answer. The check matters most for online lenders, which may be based in another state. In some states a loan from an unlicensed lender may not be enforceable, and the regulator or your state attorney general's office can tell you whether yours is one.

At the federal level, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has published plain explanations of these products and has taken complaints about lenders; check its website for what it currently handles, and if it can't help, your state regulator can. A complaint is usually forwarded to the company for a response, which can help when a withdrawal or a fee doesn't match what you signed. If you or your spouse is on active duty, a federal law adds protections on many of these loans; the legal assistance office on base can explain them.

## This week's step

If you're weighing one of these loans, write down two things before you sign: the total you would repay, in dollars, and the date it's due. Not the fee alone, and not the first payment, but the whole amount that will leave your account and the day it will leave. Then set those two figures beside the options from the previous two chapters: the payment plan or extra time a provider offered on the phone, the assistance program, the appointment with a nonprofit counselor. Some of those may cost nothing and some may take longer than you have. Seeing them side by side, with real dollars and real dates, means that whatever you choose, you choose it with the price in view.
