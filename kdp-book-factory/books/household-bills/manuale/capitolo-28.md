# A Yearly Review to Get Ready for Next Year's Bills

Your household's bills don't change all at once. They drift. The internet price creeps up by a few dollars in March and again in September. A credit card is reissued with a new number. A subscription ends, a new one begins, and a newly bought car may bring a registration due in a different month. Each change is small enough for the weekly check and monthly review to absorb, which is exactly how a year of them can leave your master list describing a household that no longer quite exists.

The yearly review is where you catch the drift. It uses the same list, calendar and records you already keep, so there is nothing new to build. What changes is the distance you look from. A single month shows you what is due; twelve months side by side show you what has moved.

## Choosing the date and booking it a year out

Any date can work, but some are easier than others. A good choice is a point when a full year of statements is already in hand. For many households that means a few weeks into the new year, once December's statements have posted. Others prefer a date tied to something that already happens every year, such as the month the homeowners or renters insurance renews, so one of the larger annual bills and the review arrive together. What matters most is that the date is fixed and that it doesn't collide with a season when you are already stretched.

Plan on an afternoon, or two shorter sittings if that suits you better. Put the review on whatever calendar the household actually looks at, with a reminder a week earlier to gather the year's statements and the login details you'll need. Then do the part that keeps the habit alive: before you finish this year's review, put next year's on the calendar. A review that has to be remembered from scratch every twelve months is a review that quietly stops happening.

Howard, with his paper notebook, chooses the second Saturday of February, after his December statements arrive and before his property tax installment comes due in spring. The date goes on the kitchen wall calendar and in the reminders on his phone, and each February the last thing he does is write in the following year's date.

## Carrying the year forward

Start with the master list and go through it line by line, with the year's statements or bank history open beside it. For each bill, you're checking three things: the amount, the due date and, for the yearly ones, the renewal date. It goes faster than it sounds, because most lines need only a glance.

Amounts are where most of the drift hides. A fixed bill may have gone up once or twice. For a variable bill, such as electricity or water, the list already holds a range; update it with the twelve months just past. It may have widened or narrowed, and the highest month may have moved. Due dates move less often, but they do move, sometimes because a provider changed its billing cycle and sometimes because you asked for a new date and never updated the list. For annual bills, the renewal date rolls forward to its next occurrence, and the new amount goes in as soon as you know it.

As you go, note what changed and by how much. Terrence keeps his list in a spreadsheet. When he compares January's statements with December's, he finds his internet bill rose from $55 to $72 in two steps, his subscriptions went from two to four, and his car insurance renewed $124 higher. None of these stood out in any single month. Taken together, they add up to a noticeable difference in what his regular bills cost. The review doesn't decide anything for him; it turns a vague sense that things cost more into a short list of specific changes, each with a provider he could call if he wants to ask about it.

The bill calendar gets the same treatment. Paydays change too: a new job, a different pay schedule, a benefit that now arrives on another date. If you're paid every two weeks, the months with a third paycheck change from one year to the next, so it's worth marking next year's on the calendar now. Where a due date and a payday have drifted apart over the year, this is a good moment to line them up again.

## What ended, what began, and where the money comes from

Some bills on the list will have ended during the year. A loan may be paid off, a subscription canceled, a child's sports fee finished with the season. Before taking any of them off, look at the last few months of bank and card history to confirm the charges really stopped. A canceled service that keeps billing is easier to catch now than a year from now. Rather than deleting the line, move it to a closed section of the list, or cross it out in a notebook, and write the date it ended. If the charge ever reappears, you'll know at once it shouldn't be there.

Then look for what began. Bills added in a hurry during the year often went onto the list with only a name and an amount. Fill in what's missing: how it's paid, from which account, and whom to contact. Look ahead as well. If you know that a lease renews, a promotional rate ends or a new bill starts next year, put it on the list and the calendar now, with whatever amount you have.

The last check in this part is the one most easily skipped: confirming that every automatic payment still draws from the right account. Over twelve months, cards expire, get lost or are reissued after a fraud alert, and bank accounts get opened and closed. Some providers receive new card details automatically and some don't, so the only reliable way to know is to look. For each autopay, compare the account or card the provider has on file, usually shown by its last four digits, with the one on your list, and check that the amount setting is still the one you chose.

In the spring Terrence moved his checking account to a different bank and switched his monthly autopays over at the time. What he missed was the renewal of his membership in a professional association. It charges once a year, so nothing came through in the weeks he was moving everything, and it was still set to draw from the old account, which he had already closed. The chapter on the account behind autopay suggests keeping the old account open until every bill, the yearly ones included, has drawn once from the new one. The yearly bills are the ones most likely to be left behind when accounts change, because they're quiet for eleven months.

## Archiving, the handover note and the set-aside

With the list current, clear out the year behind you. Gather the year's paper statements and bills into one folder or envelope labeled with the year, and move the year's downloaded statements into a digital folder named the same way. The place where current notices land stays light, and last year's records are still easy to find if you need to prove a payment or check an old charge.

How long to keep each kind of record has no single answer. It depends on the document and on your situation: a letter confirming that a debt was paid in full, for example, can matter much longer than an ordinary utility bill. For anything connected to your taxes, check with a tax professional or look at the guidance the IRS publishes on keeping records. Whatever you decide to throw away, shred it, since statements carry account numbers, addresses and other personal details.

Next, open the handover note, or write it now if you haven't, and read it as if you were the person who had to use it tomorrow. The year's changes all belong in it: the new checking account, the reissued card, the subscription that ended, the new agent at the insurance company, the new website for paying the water bill. A note that was accurate in January and wrong by December can send a helper to a closed account. Put the date on it so anyone reading it knows how current it is.

Finally, check the set-aside for irregular bills against the year ahead. It was sized to last year's bills, so any increase you've just found in an annual or quarterly bill shows up here first. Add up next year's irregular bills using the new amounts you've just recorded, then compare that total with what your monthly set-aside will come to over twelve months.

Howard sets aside $150 a month, or $1,800 over a year, which was exactly what last year's irregular bills came to. This February his list shows a homeowners insurance premium $110 higher than before and a property tax bill up $70, with the car registration unchanged, for a new total of $1,980. The $180 gap is visible in February, not in the week the insurance bill arrives. What he does with that information is his decision; the review simply gives him months to make it instead of days.

## This week's step

Pick the date for your yearly review and put it on the calendar you actually use, with a reminder a week earlier to gather the year's statements. Your system doesn't need to be a year old for the first one to be worth doing, because your bank and card history usually already holds the past twelve months. Setting the date takes a moment; the review itself can wait for its day.

Then take a few minutes to write down what this year has already taught you about your household's bills. A few plain sentences are enough: which bill surprised you, which week of the month always ran short, which habit slipped when life got busy, and what worked better than you expected. Keep the note with the master list, wherever that lives. When the review date arrives, it will be the first thing you read, and it will tell you where to look hardest.
