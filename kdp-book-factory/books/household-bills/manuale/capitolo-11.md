# Biweekly Paychecks and Income That Won't Sit Still

The calendar from the last chapter rests on a quiet assumption: that money arrives on the same dates every month. In many homes it doesn't. The paycheck comes every other Friday, so its date slides a little each month. A commission depends on what closed. Tips depend on the season, the weather and the crowd. When the income moves, a calendar that ties each bill to a fixed payday starts giving wrong answers, quietly, in the month nobody was watching.

The answer is rarely a more complicated calendar. It is picking an anchor for the bills that holds still even when the money doesn't.

## Every other week is not twice a month

Two pay schedules sound alike and behave differently. **Biweekly** pay arrives every other week on the same weekday, which makes 26 paychecks in a year. **Twice-monthly** pay, also called semimonthly, arrives on two fixed dates, such as the 15th and the last day of the month, which makes 24. Your pay stub or your employer's payroll office can tell you which one you have.

With twice-monthly pay, the calendar from the last chapter works as built, because the 15th is always the 15th. With biweekly pay, the dates drift. The paydays creep a little earlier each month, until a month comes along that holds three of them. A bill due on the 5th might come two days after a payday in March and a full week after one in May.

Here is an example. Aaron is paid every other Friday. When he first built his calendar, he matched his car insurance, due on the 8th, to the paycheck that came on the 5th. That worked the first month. Then his payday slid to the 2nd, and by the 8th most of that check had gone to groceries and gas, because in his head the insurance still belonged to "the paycheck around the 5th," and that month there wasn't one.

With biweekly pay, it often helps to think in pay periods instead of months. On each payday, look ahead to the day before the next payday. Every bill due inside that window is paid, or its amount held back, from the check that just arrived. The window is always fourteen days, so the rule never changes, even though the bills inside it do. Aaron's car insurance now simply falls in whichever window contains the 8th. Some windows carry the rent and the insurance together; those are the tight weeks from the last chapter, and with biweekly pay they wander.

Biweekly paydays are irregular by date but completely predictable. Starting from any one payday, you can count forward fourteen days at a time and mark every payday of the year in a single sitting. If a payday lands on a holiday, your payroll office can tell you whether the money comes early or late.

### The month with three paychecks

Twenty-six paychecks don't divide evenly into twelve months, so most years have two months in which three biweekly checks arrive. Which months they are depends on the date of the year's first payday, and once every payday is marked, you'll see them at a glance.

Households that plan by the month often count on two checks a month, so the third arrives with no monthly bills waiting for it. With two-week windows there is no extra check as such: the same room is spread thinly across the year. What the third check goes toward is your decision. Some households write a note beside those two paydays in January saying what it's for: a bill that fell behind, one of the yearly bills, more room in the account, or nothing in particular. A choice made in January doesn't have to compete with whatever is in front of you the morning the money appears.

## When the amount moves as well as the date

Gig work, commissions, tips and seasonal jobs add a second problem. The date changes, and so does the amount. A delivery platform might pay weekly, a client whenever an invoice gets processed, a sales job only after a deal closes, and a summer job not at all after Labor Day.

The usual instinct is to plan around an average month. An average, though, hides exactly the months that cause trouble, and the bills arrive in every one of them. So some households plan their fixed bills around a lean month instead: the slowest month of the past year, or close to it. If the fixed bills fit inside the lean month, the other months have room. If they don't fit, you've learned that from your own records at the kitchen table, not from a late notice.

Take, as an example, Nina, a hairstylist who rents a chair in a salon. Her clients pay her directly, her card tips arrive in batches, and her cash tips get deposited whenever she stops at the bank. Over the past year her deposits ran from about $2,100 in January to about $4,600 in December, with August nearly as slow as January. Measured against her average month, around $3,200, her fixed bills of about $1,900 looked easy. Measured against January, they fit but left about $200 for everything else, groceries and gas included. That didn't change her income, but it changed how she saw it: the money above the lean-month figure in a busy stretch is what carries the slow ones.

Seasonal work stretches the same idea across the whole year. A landscaper who earns most of the year's income between April and October has a lean month that may be close to zero. For that household, the calendar has to cover a full twelve months, so the winter bills are in view in July, while the money is still coming in.

## A holding account between the money and the bills

When income is uneven enough, some households stop tying bills to deposits at all. They use a holding account: one account where every dollar of income lands as it arrives, whatever the source and whatever the size. The bills are paid out of that account on their own due dates. Money for groceries, gas and everyday life is moved out on a fixed schedule, as if the household were paying itself a steady wage.

Once the account holds enough to cover the bills until the next income can reasonably be expected, the timing of any single deposit stops mattering. A client who pays two weeks late no longer makes the electric bill late. The calendar still lists due dates, but the question it answers changes from "which paycheck covers this?" to "is the balance there before this date?"

In the example, Nina set one up after a January when her rent came due in the slowest week of the slowest month. All her client payments and tip deposits now go into one checking account used for nothing but the bills, and on the 1st and 15th she moves a fixed amount to her everyday account.

The idea has limits. It needs a starting balance to work, and building that can take months. A second account may come with monthly fees or minimum balance rules that differ from bank to bank, so the account terms are the place to check. Transfers between two different banks can take a business day or more, which matters when a due date is close. And it doesn't create money: in a long slow stretch, the balance falls. What it changes is visibility. A falling balance shows up weeks before a due date, while there's still time to call someone.

## One deposit, many due dates

Retirement and benefit income often arrives once a month: a pension, Social Security, disability or veterans' benefits. The date is set by the payer, and some programs pay on a day decided by their own rules rather than on the 1st. The payer's own schedule, usually on its website or in the letters it sends, shows the dates for the year and what happens when one falls on a weekend or holiday.

A single deposit gives the month a shape. The account is at its fullest the day the money arrives and at its thinnest the day before the next deposit. A bill due just before deposit day is paid with money that has sat in the account for four weeks, exposed to everything else that came up. That bill is the one most likely to catch a household with perfectly steady income by surprise.

The calendar approach here is to line up as many due dates as possible in the days just after the deposit, leaving a few days' margin in case it shifts. The request to move a due date, covered in the last chapter, is the tool. Take, as an example, a retired household with a pension on the 1st and a benefit on the third Wednesday. They have two deposits, so they can use two anchors: the housing payment and the utilities behind the pension, the insurance and phone behind the benefit. For a bill that can't be moved and falls just before a deposit, some households hold its amount back from the day the previous deposit arrives.

## This week's step

Pull up your bank statements for the past three to six months, or the past twelve if your work follows the seasons. Look only at the money coming in: the date of each deposit, how much it was, and how many days passed between one and the next. Note the smallest month too. The picture may be steadier than it felt, or more uneven, and either answer is useful.

Then choose the approach that fits your household. If your paydays fall on fixed dates, the calendar you built last chapter stands as it is. If you're paid biweekly, mark every payday of the year and switch to two-week windows. If the amounts swing, compare your fixed bills with the lean month and think about whether a holding account would suit you. If one deposit carries the month, line your due dates up behind it. Write the approach you chose at the top of your calendar, so that next month you still know which rule it follows.
