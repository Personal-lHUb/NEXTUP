# What a Credit Card Statement Is Telling You

A credit card statement looks like any other bill, with an amount and a date, but it describes an account that never stops moving. You can keep using the card after the statement is printed, payments can land at any time, and interest can build on some parts of the balance and not on others. That is why a card statement carries more than one date and more than one balance. The ones you act on decide both whether your payment counts as on time and whether you pay interest.

Most statements gather the key numbers in a summary box near the top of the first page. Labels vary from one card company to another, so the names used here are the common ones. If yours differ, the explanations on the back of the statement will tell you which line is which.

## Two dates that do different jobs

The first date to find is the **closing date**, sometimes called the statement date or the end of the billing cycle. It is the day the card company stops counting and takes a snapshot of the account. Every purchase, payment, fee and interest charge up to that day appears on the statement, and anything later waits for the next one. The billing cycle is the stretch between one closing date and the next, usually about a month.

The second date is the **payment due date**: the day by which the card company must receive at least the minimum payment for it to count as on time. On most cards it falls on the same day every month, a few weeks after the closing date. The gap exists so the statement can reach you and you have time to pay. Within limits set by federal rules, the card's terms say what time of day the payment has to arrive and what happens when the due date lands on a weekend or holiday. Check these details on the statement or in the card agreement.

Loretta's card closes on the 3rd of each month, with payment due on the 28th. Suppose she buys school shoes for her son on the 2nd. They land on the statement that closes the next day, and she has until the 28th, a bit under four weeks, to pay for them. Had she bought the same shoes on the 4th, one day after the closing date, they would wait for the following month's statement. Their payment wouldn't be due until the 28th of the month after, close to eight weeks later. Only their place in the cycle changed, and that place decides which due date they belong to.

The same gap explains a common confusion. By the time a statement is in your hands, its balance is already out of date. When Loretta opens hers on the 8th, the balance shows what she owed on the 3rd, and she may have used the card several times since. The statement tells you what was owed on the closing date, not what is owed right now.

## The grace period, in general terms

Many cards offer a **grace period** on purchases. This is a stretch of time, ending on the due date, during which purchases don't collect interest as long as a condition is met. Unlike the grace period in the chapter on reading a household bill, a window after the due date before a late fee is charged, this one is about interest. On most cards that have one, the condition is paying the full statement balance by the due date. Pay it all, and the purchases on that statement generally cost nothing beyond their price. Leave part of it unpaid, and things work differently.

Carrying a balance means leaving part of the statement balance unpaid after the due date, and it is what usually ends the grace period. On many cards, interest then applies to the unpaid amount and to new purchases from the day they are made, with no free stretch in between. Getting the grace period back often takes paying in full for one or more billing cycles. Cash advances and balance transfers commonly have no grace period at all, so interest on them can start right away even on a card where purchases are covered.

A related surprise is worth knowing about. Someone who has been carrying a balance pays it off in full, then finds a modest interest charge on the next statement. That charge usually covers interest that built up between the closing date and the day the payment arrived. It is sometimes called residual or trailing interest. Each card sets these rules in its own agreement. To see how yours handles them, look at the box of rates and fees near the start of the agreement and the interest explanation on the statement, or call the card company.

## Three amounts that look alike

A statement usually shows at least three amounts close together, and each answers a different question. The **statement balance**, sometimes called the new balance, is what you owed on the closing date. It starts from whatever was left over from the previous statement, adds the cycle's purchases, fees and interest, and subtracts payments and credits that landed before it closed. On a card with a grace period, this is the amount that generally has to be paid by the due date to avoid interest on purchases.

The **current balance** is what you owe today. The printed statement can't show it, because the page was printed before any of it happened. You find it in the online account or by calling the number on the card. It includes everything since the closing date: new purchases, payments that have posted, returns. On a card in regular use, it rarely matches the statement balance.

The **minimum payment** is the smallest amount the card company will accept by the due date for the account to count as on time. Paying at least this much generally keeps a late fee from being charged that month. It does not stop interest on whatever part of the statement balance stays unpaid.

Sam's statement, with numbers picked to keep the arithmetic easy, shows a statement balance of $840 and a minimum payment of $35. A week later the online account shows a current balance of $910, because he has bought gas and groceries since the closing date. If he pays $35 by the due date, the payment is on time, but the other $805 carries into the next cycle and may collect interest. If he pays $840, he has covered the statement balance. If he pays $910, he has also covered the newer charges, which will already be paid when they appear on the next statement. Each choice does something different, and which one fits depends on the household's own situation, not on a rule from this book.

### The notice about paying only the minimum

Most US card statements include a box near the payment information, with a heading along the lines of a minimum payment warning. It starts from the statement balance. It estimates how long paying off that balance would take if you paid only the minimum each month, and roughly what you would pay in total. Many also show a monthly amount that would clear the same balance in about three years, with its own total.

The box is an estimate. It rests on assumptions printed beside it, usually that no new charges are made and the rate stays the same. So it reads best as a picture of how the minimum and the interest work together, not as a forecast. On many cards, the minimum is worked out as a small share of the balance plus that cycle's interest and fees, with a flat floor for small balances. As the balance shrinks, so does the minimum, which is why paying only the minimum stretches repayment over a long time. The box shows you that pattern in numbers for your own card.

## Where the costs show up

Card costs aren't hidden, but they are spread across the statement, and three places are worth knowing. The first is the **late payment warning**, usually printed near the due date. It states what fee could be charged if the minimum payment doesn't arrive in time, and whether a higher interest rate could apply.

The second is the **transactions section**, which is usually split into payments, purchases, fees and interest. Late fees, returned payment fees, annual fees and similar charges appear under fees. Interest appears as its own line or lines, often separated by type of balance. Many statements also show the total fees and interest charged so far this year. That total is a quick way to see what the card has cost over the months.

The third is the **interest charge calculation**, a small section usually toward the end. It lists each type of balance on the card, such as purchases, cash advances, balance transfers and any promotional offer. For each one it gives the rate that applies, the balance the interest was figured on and the interest charged. The rate is shown as an **annual percentage rate**, or APR: the yearly interest rate on that balance, expressed as a percentage. If a **penalty rate** has been applied after a late payment, a higher APR may appear next to one or more balance types. Whether a card has a penalty rate and what triggers it are set by the card's terms, within limits set by federal law. That law also requires advance notice before a rate goes up and sets conditions for applying a penalty rate to an existing balance and for bringing it back down. For your card's fees and rates, go by the statement, the agreement and any notice of a change, not a number heard somewhere else. If any line on the statement doesn't make sense, call the customer service number on the back of the card and ask someone to find the line with you and explain it.

## This week's step

Gather the most recent statement for each credit card in the household, on paper or through the online account. On each one, find four things: the closing date, the payment due date, the statement balance and the minimum payment. Then turn to the rough list you started earlier and write those four next to the name of that card. A card you haven't used lately may have no recent statement. Its cycle keeps running anyway, so the online account or the number on the back can still give you its closing and due dates. Once they're written down, each card on your list shows its two dates, the minimum that keeps a payment on time and the balance that, paid in full, generally avoids interest.
