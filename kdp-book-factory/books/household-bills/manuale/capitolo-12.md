# Choosing What Goes on Autopay and What Doesn't

An automatic payment is a standing instruction: on a certain day, a certain amount leaves a certain account without anyone lifting a finger. That is exactly its strength and its risk. It doesn't forget a due date, and it doesn't notice when something is wrong. The question for each bill on your list is not whether autopay is good or bad in general, but whether this particular bill is one you want paid without looking or one you want to see first.

Your calendar from the last two chapters is what makes this decision possible. An automatic payment set for the 3rd only works smoothly if money reliably arrives before the 3rd. With the payday map in front of you, each autopay becomes a question you can actually answer: will the account hold enough on the day this pulls?

## Bills that tend to suit autopay

The easiest bills to automate share two traits: the amount stays the same and the due date doesn't move. Typical examples are a car insurance premium billed monthly, a fixed phone plan, a loan payment and a streaming subscription. When you already know to the dollar what will leave and when, paying it by hand adds effort without adding much information. Your attention is better spent on the bills that can change.

A steady track record helps too. A bill that has arrived correct for a year or two, from a company that has never surprised you, is a reasonable candidate. So is any bill where paying late would cost more than an occasional wrong amount, a car loan with a late fee for instance, because timing is the one thing autopay does best.

## Bills worth watching by hand first

Other bills earn a closer look before they are automated, or instead of it. Variable bills come first: electricity, gas, water, anything that depends on use or on a meter. Automating them is possible, and many households do, but the amount that leaves is whatever the company decides to bill. If the meter reading was estimated high, or a month of air conditioning doubled the total, the payment goes out on schedule whether or not you have looked at the bill.

Then there are bills with a history. If a provider has billed you twice for the same month, applied a charge you never agreed to, or quietly raised a price at the end of a promotion, reading each bill before it is paid gives you a chance to catch the next one. Once a few clean months have gone by, you can revisit the choice. Nothing about this decision is permanent.

Here is an example. Gloria's car insurance and phone plan are the same every month, so she lets both run automatically. Her electric bill ranges from about $70 in spring to over $200 in August, and last winter it came in based on an estimated reading that was well above her real use. She decides to keep paying that one by hand, which means reading it first. Her internet bill is fixed, but the promotional price ends in March. She keeps it automatic but marks March on her calendar as the month to look at the bill before it clears.

## Two ways a payment can run automatically

The word autopay covers two arrangements that feel alike and behave quite differently. The difference is who holds the controls: the company that sends the bill, or your own bank.

In the first, **the company pulls the money**. You give the provider your bank account or card number and authorize it to collect each bill on or near the due date. The company sets the exact day, sometimes letting you pick from a few options, and takes the amount it billed. You don't have to update anything when a bill changes, which is convenient, but that same fact means a wrong bill is paid as written. The authorization lives in your account with the company, so that's where you go to see its settings.

In the second, **your bank sends the money**. Many banks offer a bill pay service in their website or app: you enter the payee and tell the bank how much to send and when, once or on a repeating schedule. Here you control both the amount and the date.

The trade-off is timing and upkeep. The bank may send the payment electronically or mail a paper check on your behalf, and delivery can take several days depending on the payee and the bank, so the send date and the arrival date are not the same. Your bank's bill pay screen usually shows an estimated delivery date, and that date, not the send date, is the one to compare with the due date. And if the bill varies, a repeating payment from your bank sends the same figure each time unless you change it.

Take Gloria's rent as an example. Her landlord doesn't take card payments or run an online portal, but accepts checks. A repeating payment through her bank's bill pay, set to arrive a few days before the 1st, fits well: the rent stays the same for the length of her lease, and the landlord never needs her account details.

## How a card's autopay amount is set

Credit cards add a choice that other bills don't have. Most card issuers let you choose which amount is paid automatically, and each choice does something different. The chapter on card statements explained what the minimum payment and the statement balance mean; here the question is what each one does when it runs on autopay.

**Autopaying the minimum** means the card is paid on time every month, as long as the money is there, and the payment stays relatively small. Whatever remains after the minimum carries over to the next month as a balance, and the card charges interest on it according to its terms. The minimum keeps the account current, meaning not past due. It doesn't, by itself, bring the balance down quickly.

**Autopaying the statement balance** pays the full amount the statement showed. On most cards, paying that balance in full by the due date is what keeps new purchases from being charged interest, though the exact terms are in your cardholder agreement. The catch is that the amount moves with your spending. A quiet month pulls a small amount; the month after a car repair or the holidays pulls a large one, and the checking account has to hold it on that day.

**Autopaying a fixed amount** sends the same figure each month, say $250. That makes the draw on your account predictable. If the statement balance is higher, the difference carries over, with interest under the card's terms, just as it would after a minimum payment. What happens when the fixed amount is lower than the minimum, or higher than what you owe, depends on the issuer; the autopay settings page or the card's customer service can tell you.

Here is an example of why the answer depends on the household. Ray has one card and a checking account that his paychecks fill on the 1st and the 15th. His card is due on the 22nd. In a typical month the statement balance is around $400, which the account covers without strain. In January, after the holidays, it was $1,300, and a pull that size on the 22nd would have taken most of his mid-month paycheck.

With the statement balance option, the statement is paid in full each month, and the account has to absorb months like January. With the minimum, the pulls stay small, and the rest of the balance keeps collecting interest. A fixed amount lands somewhere between the two. None of the three is right in general. What each one does on Ray's own calendar is what decides it, and Ray is the one who knows what his account can carry.

Some cardholders pair options: an automatic minimum as a floor so the due date is covered even in a busy month, and a larger payment by hand once they have read the statement. That arrangement depends on remembering the second payment. Whatever setting you choose, most cards also accept an extra payment by hand at any time.

## Putting every automatic payment on the list

An automatic payment that isn't written down is the one that surprises you months later. Your master list already says how each bill is paid and from which account. For an automatic payment, those entries need to be exact, because nobody will be looking at the payment when it runs.

Write the date the money actually leaves, which may not be the due date: a company may pull a few days early, and a bank payment is sent before it arrives. Write the amount, or its usual range if the bill varies. And write the specific account or card it draws from. A payment charged to a card never shows on your bank statement; it lands on the card, and the card's own payment is what finally covers it.

It also helps to note which kind each one is, pulled by the company or sent by your bank, because that tells you where it can be seen and changed. Then put each pull date on your calendar beside the paydays. That is where you will notice two large automatic payments landing in the same tight week, before the week arrives.

## This week's step

Sit down with your master list and go through it bill by bill. For each one, write one of three words next to it: automatic, by hand, or undecided. Undecided is a perfectly good answer: it marks the bills where you don't yet know the amount, the history or the timing well enough to choose. For every bill marked automatic, check that the pull date, the amount and the paying account are written down, and that the date is on your calendar.

Then pick one autopay to set up or adjust this week. It might be a fixed bill you have been paying by hand out of habit, or a card autopay whose amount no longer fits your paydays. Make that one change, write it on the list, and let the undecided ones wait.
