# A Short Weekly Check of What Cleared and What's Due

Your list is accurate on the day you finish it. After that, the bank account moves every day, and the list moves only when you move it. Payments go out, some come back, new notices arrive, and a provider moves a due date. Left alone for a month, the list and the account drift apart, and the list slowly turns back into a guess. The weekly check is the small habit that keeps the two in step. It answers three questions: did last week's payments actually go through, what is due before the next check, and what has arrived since the last one.

## A fixed time, tied to something you already do

A check that happens "sometime this weekend" tends not to happen. What makes a weekly habit hold is an anchor: something that already takes place every week without effort, which the check can simply follow. It might be Sunday evening after the dishes, Saturday morning with the first coffee, the night the trash goes out to the curb, or the morning after the last shift of the week. The anchor does the remembering for you. When it happens, the check comes next.

The place matters almost as much as the time. The check needs three things within reach: the list, the account history, and whatever holds the paper mail and billing email. If your list lives in a notebook in a kitchen drawer and the account opens on a laptop in another room, sit where both can be open at once. Walking back and forth between rooms is exactly the kind of small friction that lets a routine lapse.

Here is an example. Paula works rotating shifts at a hospital, so no weekday is reliably free. What does repeat is Sunday evening, when she lays out her kids' clothes for the school week. She does the check right after, at the dining table, with her spreadsheet and the bank's website open side by side and the mail basket next to her laptop. If a family visit swallows her Sunday, her backup is Monday evening. She keeps a backup because a check pushed back one day still does its job, while a check skipped until the following Sunday leaves two weeks unseen.

## Cleared or only pending

Start by looking back. Take every payment that fell due since the last check, whether it went out by autopay, through the bank's bill pay, by card or by check. Then find it in the history of the account or card it came from. What you find there comes in two kinds.

A **pending** transaction is one the bank knows about but hasn't finished processing. Its amount can still change, and now and then a pending item disappears altogether. A **cleared** transaction, which many banks call *posted*, has gone all the way through and is now part of the account's record. Banks don't all use the same labels or show pending items the same way. The time between the two stages also varies with the bank and the kind of payment. The bank's help pages, or a call to its customer service line, will tell you what its own labels mean.

Mark a bill paid only when its payment has cleared. When you press "send" or drop an envelope in the mailbox, the money hasn't reached the provider yet, and a payment can still fail along the way. It can be returned for lack of funds, sent to an account number that's no longer valid, or held up in the mail. If the list says paid at that moment, it will go on saying paid while the provider is still waiting.

One simple way to cover the gap is a second mark for "sent." On paper, it might be a small dot that becomes a check mark once the payment clears. In a spreadsheet, a status column can read *sent* and later *paid*. In the bank's own tools, a scheduled payment usually carries a status of its own, which you can read the same way. Whichever you use, *sent* is the mark worth watching: the money has left your hands but hasn't landed yet.

Autopay needs this look as much as anything paid by hand. An automatic payment does not check itself. It can fail because a card on file expired, or it can come out for a different amount than the bill you saw. The weekly check isn't the place for a line-by-line comparison with the statement. It only asks whether the payment happened and whether the amount is roughly what you expected. If something looks off, write a short note beside it so it doesn't slip away.

Take, as an example, Victor, a retiree who still pays the water bill by check. His notebook shows the water bill with a dot beside it: mailed eight days ago. Because the line shows a dot and not a check mark, he looks for the check number in his account history, and it isn't there. His checks to the water department have always cleared within a few days, so he calls the utility. The check arrived and simply hasn't been deposited yet, and he writes the date of the call on the line. Had he marked it paid when he mailed it, the first sign of trouble, if there had been any, would have been a late notice.

## What's due before the next check

Now look forward. Open the calendar and read it from today to a day or two past your next check. The extra days are margin: the next check may slip, and a payment made by hand often has to leave before its due date to arrive on time. How much earlier depends on the method and the provider. The bank's bill pay screen usually shows an expected arrival date, and the provider can tell you how long its own payment options take.

For each bill in that window, the question is the same: will the money be in the right account on the day it's needed? For an automatic payment, that means the account that funds it, on the day it draws. For a bill you pay by hand, you can schedule it tonight or note the evening you plan to send it. If the money for a bill sits somewhere else, in savings or in what you've set aside for irregular bills, moving it takes time too. A transfer between accounts, especially at two different banks, may not arrive the same day.

Here is an example. Paula's Sunday check shows the quarterly sewer bill due Thursday, drawn by autopay from checking. The money for it sits in the separate account where she sets a little aside each month. She makes the transfer that evening, so it lands before Thursday's draw rather than on the same morning.

Sometimes the look forward shows a gap: a bill due before the paycheck meant to cover it, or a total larger than what the account holds. The weekly check doesn't close that gap. What it does is find it with days to spare instead of on the due date, and days to spare leave room for a call to the provider while the bill is still current.

## Emptying the paper spot and the email folder

Last comes everything that arrived since the last check: the paper in the spot where mail collects, the messages in the billing folder, any alerts or texts from providers. Go through each item and decide where it belongs. The aim is to leave the spot and the folder empty, so that next week everything in them is new.

Most items fall into a few kinds. A bill already on your list needs only its amount and due date written in, if they change from month to month. A bill for something new, such as a subscription someone in the house started or a medical bill after a visit, gets its own line on the list and its date on the calendar. A notice of change, like a new amount, a new due date or a new card number, means updating the line it affects. Anything that needs no action, like an advertisement or a statement kept only for your records, goes into storage or the shredder.

Notices of change are the easiest to miss, because nothing about them looks urgent. Take Victor again, as an example. In his mail this week is a letter from the electric company saying that his averaged monthly payment goes up starting next month. It asks him to pay nothing, and it would be easy to leave on the counter. He changes the amount in his notebook, so that when the higher payment comes out, the list already expects it.

A replacement card works the same way. If your bank replaces a card with a new number after a fraud alert, a bill still charged to the old number can fail on its next attempt. Because your list records how each bill is paid, you can find every bill on that card in one pass and update them the same week. You don't have to learn about them one declined payment at a time.

## When a week gets skipped

Weeks get skipped: a flu, a trip, a new baby, a hard month. The routine doesn't need to start over. The next check simply covers more ground. Look back to the last check you actually did, not just the past seven days, and look forward as usual. It takes longer than a normal week, and the week after, the usual window is enough again.

## This week's step

Choose the anchor for your weekly check, the weekly event it will follow. Choose the seat too, where the list, the account and the mail can all be in front of you. Then do a first check this week, however short. If all you manage is to find last week's payments in the account history and mark the ones that cleared, that is a first check. Before you close the list, put the next one on whatever calendar you actually look at, with the backup day beside it, so the second check doesn't depend on memory either.
