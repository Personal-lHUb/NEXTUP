# Staying in Control of the Account Behind Autopay

Which account actually pays each automatic bill? Most households set up autopay once and never look at that account again. On the chosen day, though, the company asks for its money and the account answers. That answer can be a clean payment, a returned one or an overdraft. Which one you get depends on the account behind the payment, not on the bill. Once three or four bills draw from the same place, the account needs as much attention as the bills themselves.

## One account for the bills

Answer that question first. Your master list already records how each bill is paid and from which account, and reading down those entries often turns up a surprise. Many autopays were set up with whatever account or card was handy that day. The electric bill may come out of checking, the phone bill off a credit card and the gym from an old account nobody uses anymore. That last kind carries the most risk: a shortfall or an unexpected charge in an account nobody checks can go unnoticed until a company calls.

Some households send every regular monthly bill through one account kept apart from everyday spending. It works like the holding account in the chapter on biweekly paychecks and uneven income, and just as well with a steady paycheck. The appeal is being able to see where you stand. When the bills account holds only bill money, its balance tells you directly whether the next few automatic payments are covered, and a big weekend at the store can't quietly eat into the rent.

Here is an example. Sofia is paid every two weeks. She asked her employer to split her direct deposit, which many employers allow, so that a fixed amount lands in her bills account each payday and the rest goes to her spending account. Her mortgage, utilities, car insurance and phone all draw from the bills account. When she checks it, she doesn't have to subtract last Tuesday's pharmacy run. The number either covers what's coming or it doesn't.

A separate account has costs too. It is one more balance to watch and one more login to keep safe. Some accounts also carry monthly fees or minimum-balance requirements, which vary by bank and by account type. If money reaches the bills account through a transfer you make yourself rather than by direct deposit, a forgotten transfer leaves it short, just as overspending can empty a single account. When you choose the bills account, ask whether your paycheck can be split straight into it and what the bank does with an automatic payment the balance can't cover.

Keeping everything in one account works too, as long as you know which bills draw from it and when. Two accounts keep the bills apart from everyday spending. A third can hold the money set aside for irregular bills, as in the chapter on setting a little aside for them. These are alternatives, not a checklist. What counts is knowing which account stands behind each bill, and having chosen it on purpose.

## When a payment arrives and the money doesn't

This is what happens when a payment comes due. On the draw date, the company sends your bank a request for the amount due. If the balance covers it, the payment goes through. If it doesn't, the bank generally does one of two things. It pays the bill anyway and lets the account go below zero, or it refuses the payment and sends it back.

A refused payment is called a **returned payment**. The bank may charge you a fee for returning it, often called an NSF fee, short for "non-sufficient funds." The company, now without its payment, may charge its own returned-payment fee. If the bill then passes its due date before you pay it some other way, a late fee can follow. Some companies also suspend autopay after a return, so next month's bill won't pay itself either. One short balance can bring charges from both sides.

If the bank pays the bill anyway, that is **overdraft coverage**. In effect, the bank lends you the difference. The bill is paid on time, but the account is now negative. The bank may charge an overdraft fee for covering it, and sometimes more charges if the account stays negative for a while. Your next deposit first brings the account back to zero, which can leave the following bill short. Some banks offer a different arrangement, often called overdraft protection, which pulls the missing money from a linked savings account or a line of credit. That can carry a transfer fee or interest of its own.

Hank's car insurance of $186 draws on the 15th. A plumber's bill paid by debit card on the 12th left $140 in the account. It depends on how his account is set. The insurer might have been paid, leaving Hank owing the bank $46 plus a possible fee. Or the insurer's request might have been returned, leaving the premium unpaid, with possible fees from both the bank and the insurer. The shortfall is the same, the outcomes are different, and nothing about the bill itself decided which one Hank got.

Some banks have dropped certain fees, some give you a short window to add money before they charge, and some decline the payment without charging at all. One detail is worth asking about directly. In many accounts, the choice you made about overdraft on debit card purchases and ATM withdrawals is separate from what happens to an automatic bill payment. Turning overdraft off for your card may not have changed anything for your bills. The rules that apply to you are in writing, and they change over time: the bank's account agreement and fee schedule, on its website or at a branch, and the autopay terms you accepted with the company.

## Changing or stopping an automatic payment

Before you change anything, check your master list for the note suggested in the chapter on choosing what goes on autopay: is this payment pulled by the company or sent by your bank? A payment your bank sends is changed or stopped in the bank's bill-pay tool, where you set it up. A pulled payment starts with the company.

To stop a pulled payment cleanly, tell the company first. Cancel autopay in your online account with them or by phone. Ask for a confirmation, such as an email, a reference number or the name of the person you spoke to, and note the date. Then tell your bank, which is your backup if the company keeps drawing. For an automatic electronic payment from a personal bank account, federal rules generally let you stop it by telling your bank in time; the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's website explains how. Ask how far ahead of the next draw they need the request, whether there's a fee, how long the stop lasts and whether they need it in writing. If the payment comes from a credit card, call the card issuer instead, because its process is different.

Timing matters. A payment already being processed may still go through, so make the change well before the next draw date, not the night before.

Then check the next statement from both sides. On the bank or card statement, confirm that nothing was drawn. On the company's bill, confirm that the balance shows what you expect. Stopping an automatic payment does not pay the bill, and it does not end the service or the contract. If you still owe the company, decide how the next payment will be made and write that into your system. Otherwise the bill can slip through between the old way of paying and the new one.

Changing an amount or a date follows the same pattern. Make the change where the payment was set up, get a confirmation and check the following statement.

## A new card or a closed account

A replacement card, whether after a loss, a fraud alert or simply an expiration, usually comes with a new number or at least a new expiration date. Some companies receive the new details automatically through the card network. Others don't, and from the outside you can't tell which is which. Payments that aren't updated get declined, and the bill is left unpaid. When the new card arrives, find every bill on the master list that was paid with the old card and update each company. Then check each of those bills when it next comes due.

Closing a bank account takes more care. Some automatic payments draw only every few months, and they can stay pointed at an old account long after you've stopped thinking about it. Hank switches banks after a move. He opens the new account first and moves his direct deposit. Then he moves each automatic payment one at a time, checking them off on his master list. He keeps the old account open, with a little money in it, until every bill has drawn once from the new account. That includes the water bill, which comes every other month. Only then does he close the old one. Ask your old bank what happens to a payment that arrives after the account is closed. It is usually returned, and that can bring the company's fees with it.

## This week's step

Contact the bank that holds the account paying your bills, by phone, secure message or at a branch, and ask how overdraft coverage is set on that account. Start with what happens when an automatic payment arrives and the balance is short: is it paid or returned? Then ask what fees apply in each case and whether a savings account or line of credit is linked to cover a shortfall. Ask, too, whether the setting for automatic payments is different from the one for debit card purchases. If the person you talk to isn't sure, ask where the setting appears in the account agreement so you can read it. Write the answers in your system next to that account, whether that's a page in your notebook, a note in your spreadsheet or the notes field in your bank's tools. Add the date and the name of the person who answered. If the answer surprises you, the bank can explain what each option involves, and whether to change anything is up to you.
