# What Each Bill Puts at Stake When Money Runs Short

When the account can't cover everything due this month, every bill seems to demand the same thing at the same moment. They don't all carry the same consequences, though. One protects the roof over your head, one keeps the heat on, one keeps the car you drive to work, and another mostly affects what you'll owe later and how your credit looks. Each also runs on its own clock. Some take weeks or months to reach a serious step, some move faster, and some can't reach that step at all without a judge.

None of what follows is a ranking. Only you know what your household depends on, and the order that makes sense for one family can be wrong for the next. The rules vary by state, by contract and by provider, so each section also says where yours are written down.

## Rent and mortgage

If you rent, your lease states the due date, any grace period and the late fee. Many states and some cities also require a minimum grace period or cap late fees, so the lease isn't always the last word. When rent stays unpaid, a landlord generally has to start with a written notice, often called a notice to pay or quit. That notice gives you a set number of days to pay before anything else happens. How many days depends on your state, and sometimes on your city.

If the rent still isn't paid, the landlord's next step is usually to file an eviction case in court. You have the right to be notified of the case and to respond. Removal normally requires a court order, which a sheriff or similar officer carries out. Some states and cities add further steps, and tenants in subsidized housing may have federal protections too.

A mortgage follows a different path. The loan documents set the grace period and the late fee, within limits that some states and some loan programs impose. A payment that stays unpaid makes the loan delinquent, and the servicer, which already handles your payments, starts contacting you. Foreclosure is the legal process that lets the lender take the home and sell it. Federal rules require most servicers to reach out early with information about your options, and they generally can't start foreclosure right away, so the window is usually measured in months rather than days. Beyond that, your state's law decides whether foreclosure has to go through a court and how long it takes.

For either kind of housing, the details are in the lease or loan papers you signed, in the notices you receive and in your state's law. A legal aid office or an attorney can tell you what applies where you live.

## Utilities: notices, shutoffs and reconnection

Electric, gas and water companies usually have to warn you before disconnecting service. The warning is a written notice with a disconnection date. The rules that can delay that date, and the state commission that enforces them, are covered in the chapter on utility assistance and legal aid.

Here is an example. Brenda rents a two-bedroom apartment, and this month her paychecks won't cover everything that's due. Her electric bill is two months behind, $286 in all, and a notice has arrived with a disconnection date of the 18th. Reading it closely, she finds that the amount needed to stop the shutoff is $143, not the full balance, and that the back lists the state commission's phone number. Even counting the electric bill at $143, her month is still $140 short. But she now knows the date, the amount and whom to ask about her rights.

After a shutoff, getting service back usually costs more than the missed bill alone. Restoring it can mean paying the past-due balance, a reconnection fee and sometimes a new security deposit, and then waiting for a technician. Phone and internet service often don't carry the same protections. The provider suspends service under the terms of your contract, and an unpaid balance can be sent to collections. To check the rules, look at the notice, the provider's terms and your state commission's website or consumer line.

## Insurance: when coverage lapses

An unpaid insurance premium doesn't take away anything you can see. The policy lapses, which means it stops, and whatever it covered becomes your risk again. Before that happens, the insurer typically sends a notice of cancellation for nonpayment. How much time you have depends on the policy and on the rules where you live, which your state's insurance department can explain.

Car insurance also has a legal side. Nearly every state requires a minimum level of coverage to drive. Some states check insurance electronically and can suspend a registration when it lapses. A gap can also raise the price of a new policy, because insurers often ask about past coverage. With homeowners insurance, much depends on whether the premium is paid from escrow, the account described in the chapter on the bills you forget. If it isn't, and the policy lapses, the loan agreement usually allows the lender to buy a policy and charge you for it. This is called force-placed insurance, and it tends to cost more and mainly protects the lender's interest.

Health insurance grace periods depend on the kind of plan and on whether you get help paying the premium. Claims made during that time may be held until the premium is paid. The plan documents, your employer or the marketplace can confirm the rules. Reinstatement, getting a lapsed policy back, varies too. Some insurers will reinstate a policy if the overdue premium is paid within a set time, and others require a new application. For life insurance, a new application can mean answering health questions again and paying a price based on your current age.

## Secured loans and unsecured debts

A secured loan is backed by property that the lender can take back if the loan isn't repaid. That property is called collateral. The usual household example is a car loan. When payments stop, the lender can repossess the car, and in most states it can do so without going to court first, as long as it doesn't "breach the peace." That generally rules out force, threats or breaking into a locked garage. The lender then usually sells the car. If the sale brings in less than you owe plus the costs, you may still owe the difference, which is called a deficiency. Lenders generally have to send a notice before the sale. Until the sale, you can get the car back by paying off the whole loan plus the lender's costs; some states also let you get it back by catching up on the missed payments. The notice should explain how, and your loan contract sets out the rest.

Unsecured debts have no property attached. Credit cards, most personal loans and most medical bills are unsecured. Missed payments bring late fees and, under many card agreements, a higher penalty interest rate. If the missed payments continue, the account can be closed and charged off, which means the lender writes it off as a loss on its books, though you still owe it. It can then be passed or sold to a collection agency. The creditor or collector can sue. If a court enters a judgment against you, including a default judgment because the person sued didn't respond, state law may allow the creditor to take part of your wages (called garnishment) or to freeze a bank account. Some money, such as many federal benefits, is generally protected. Federal law also caps how much of a paycheck can be taken for ordinary debts, and some states set lower limits or allow no wage garnishment for most consumer debts. This path is usually slower than a repossession, but court papers come with their own deadline to respond. If you're sued over a debt, ask a legal aid office or an attorney what your options are before that deadline passes.

## Medical bills

Medical bills are usually unsecured, so the collection and court steps described above can apply to them as well. They often arrive as a single total with little explanation. You can ask the provider's billing office for an itemized bill, a list of every charge with its date and a description or code. You can then compare it with the explanation of benefits from your insurer, the statement showing what was billed, what the plan paid and what the plan says you owe. Errors to look for include services never received, the same charge listed twice, and a bill sent before the insurer finished processing the claim.

You can also ask whether the hospital or practice has a financial assistance program, sometimes called charity care. Nonprofit hospitals are generally required to have a written financial assistance policy, and some other providers offer something similar. Eligibility usually depends on household income and size, and in many cases you can apply after the bill has arrived.

Isaac, who is retired, owes $2,340 for an emergency room visit after his insurance paid its share. The itemized bill shows the same scan charged twice and a medication he declined. He flags both charges with the billing office and asks for the hospital's assistance application. Whether he qualifies depends on the hospital's policy, but the application costs him an afternoon, not money.

The rules on how medical debt appears on credit reports need checking before you rely on anything you've heard. The credit bureaus, federal regulators and several states have all changed or tried to change these rules in recent years, and they may change again. For the current rules, check the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's website, your state attorney general's office or the credit bureaus themselves.

## This week's step

Open your master list and pick out the bills due between today and the end of the month. Next to each one, in your notebook, spreadsheet or bank tool, add a few plain words. Note what the bill protects, such as your home, power, car, coverage or credit. Note what the first serious step would be if it went unpaid, and whether a notice with a date on it has already arrived. Where you aren't sure, take out the lease, the loan agreement, the policy or the last notice and read the part about missed payments. Then sort the bills into groups by what they put at stake. You aren't deciding tonight what gets paid. You're making sure that if you pick up the phone, you already know what that bill puts on the line and how much time its process allows.
