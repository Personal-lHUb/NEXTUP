# Fee Waivers and What Reaches Your Credit Report

Once the missed bill is paid and the account is back in order, two questions tend to stay behind. The first is about money: can the late fee or the overdraft charge be taken off? The second is about the longer record: will this show up on your credit report, and if it does, for how long? Both can be answered with a phone call and a little reading.

## A waiver is a request, not a right

A late fee or an overdraft fee is usually written into the agreement you accepted when you opened the account. That's why a company can charge it, and, when the fee was charged correctly, it's also why you can't insist it be removed. If the fee itself looks wrong (the payment arrived on time, or the amount is more than the agreement or the law allows), that is an error to dispute, not a favor to ask. When a provider takes a fee off, it is using discretion. Many companies give their service staff some room to do this, because a customer who has paid reliably for years is often worth more to them than one fee.

How much room varies a great deal. One card company may waive a first late fee with little discussion. Another may allow one courtesy waiver in a set period and note it in your file. A utility may follow rules set partly by the state regulator, while a small local provider may have no written policy at all. Banks differ too. None of this can be looked up in one place, so ask, and treat the answer as information about that company, not as a verdict on you.

## Making the call

Start with the customer service number printed on the bill, the statement or the back of the card. For a bank fee, use the bank's main service line or visit a branch if you have one. Chat and secure messages can work too, but a conversation with a person gives you more room to explain.

Keep what you say short and specific. Three things help. First, your history with the account: how long you've had it and whether you've usually paid on time. Second, what went wrong, in one or two plain sentences, without a long apology. Third, what you've done since: the bill is paid and the cause has been fixed. Then ask the question directly. "Would you be willing to waive the fee this time?" is clear, and it gives the person on the other end an easy yes.

Here is an example. Denise has had the same credit card for nine years and has paid it on time for all of them. In March her payment went in six days late, because the reminder email landed in a folder she no longer checks. A $30 late fee appeared on her next statement. When she called, she said: "I've had this card nine years and I've never been late before. The reminder went to an old email folder. I've made the payment and updated my alerts. Is there any way you can remove the late fee?" The representative looked at her record and credited the fee. It could have gone the other way, and Denise would still have lost nothing by asking.

Before you hang up, whatever the answer, note the date, the name or ID of the person you spoke with, and what was agreed. If a credit was promised, check the next statement to confirm it shows up. It usually appears as a separate credit line, not as the fee quietly disappearing.

### When the first answer is no

A no on the first call is not always the final answer. Representatives don't all have the same authority. You can ask, politely, whether anyone else can review the request, such as a supervisor or the department that handles account adjustments. You can also thank the person, end the call and try again another day. Some people ask whether part of the fee can be credited when the whole of it can't.

Carl was charged a $35 overdraft fee when an insurance premium came out two days before his paycheck arrived. The first person he spoke with said the bank doesn't waive overdraft fees. Carl asked whether someone else could look at it, was told no, and called back the next week. The second representative credited half. That's a partial result, and with a different representative the answer might have stayed no. When each person you reach gives the same answer, that is usually the policy, and the fee stands.

## Late on the books, late on the report

A payment can be late in two different places. The first is the company's own records. Once the due date passes without payment, the account is late as far as the company is concerned, and that is what usually leads to the late fee, though some bills allow a grace period after the due date before the fee applies. The second is your credit reports: the files kept by the nationwide credit bureaus, companies that collect information about how you handle credit and pass it on to lenders, landlords and others the law allows to see it.

These two don't move together. The company decides whether and when to report a late payment, and federal law requires what it reports to be accurate. Many lenders wait until a payment has fallen a full billing cycle behind, so a payment made a few days after the due date may cost a fee without ever reaching a report. That is a common practice, not a rule, and timing can depend on the type of account and, in some cases, on state law. Many utilities and landlords don't report monthly payments at all, yet an unpaid account sent to a collection agency can still appear.

The only reliable way to know is to ask the lender directly. Two questions cover it: "At what point do you report a late payment to the credit bureaus?" and "Has this payment been reported?" In Denise's case, the representative explained that the card company reports a payment as late only once it is a full cycle past due, so her six days had not been reported. Another company could have given a different answer.

## Getting and reading your credit reports

Federal law gives you the right to free copies of your credit reports from each of the three nationwide bureaus, and there is one official website set up for this under that law: AnnualCreditReport.com. The safest way to reach it is through the websites of the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) or the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), which link to it and explain how often free reports are currently available. Other sites with similar names, or that advertise free reports, may be selling something alongside them.

Request a report from each bureau, not just one, because lenders don't always report to all three and the files can differ. Each report lists your accounts, and each account has a payment history, usually shown month by month, with each month marked as on time or late by a number of days. The labels differ a little between bureaus, and each report explains its own. Read the history for every account, not only the one you're worried about. Check that the accounts are yours, that closed accounts show as closed, and that the late marks match what actually happened.

Under federal law, most negative information, including an accurate late payment, can stay on your report for up to seven years, and some states set shorter limits for certain items. The FTC's and CFPB's consumer pages explain the details.

## Disputing a mistake in writing

If something is wrong, such as an account you don't recognize, a month marked late that you paid on time, or a balance that doesn't match, you can dispute it. You can dispute with the bureau whose report shows the error and with the company that supplied the information. Each bureau explains how to file, and the CFPB and FTC websites describe the process and your rights. If the error is an account you never opened, the FTC's site also explains the separate steps for identity theft.

Put the dispute in writing, by letter or through the bureau's online form, and keep a copy of exactly what you submitted. Say what is wrong, which account it concerns and what the correct information is. Include copies, never originals, of anything that supports your point, such as a bank statement showing the payment cleared. If you mail it, choose a method that gives you proof of delivery. The bureau generally has to investigate and tell you the result within a deadline set by federal law, which the bureau's own dispute page explains.

Carl requested his three reports. On one of them, his phone account showed a late mark for a month when his bank statement shows the payment clearing four days before the due date. The other two reports didn't show the late mark at all, which is exactly why reading all three matters. He wrote to the bureau that showed it and to the phone company, enclosed a copy of the statement and kept copies of both letters. The outcome now depends on the investigation, but he has the paper trail to follow up.

A dispute is the tool for mistakes. A late payment that really happened is not a mistake, and the dispute process isn't meant to remove it. Companies that promise to erase accurate negative information for a fee are promising something the law does not provide.

## This week's step

If a late fee or an overdraft fee was charged after your missed payment, call the company this week and ask for it to be waived. Mention how long you've had the account, say in a sentence what went wrong and what you've changed, and ask plainly. Write down who you spoke with and what they said. While you're on the line, ask whether the payment was reported to the credit bureaus.

That call is the step. The credit reports are optional and can wait for another evening: go to AnnualCreditReport.com and request a report from each of the three bureaus. At the kitchen table, read the payment history on every account, month by month. If everything matches what you know, you have your answer. If something doesn't, you know how to put it in writing.
