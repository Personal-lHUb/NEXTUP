# Reading a Household Bill Without Guessing

A utility bill carries more numbers than most people ever read. There are account numbers, meter numbers, rates per unit, dates in three or four places and dollar amounts in five or six. Only a few of them decide whether you pay on time and pay the right amount, and the layout doesn't always make it obvious which ones. Once you know what each part is for, a bill takes a couple of minutes to read and no guessing at all.

The bills here are utilities and household services: electricity, gas, water and sewer, trash, internet and phone. Their layouts differ from company to company, and the same company may redesign its bill every few years. The parts, though, tend to repeat, and so do the questions they answer: when the payment is due, how much is due, and what it pays for.

## Three dates that look alike

Here is an example. Priya opens her electric bill and finds three dates near the top. One reads *Statement date: April 8*. Another reads *Service period: March 6 – April 5*. The third, in a shaded box, reads *Due date: April 29*. The three are printed in similar type, and for months Priya had treated the first date her eye landed on as the deadline, paying three weeks before she had to.

The **statement date**, sometimes called the bill date or issue date, is the day the company prepared the bill. It says nothing about when to pay. The **service period**, also called the billing period, is the stretch of days the charges cover. On a metered utility it ends before the statement date, because the company has to read the meter and run the numbers first. Internet and phone bills often work the other way and charge for the month ahead. The **due date** is the one that decides whether you are on time: the day the payment has to reach the company.

Read the wording around the due date closely. Many bills say *payment must be received by*, which means the money has to arrive by that day, not just leave your hands. A check mailed on the due date will arrive after it. An online or phone payment can take a business day or more to be credited. The bill or the company's payment page usually says how long each method takes. Some bills also print a line such as *amount due after* followed by a date and a higher figure. That line does not give you extra time to pay. It shows what the bill becomes once a late charge is added.

The gap between the statement date and the due date varies. Some companies allow several weeks and others much less, and for regulated utilities state rules may set a minimum. Only your own bills can tell you the gap on each one.

## Four amounts and the one that counts

The amounts are where most misreadings happen, because a bill usually shows its arithmetic and each step gets a line of its own. Greg's gas bill this month has a summary box that shows a previous balance of $142.60, a payment received of $100.00, a balance forward of $42.60, current charges of $118.35 and a total amount due of $160.95.

The **previous balance** is what the last bill said you owed. The payment line shows what the company has credited since then. Whatever is left becomes the **balance forward**, which some bills call the unpaid balance or the **past-due amount**. **Current charges** are the new charges for this service period and nothing else. The **total amount due** puts it together: previous balance, minus payments, plus current charges.

Greg had been paying the current charges each month, because that figure sat right beside the usage chart and looked like the bill. Each time, an older leftover stayed unpaid and rolled forward to the next bill, and in time a late charge appeared on it. Nothing had been hidden from him. The layout simply put the wrong number where his eye went first.

In most cases, what the company expects by the due date is the total amount due. When the bill shows a past-due amount, though, read the words printed next to it. Some bills mark it *due immediately* or *due now*. That means it was already late before this bill went out, and the due date lower on the page applies only to the new charges. If a past-due amount comes with a disconnection notice, call the customer service number on the bill to confirm what has to be paid, and by when. Disconnection protections vary by state: the state utility regulator can say which apply, and the chapter on finding utility assistance covers help beyond the company.

Two arrangements change which number matters. On a budget billing or level payment plan, the company averages your usage into a steadier monthly amount. The bill may then show your actual charges and, separately, the plan amount. The plan amount is what is due. The actual charges show how your usage compares. The difference is settled later, often yearly or when you leave the plan, depending on the utility. And a balance printed with a minus sign or the letters CR is a credit: the company owes you that amount, often because you paid ahead or a charge was corrected, and it usually comes off future bills.

Doing the subtraction yourself takes less than a minute. If the payment line is missing a payment you made, or shows a different amount, you have found the problem before a late charge does. Payments do sometimes land on the wrong account or post late, and the bill is often where that first shows.

## Estimated or actual readings

Electricity, gas and water are usually billed from a meter. The company records the number on the meter at the start and the end of the service period, and the difference is the usage you are charged for. Next to those readings, most bills say whether the meter was actually read. They show *Actual* or the letter A if it was, and *Estimated*, *Est.* or the letter E if it wasn't, sometimes in a column headed reading type and sometimes in a footnote. An estimate happens when nobody could read the meter. The gate may have been locked, a dog may have been in the yard, snow may have covered the meter, a route may have been missed, or a meter that reports on its own may have failed to send a reading. The company then works out a likely figure, usually from your past usage in the same season.

Priya's electric bills for December and January were both estimated, because snow had drifted against the side of the house where the meter sits. The estimates were based on the previous winter, when she had been away for several weeks. In February the meter was read again, and that actual reading covered all the electricity used since the last real one. The February bill included everything the two estimates had missed and came out far higher than usual. The company had not made a mistake. It had caught up.

It can run the other way, too: an estimate that was too high turns into a credit or a smaller bill once the real reading arrives. Either way, a string of estimates means your bills are not measuring what you use, and the real figure is still coming. You can compare the number on the meter with the present reading printed on the bill. A standard meter only counts up, so if today's number is lower, the estimate ran high. With solar panels or a new meter, ask the company how to read yours. Many companies also accept a reading you submit yourself.

How many estimated bills in a row a utility may send, and how far back it may charge for usage it missed, depend on state rules and on the company's own policies. The state utility regulator publishes those rules. Its name varies: often the public utility or public service commission, sometimes a board, department or corporation commission; your state government's website lists it. Utilities run by a city, and cooperatives, often answer to a city council or their own board instead.

## Fees, taxes and the lines in between

Between the usage and the total, most bills list a set of smaller charges. Some are fixed, such as a **customer charge** or basic service charge you pay every month for being connected, whatever you use. Some move with usage or with the price the utility pays for fuel, under names like delivery charge, supply charge or fuel adjustment. Then come taxes and government fees, whose names depend on where you live: state and local sales tax, a utility users' tax, a franchise fee passed through from the city. Internet and phone bills add lines with official-sounding names. Some are taxes or fees collected for a government, and others are charges the company sets itself. The bill or the company's website usually says which is which, and if it doesn't, you can ask.

A **late charge** is added when payment arrives after the due date, or after a grace period, a short window some companies allow. It may be a flat amount or a percentage of the unpaid balance. Its limits, and any grace period, usually come from rules the utility commission approves for a state-regulated utility, the city's rules for a city-run one, and the contract or terms of service for other services. Less common lines include a returned payment fee when a check or automatic payment bounces, a deposit and a reconnection fee.

Names and amounts change with the provider, the state and sometimes the town, so one bill is no guide to another. Once you have read your own bill line by line, it becomes the guide to itself: a new line, or a jump in a familiar one, stands out the next time. The company's rate schedule, sometimes called the tariff, is usually posted on its website. The state utility commission can answer questions about charges on the bills it oversees.

## This week's step

Take one current utility bill, on paper or on screen, and give it ten quiet minutes. Find the due date and read the words beside it, so you know whether the payment has to be sent or received by that day. Find the total amount due. Then run the subtraction from the previous balance and the last payment, to see whether that payment was credited and whether any part of the total is past due. Last, look next to the meter reading for the word or letter that says whether it was actual or estimated. If it was estimated, compare the number on the meter with the one on the bill the next time you can get to it. The due date and the amount you found are the two facts the rest of the system will rely on for this bill.
